Lineage

The projects behind the project. The MAP is a study of lineage — where sounds came from, who carried them, what they became — and it has a lineage of its own. These are the works it descends from, each with what it taught, and where to go to see it whole.

1924 – 1929 · unfinished

The Mnemosyne Atlas

Aby Warburg — Hamburg; the digital edition by Cornell University Library with the Warburg Institute

The art historian Aby Warburg spent his last years pinning photographs to sixty-three black panels — antiquities beside Renaissance paintings beside newspaper clippings — arranging and rearranging them so that the connections between images, not the images alone, carried the argument. He called it an atlas: in Cornell's words, an unfinished attempt to map the pathways that give art history its pathos-laden meanings. Cultural memory, organized by association rather than by chronology or geography.

What it gives this projectThe founding permission. A serious scholar can present connection itself as the work — the lines between the panels are the thesis. The MAP's graph is a Mnemosyne panel that moves.

1970 – present

Rock Family Trees

Pete Frame — ZigZag magazine, then decades of volumes

Beginning in ZigZag, the magazine he founded, Pete Frame drew the genealogies of bands by hand — who left, who joined, who formed what next — some hundred and forty trees covering more than two thousand artists, lettered in his own calligraphy on sheets four feet wide, then photographed down for print. Every membership change, every splinter group, every session drummer with a month's tenure, held in one drawing you can stand in front of.

What it gives this projectThe hand-made pole. Personnel is history — who was in the room explains the record — and care of execution is itself content. A Frame tree is trustworthy because you can see the labor in it. The MAP's answer to that is prose written one note at a time.

1977

The Genealogy of Pop/Rock Music

Reebee Garofalo, drawn by Damon Rarey — from Rock 'n' Roll Is Here to Pay

A fold-out chart from a book about the music industry's politics: some seven hundred artists and thirty styles, 1955 to 1974, drawn as currents that widen, narrow, merge, and fork — the length of each artist's ribbon tracking their years as a hit-maker. Edward Tufte reproduced it as a classic of information design. It is the direct ancestor of every genre-flow visualization since, this site's included.

What it gives this projectThe scholarly chart. Influence can be drawn to scale, with real dates and real durations, and still be beautiful — rigor and design as one gesture, not a trade.

2008 – 2016

Musicmap

Kwinten Crauwels — Belgium

An architect who couldn't find a cartographic treatment of genre history spent the better part of a decade building one: nearly two hundred genres from 1870 to 2016, drawn as a subway-like map with zoomable levels of detail, playlists in every station, and — rarest of all — a published theoretical framework and two hundred listed sources explaining the choices. Its stated belief: knowledge about music genres should be part of basic education.

What it gives this projectThe nearest relative, and the bar to clear. One person, years of care, sources shown. Where Musicmap fixes its map in a final drawing, the MAP keeps its alive — every node a full text, every line re-drawable as the notes grow.

2013 – 2023 · frozen

Every Noise at Once

Glenn McDonald — from inside Spotify's data

An engineer's scatter-plot of the entire genre space as the platform's algorithms heard it — thousands of named styles arranged by sonic character, denser to the left, more electric toward the top, a click playing each one aloud. The opposite method to everything above: no editor, no argument, the map emerging from listening data at industrial scale. When McDonald left Spotify at the end of 2023, the data stopped; the site stands frozen where it was.

What it gives this projectThe algorithmic pole — and the caution. Scale without an editor produces wonder but not understanding; a project built on a platform's data lives at the platform's pleasure. The MAP is static files and prose, hostable anywhere, dependent on no one's API.
