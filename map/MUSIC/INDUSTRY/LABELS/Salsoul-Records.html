Label

Salsoul Records

Founded1974
LocationNew York City
Key years1975–1983
Founders
Joseph Cayre and his brothers Kenneth and Stanley
LinksRYM

In May 1976 a record label did something no label had done: it put a DJ’s version of its own single on sale. Double Exposure’s “Ten Percent,” stretched by Walter Gibbons from the single’s four minutes to nine minutes forty-five, went into stores as a twelve-inch at $2.98, in a four-color jacket with a window cut to show the label — the first commercially available twelve-inch single, front-page news in the trades.1 Ken Cayre, the label man who had watched Gibbons double the record in a booth, kept the story plain: “I asked Walter Gibbons to come in and remix it, and that was the first 12” we ever sold.”2 Salsoul spent the next eight years running on that idea: it bought Philadelphia’s orchestra outright and sold the dancers’ versions of its records back to the dancers — a label built from the floor up.

Pantyhose to the mother label

The Cayre brothers arrived at disco from everywhere except music. Joseph, Kenneth, and Stanley had run a plastic-injection-molding factory and a Florida pantyhose operation before paying a million-dollar advance for the US rights to Spanish-language eight-track cassettes. The distribution firm they built on that license, Caytronics, made them the country’s Latin-music middlemen, and by the early seventies they were recording their own acts on a label called Mericana.3 The word that named the next label came from one of those acts. Joe Bataan, the Afro-Filipino king of Latin soul, conceived “Salsoul” — street language for the urban Latino culture that ran salsa and soul together — and carried the first releases himself.4 The label proper started in business in 1974, run from New York by the three brothers, with Ken, the youngest, on the music.5

The Philadelphia raid

The sound was bought wholesale from Philadelphia. Ken Cayre went to the source and met Vince Montana Jr., the vibraphonist of MFSB: “I met Vince Montana, who was the vibes player, and he introduced me to the rhythm section and we recorded three songs. One of the songs I called the ‘Salsoul Hustle’; I called the band the Salsoul Orchestra on the Salsoul label.” The hustle record became the label’s first hit in 1975.6 The players came willingly, because Philadelphia’s system underpaid its engine room; the guitarist TJ Tindall put the grievance in one line: “[I] made more money doin’ Wranglers commercials than I did on [MFSB sessions].”7 The defectors Cayre lured included the Baker-Harris-Young rhythm core, the string arranger Don Renaldo, and Bunny Sigler, and by October 1975 the first Salsoul Orchestra album was in stores a month ahead of the O’Jays’ Family Reunion — Gamble and Huff’s own orchestra, in effect, recording for New York money.8 Montana came with a founder’s pedigree; he had helped Joe Tarsia set up Sigma Sound itself.9

The booth on the payroll

The commercial twelve-inch made the remix a product, and Salsoul turned remixing into a staff position: Gibbons first, and after him Larry Levan, who finished the “I Got My Mind Made Up” mix Gibbons walked away from and rode Instant Funk’s record to No. 1 on the R&B and dance charts.10 Levan kept remixing for the label into the eighties — Loleatta Holloway, First Choice, Skyy, Inner Life — and Shep Pettibone’s early-eighties mixes carried the catalog into the next genre’s hands: his 1983 rework of First Choice’s “Let No Man Put Asunder” became a founding document of the house floor.11 The label’s remix ledger reads now like a succession chart of the DJ booth itself.

The roster

  • Double Exposure — the Philadelphia vocal group whose “Ten Percent” carried the format into stores
  • First Choice — on Gold Mind, Norman Harris’s Salsoul-distributed imprint: “Doctor Love” (1977) and the eternally recut “Let No Man Put Asunder”
  • Loleatta Holloway — the label’s great voice and its unluckiest: “I couldn’t get a major hit single on her,” Cayre admitted12
  • Instant Funk — Bunny Sigler’s funk unit, the label’s R&B chart-topper
  • The Salsoul Orchestra — the house band as marquee act, strings by the section
  • Skyy — Randy Muller’s Brooklyn group, whose “Call Me” (1981) took the label into the boogie era
  • Inner Life — Jocelyn Brown’s group, a Levan remix staple
  • Cándido — the conga master whose 1979 Salsoul sides became after-hours canon

The close and the long echo

The founders left the business faster than the records did. The Cayres wound recorded music down in 1984 for a home-video venture, and the label went defunct in 1985 after issuing some 300 singles.13 The catalog then out-earned the company. Holloway’s “Love Sensation” (1980), written and produced by Dan Hartman, mixed by Tom Moulton, a dance No. 1 in its own year, became the raw material of two later hits: Black Box’s “Ride On Time” (1989) took her vocal to No. 1 in Britain without crediting her, and paid her a settlement; “Good Vibrations” (1991) credited her and handed her the only US pop No. 1 of her life.14 The family relaunched the imprint in 1992 and licensed the catalog to Verse Music Group in 2010. In June 2015 BMG bought Verse whole — Salsoul and West End among some 40,000 songs and recordings — and the masters keep returning in remastered and re-edited editions.15 A label that lasted eleven years has now spent four decades as source material.

Key records

  • “Salsoul Hustle” (1975, the Salsoul Orchestra) — the three-song Philadelphia session that named the label’s band
  • “Ten Percent” (Walter Gibbons 12” Mix) (1976, Double Exposure) — the first twelve-inch single sold to the public
  • “Doctor Love” (1977, First Choice) — Gold Mind’s disco peak
  • “I Got My Mind Made Up (You Can Get It Girl)” (Larry Levan version) (1978, Instant Funk) — the remix chair passing from Gibbons to Levan
  • “Love Sensation” (1980, Loleatta Holloway) — the raw material of two later No. 1s
  • “Let No Man Put Asunder” (Shep Pettibone 12” Mix) (1983, First Choice) — the catalog crossing into house
