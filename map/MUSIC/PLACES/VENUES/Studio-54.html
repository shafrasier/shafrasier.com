Venue

Studio 54

Address: 254 West 54th Street, Midtown, Manhattan
Active: April 26, 1977 – March 1980 (Rubell-Schrager era); reopened 1981 – April 1986
Owners: Steve Rubell and Ian Schrager; later Mark Fleischman

Wikipedia

The velvet rope did the performing. Studio 54’s founding insight was that the door itself was the show: Steve Rubell stood outside his own club choosing the cast for the night, sorting the crowd against categories of his own invention, admitting the beautiful and the famous and turning away what he called “the gray people” — among whom, he admitted, he would have counted himself and his partner had they not owned the place.1 “The perfect party is like a tossed salad,” he explained; some nights that meant waving in the limousine drivers of the stars to vary the mix.2 Andy Warhol, who came almost nightly, named the arrangement for the record: “a dictatorship on the door but a democracy on the dance floor.”3 For thirty-three months it worked, and then the money, which had been the point, ended it.

Why here, why then

The building had been in show business for fifty years. It opened in November 1927 as the Gallo Opera House, cycled through Depression-era names, and spent the postwar decades as CBS’s Studio 52, taping The $64,000 Question, Password, and Captain Kangaroo on the stage the dancers would later occupy.4 Rubell and Schrager, Syracuse fraternity brothers (Rubell ran a pair of steak restaurants), took the space with partners in 1976, spent about $400,000 and six weeks on the conversion, and kept the television infrastructure: moving sets, a thirty-by-forty-foot cyclorama, sixteen-foot poles of chase lights, and the effects rigs that let the room re-stage itself nightly for a crowd of two thousand or more.5 On opening night, April 26, 1977, some four thousand people showed up; the photographs of who got in made the next morning’s front pages — the business model announcing itself.6 Days later, at a birthday party the designer Halston organized for Bianca Jagger, Rubell surprised her with a white horse; she climbed on for a few minutes, the photograph circled the world, and the story grew until she was riding the animal through the front door. Her correction came decades later, in a letter to the Financial Times — “it is one thing to, on the spur of the moment, get on a horse in a nightclub, but quite another to ride in on one” — and it never caught the myth.7

The machine

Inside, the theater ran on spectacle synchronized to the sound: the man-in-the-moon set piece with its animated cocaine spoon descending toward the end of the night, the mirrored walls, the balcony with binoculars for watching the floor, the basement as the VIP sanctum where, by the Oxford case study’s account, prominent guests found the Quaaludes and cocaine complimentary.8 Promoters were paid per celebrity delivered, “$250 for a major one, $150 for a minor one,” and the house pantheon (Halston, Liza Minnelli, Warhol, Bianca Jagger, with Truman Capote, Salvador Dalí, and half of fashion in orbit) kept the pictures moving: front pages on opening day, the horse photograph fed to the press.9

The music history happened almost incidentally, and mostly in the booth. Richie Kaczor, the resident DJ from opening night, broke Gloria Gaynor’s “I Will Survive” from the 54 floor before radio touched it — the record that went on to win the only Grammy ever awarded for disco.10 Nicky Siano, the downtown master hired alongside him, lasted months: the room wanted big records timed to the light show, Rubell heard Kraftwerk clear half the floor and stormed the booth (“For instance! What the fuck is this song?!”), and the club and the selector parted; his account of the place — the cramped booth, the big-record light-show policy — is the working DJ’s view of a room that had no use for clergy.11 The door even wrote a hit against itself: turned away on New Year’s Eve 1977 despite a Grace Jones invitation, Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards went home and built the riff that became “Le Freak,” its original chant an obscenity aimed at the doorman, by the account the two told for the rest of their careers — Chic’s first No. 1, born of not getting in.12

The money and the fall

The club grossed seven million dollars in its first year, and Rubell could not stop saying so. “Only the mafia does better,” he exulted to a New York magazine columnist in November 1977; the club’s tax return that year declared net income of $47,000.13 The IRS read the interview. On December 14, 1978, acting on a tip from a passed-over ex-employee, about thirty agents raided the club and found the second set of books, a financial statement with a column labeled “skim,” some three hundred Quaaludes, and over a million dollars in cash hidden on the premises — while the club’s attorney, Roy Cohn, knocked over a table shouting “Look what these Nazis are doing!”14 Rubell and Schrager pleaded guilty to tax evasion, drew three and a half years each in January 1980, and threw themselves a farewell party on February 2 titled “The End of Modern-day Gomorrah”: Diana Ross and Liza Minnelli sang from the movable bridge above the floor, and Sinatra attended only as a record, “My Way” playing on the sound system.15 Two nights later, on February 4, 1980, they reported to prison; they were released on April 17, 1981, winning early release by informing on other clubs’ skimming practices — the door policy’s logic, applied one last time to their own industry.16

The eras of the room

Mark Fleischman bought the club out of the wreckage and reopened it in September 1981 under a license conditioned on the founders’ absence; the second era ran with less wattage until April 1986, when the club closed unable to obtain liability insurance.17 The founders’ own second act went the other way: their Palladium club, then the Morgans hotel — the beginning of the boutique-hotel industry Schrager went on to build. Rubell died of AIDS-related illness in July 1989, at forty-five, and Kaczor died of AIDS in 1993, at forty. Schrager received a full presidential pardon from Barack Obama on January 17, 2017, among the administration’s final acts.18 The building went back to the theater business it was built for: the Roundabout Theatre Company moved its Tony-winning Cabaret revival in during 1998, the house seats just over a thousand for Broadway runs today, and the basement that had been the VIP sanctum is now a cabaret room named 54 Below.19

What it changed

Studio 54 fixed the popular memory of an entire genre. Tim Lawrence has noted that disco “conjures up images of Studio 54” in “the popular imagination,” while the culture that invented the music ran downtown on membership cards; when the backlash came, he observes, the private parties “survived with ease,” and David Mancuso remembered the whole “disco sucks” campaign as “more of an out-of-New-York phenomenon.”20 The Paradise Garage, opened nine months later on the other model, built its decade around a DJ and a sound system where 54 built its three years around a door and a camera. What 54 gave nightlife permanently was the grammar of exclusivity: the rope-line as spectacle, the door as editorial. The same epidemic took the men who made both rooms, and only one of them is remembered by the people it let in.
