On December 2, 1889, in a friend’s Vienna parlor, Johannes Brahms sat at a piano with Edison’s recording funnel mounted underneath it and played an abridged Hungarian Dance into wax for the phonograph’s traveling agent.1 Recording had arrived to catch a tradition already ancient — and the cylinder itself, worn nearly to noise by decades of family playback, now survives mostly as hiss with a piano somewhere inside it.2 The gap in that scene is the tradition’s whole condition: fourteen hundred years of music, and recordings for barely a tenth of them. What holds the tradition together across the unrecorded centuries is its strangest habit: this is the music that learned to write itself down. And the writing, as the historian Richard Taruskin puts it in his Oxford History, “not only made history possible, but in large part determined its course.”3

The invention of writing

The writing began as politics. When the Carolingian kings allied with the Roman popes in the mid-700s, Charlemagne set out to impose Rome’s liturgy, texts and tunes alike, on every church in his empire, decreeing in 789 that clergy “emend carefully in every monastery and bishop’s house the psalms, notes, chants.”4 The texts traveled in books; the tunes could not, so Roman cantors taught Frankish singers by rote, each side blaming the other when it failed.5 The chant that emerged from the merger was named for Pope Gregory I, who had died almost two centuries before it was written down; the legend, complete with the Holy Spirit as a dove dictating into Gregory’s ear, was in Taruskin’s words “a propaganda ploy contrived to persuade the northern churches that the Roman chant was better than theirs.”6 The first notation, the neumes, solved only half the problem: little marks tracing a melody’s rise and fall, useless unless you already knew the tune.7 The real invention came around 1026, when the Benedictine monk Guido of Arezzo set the notes on the lines and spaces of a staff — “each sound,” he wrote, “is found always in its own row” — and named the scale-steps with syllables cut from a hymn, the ut-re-mi that still trains singers.8 Guido was summoned to Rome, where Pope John XIX tested the system himself and, by Guido’s account, would not leave his seat until he had sung an unfamiliar verse from the page.9 A melody could now travel without a voice to carry it. Within two centuries the consequence had a name attached: at Notre-Dame in Paris, Léonin (“the best maker of organum,” a later witness records) assembled the Magnus liber, the Great Book of polyphony for the church year, and Pérotin then revised it, “and made many better clausulas, he being the best discantor.”10 That sentence is the tradition starting its engine: once music is written, a successor can correct it, improve it, and build on it, and composition becomes cumulative.11

The six periods

The conservatory’s period scheme (medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, Classical, Romantic, modern) is a set of conveniences, and the tidiest boundary shows why: the Baroque conventionally ends in 1750 because Bach died that year, though the galant style that displaced his had been fashionable for decades and his own sons led it. The dates mark deaths and publications; the sound always changed earlier, and gradually.12 Still, the eras name real hearable shifts. The Renaissance let the third, the sweet middle note of the chord, into the structure of polyphony, and the sound warms from hollow ceremony into harmony a modern ear recognizes; Josquin des Prez rode the new warmth to the first true composer-fame, placed first in the first printed book of motets in 1502, praised at Luther’s table as the master of the notes when other composers were mastered by them.13 Palestrina’s serene counterpoint then became the church’s model of correctness — though the beloved story that his Missa Papae Marcelli saved polyphony from a ban at the Council of Trent is a legend first recorded half a century after the fact.14

The Baroque’s signature is the continuo: a bass line with numeric shorthand over which a keyboardist improvises the harmony in real time, a rhythm section three centuries before the term. Opera arrives with it: the day before Monteverdi’s L’Orfeo opened at Mantua in 1607, a court official could describe the new thing only as a play in which “all the actors are to sing their parts.” Vivaldi industrializes the concerto, five hundred of them, four published with sonnets describing the birdsong and storms the music paints.15 The era closes on a scene: Bach at Potsdam in May 1747, handed a chromatic theme at the fortepiano by Frederick the Great and answering, in the Musical Offering, with the most learned counterpoint of a style already going out of fashion.16

The Classical period turned instrumental music into plot. A Baroque movement sustains one mood; a sonata movement is a drama — two themes in two keys set against each other, developed through instability, resolved by homecoming — which is why a Haydn symphony can be witty and a Mozart movement can play like an opera scene without words.17 The string quartet had an accidental birth: Haydn’s quartets began, his first biographer records, when a baron at a country house asked for music his foursome of amateurs could play.18 Its verdicts were delivered in parlors: in February 1785, after an evening of quartets at Mozart’s apartment, Haydn told Mozart’s father, “Before God and as an honest man I tell you that your son is the greatest composer known to me either in person or by name.”19

Romanticism made expression the organizing value and the performer an idol. Heinrich Heine coined “Lisztomania” in 1844 for the frenzy around Liszt’s recitals — admirers fought over his gloves and made bracelets from his broken piano strings.20 Berlioz handed the audience at his Symphonie fantastique premiere a printed program narrating the lovesick story that ends in an opium dream. Balakirev’s circle of self-taught Russians (an army officer, a chemist, and a naval officer among them) built a nationalist music in deliberate refusal of the German academy. And Wagner ended the century’s crescendo by requiring a purpose-built theater at Bayreuth and four evenings of an audience’s life for one work.21 The boundary into the modern era is a flute: Debussy’s faun, Paris, 1894, a tritone left hanging in the air, unresolved — and from there the century of refusals belongs to Modern classical, serialism to minimalism to the living wave.22

The forms

The forms cut across every period, and each was invented somewhere specific. The symphony grew out of the Italian opera overture’s fast-slow-fast frame, then found its orchestra at Mannheim, whose court band the visiting historian Charles Burney called “an army of generals, equally fit to plan a battle, as to fight it.”23 Vivaldi’s L’estro armonico, published in Amsterdam in 1711, spread the concerto’s soloist-versus-orchestra scheme across the continent, and Bach copied out six of its concertos for his own study.24 Opera became a public art in Venice in 1637, when the Teatro San Cassiano first sold tickets, to anyone who could pay, for what had been a court spectacle.25 The oratorio’s most famous night belongs to Dublin: Handel’s Messiah, April 13, 1742, a charity benefit — debtors’ relief among the causes — so oversubscribed that women were asked to come without hoops and men without swords.26 And “sonata form” itself was named only in 1845, by a theorist abstracting a rulebook from what Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven had simply done — the textbook written after the practice.27

The museum turn

For most of its history the tradition performed the music of the living. The turn came in the nineteenth century, and its founding scene is Berlin, March 11, 1829: the twenty-year-old Felix Mendelssohn conducting Bach’s St Matthew Passion — cut nearly in half — to a capacity house with the Prussian king, Heine, and Hegel in it and a thousand ticket-seekers turned away, the first hearing any public outside Leipzig had of the work in the seventy-nine years since Bach’s death.28 The canon scholar William Weber has shown the shift was gradual (most old works had never entirely left circulation), but from the mid-1860s the dead began eclipsing the living on concert programs in earnest, and they have never given the stage back.29 The philosopher Lydia Goehr dates the deeper change to around 1800: only then did musicians begin treating pieces as fixed, repeatable works, objects for a permanent collection, rather than occasions.30 The concert ritual followed the museum logic. Mozart delighted in a Paris audience that burst into applause mid-movement in 1778; a century later Bayreuth’s quasi-religious hush spread outward, and by about 1910 the reverent silence between movements had hardened into law.31

The wing abroad and on record

The tradition stopped being geographically Western in the same decades it became a museum. Vienna and New York founded philharmonics in the same year, 1842. Shanghai’s orchestra began as a public band in 1879, and Japan built conservatory training the same year, importing a Boston music educator to design its school curriculum; the Tokyo Music School opened in 1887.32 Recording then rebuilt the economy of the whole tradition. Arthur Nikisch and the Berlin Philharmonic cut Beethoven’s Fifth in 1913, a reduced orchestra crowded around a metal horn, and established the pattern that still governs the catalog: the repertory is re-recorded, generation after generation, which is why dead composers dominate the shelves — every era makes its own Beethoven cycle.33 The re-recording economy even produced a reform movement: period-instrument performance, from Nikolaus Harnoncourt’s Concentus Musicus Wien of 1953 to the Academy of Ancient Music the harpsichordist Christopher Hogwood founded in 1973 to “rediscover their works” by “returning to the colours, the style and clarity of their individual periods” — the museum restoring its own paintings.34 The streaming era conceded the tradition’s strangeness outright: Apple bought a classical-only service in 2021 and launched a separate classical app two years later, an admission that a catalog built on works, movements, and rival recordings of the same piece would not fit fields designed for songs.35

The edges of the wing

The wing’s one branch still writing new orchestral music for a mass audience is Cinematic classical, the film-score tradition; a John Williams score belongs to this lineage however the record stores shelve the soundtrack. Operetta and Singspiel are the doorways through which the concert tradition drains into the musical stage, and that border stays open by design. Scott Joplin’s classic ragtime sits inside the wing on the strength of what it always was: notated, formal concert music, whatever the concert halls thought at the time. Stravinsky’s interwar neoclassicism revisits eighteenth-century manners with modern ears, a backward glance kept apart from the modern wing’s forward motion. The tape and laboratory practices that grew from the postwar studios belong to Electronic: the studio, not the concert hall, is their home ground. And the name itself carries the wing’s oldest unresolved tension: “Western” describes a birthplace, no longer a boundary — the tradition’s practitioners work from Tokyo to Caracas, its living repertory folds in Azerbaijan’s symphonic mugham and American gamelan.36
