Funk

Periodc. 1965 – ongoing
Influences

Early in 1965, on a stopover between one-nighters, James Brown pulled his band into Arthur Smith Studios in Charlotte and cut “Papa’s Got a Brand New Bag” in about an hour, in a single take he had intended as a run-through — he was still reading the words off a sheet.1 The take held a new place to stand. Brown drove the whole arrangement onto the downbeat and heard the band as a drum kit: “I was hearing everything, even the guitars, like they were drums.”2 The record spent eight weeks at No. 1 on the R&B chart, reached the pop top ten, and won Brown a Grammy, and the rhythmic decision underneath it became a genre.3 Funk is what happened when R&B’s groove pole seceded and built a country of its own: rhythm promoted from accompaniment to subject, the band locked into one pattern the way a congregation locks into a chant, every instrument a percussion instrument with a pitch. Disco’s story ends in a crash; funk’s never does. It ran forward without interruption, into the machines and into the present tense.

The One

The founding had a name, and Brown’s musicians learned it as a discipline before anyone wrote it down. The One is the first beat of the bar, hit by everyone at once, the anchor that lets everything between the downbeats scatter into syncopation. “Cold Sweat”, cut in May 1967 at King Records’ Cincinnati studio, went further: a groove essentially without chord changes, assembled overnight by bandleader Pee Wee Ellis after Brown grunted him a rhythm in a dressing room. “I then worked on it on the bus from New York to Cincinnati. I started to design the bass line to ‘Cold Sweat’,” Ellis remembered — and the horn line came from jazz: “I had Miles Davis and his ‘So What’ in mind… My jazz soul influenced me and sneaked into his R&B sound, and it came out Funk.” Asked whether Brown ever acknowledged the debt: “No. He thought that was from him. Everything was always from him.”4 Public television’s obituarists would later compress Brown’s whole method into one phrase — “mercilessly reducing every instrument to a percussive role” — and the records bear it out.5 “Funky Drummer”, cut at sunrise on November 20, 1969 because the label needed a single, carried Clyde Stubblefield’s eight-bar break, and Brown’s spoken instruction to his drummer survives on the record itself: “You don’t have to do no soloing, brother, just keep what you got… Don’t turn it loose, ‘cause it’s a mother.” The break’s second life belongs to Sampling.6

In 1970 the classic band walked out over money, and Brown’s answer rebuilt funk’s future by accident. He flew a Cincinnati bar band called the Pacemakers to Georgia in his Learjet overnight — among them an eighteen-year-old bassist, Bootsy Collins, and his guitarist brother Phelps “Catfish” Collins — and within weeks had christened them the J.B.’s.7 “Get Up (I Feel Like Being a) Sex Machine”, sketched on a paper sandwich bag on the bus and cut in two takes that April, is the new band’s arrival. What Brown gave Bootsy in return was the doctrine itself: “‘On the one’ is a musical term I learned from James Brown. He wanted me to play on the one, to emphasize that first beat of every measure.” Decades later Bootsy was still preaching it as philosophy: “The power of the one is all of us collectively going in the same direction together.”8

The family across the bay

San Francisco built funk’s second engine on a different premise: that the band itself could be the argument. Sly and the Family Stone, formed in 1966–67, was the first major American group to include Black and white men and women, with Cynthia Robinson’s trumpet and Rose Stone’s keyboards putting women in the engine room at a moment when neither pop nor soul did.9 The optimism years ran fast: “Dance to the Music” into the top ten in April 1968, “Everyday People” to No. 1 for four weeks in early 1969, a Woodstock set in the pre-dawn hours of Sunday, August 17, 1969 that many attendees ranked the festival’s best.10 The band’s bassist had brought his own invention with him. Larry Graham had developed his technique as a teenager in his mother’s drummerless lounge trio, inventing a substitute for the kit’s backbeat: “When my mom decided she was no longer going to have a drummer, that’s when I started thumping the strings — to make up for not having that bass drum — and plucking the strings to make up for not having the backbeat on the snare drum.”11 He called it thumpin’ and pluckin’; the world calls it slap bass, and “Thank You (Falettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)”, No. 1 for two weeks in February 1970, is its founding document on record.12 The technique traveled — into jazz through Marcus Miller, who put the inheritance plainly: “He was one of my heroes when I was growing up, and I learned all his songs.”12

Then the argument turned inward. There’s a Riot Goin’ On (1971), made largely alone in the attic studio of a rented Bel Air mansion and at the Record Plant in Sausalito, replaced the band with overdubs and the drummer with a Maestro Rhythm King preset box; “Family Affair”, No. 1 for three weeks from December 4, 1971, was the first chart-topping single built on a drum machine.13 Critics heard the darkness immediately. Vince Aletti, reviewing it that month, confessed he “began to respect the album’s honesty” after first hating its exhaustion, and called it “one of the most important fucking albums this year”; Greil Marcus later fixed its place in one sentence — “It is Muzak with its finger on the trigger.”14 The utopian band was over; the groove survived, and Graham carried the thumb to Oakland, where Graham Central Station made it a lead instrument from 1973.15

The Mothership

George Clinton ran funk’s third engine as a cosmology. His route began in a Plainfield, New Jersey barbershop quintet, the Parliaments, whose 1967 hit “(I Wanna) Testify” arrived just as their label collapsed — and the contractual wreckage cost Clinton the group’s name.16 His workaround became the structure of an empire: he signed the backing band to Westbound as Funkadelic, guitar-heavy and acid-fried, then relaunched the regained name as Parliament, horn-forward and smooth — the same ten musicians under two contracts, aimed at two audiences at once.17 Funkadelic’s Maggot Brain (1971) opens with ten minutes of Eddie Hazel guitar recorded in one take, after Clinton told him to play the first half as though his mother had just died and the second as though he had learned she was alive; Clinton then faded the rest of the band down and left the grief exposed.18

Parliament built upward. Mothership Connection (1975) put Black voyagers at the center of space — “Afronauts, capable of funkitizing galaxies,” in Clinton’s phrase — and “Give Up the Funk (Tear the Roof off the Sucker)” became the organization’s first million-seller in 1976.19 That October the metaphor became stagecraft: the P-Funk Earth Tour opened in New Orleans with a smoking, light-studded Mothership descending to the stage, designed by the Broadway lighting designer Jules Fisher on a million-dollar loan from Casablanca. “I wanted to have a prop that not only was deeper than anything that any black group had done but bigger than any white group had done,” Clinton said.20 More often than the credits said, the empire’s engine was Bernie Worrell, a classically trained keyboardist who had performed concertos as a child; on “Flash Light” (1978), Parliament’s first R&B No. 1, the bassline is Worrell on stacked Minimoogs — “There’s three Moog’s: there’s the bass line, then there’s the little cartoony one, I’m having fun. It’s a conversation, that’s how I speak.”21 One Nation Under a Groove (1978) took Funkadelic platinum, and “Atomic Dog” (1982) closed the chart era: four weeks at No. 1 R&B without ever entering the Hot 100.22 The mythology — Starchild, Dr. Funkenstein, Sir Nose D’Voidoffunk and his war on the groove — now reads as a founding text of Afrofuturism, and the Smithsonian agrees: a Mothership stands in the Smithsonian’s National Museum of African American History and Culture, acquired in 2011 for its inaugural music exhibition — a vessel the museum frames as carrying audiences away from Earth’s racist confines to the liberated cosmos, in the lineage of Sun Ra.23 Clinton’s own definition of the music stays the loosest: “Funk is anything you need to be when it needs to be that.”24

The engines

Beyond its founders, funk ran on city-scale engine rooms. The strangest is Dayton, Ohio, a mid-size manufacturing city that produced the Ohio Players, Zapp, Slave, Lakeside, and Heatwave’s founding brothers — a concentration Rickey Vincent, whose 1996 history was the genre’s first, attributes to Great Migration families drawn by General Motors and National Cash Register jobs, public-school music programs, and a Battle of the Bands culture that let every band break nationally on its own.25 The Ohio Players put two singles at No. 1 on the pop chart in twelve months — “Fire” in February 1975, “Love Rollercoaster” in January 1976 — while Slave’s “Slide” took R&B’s summit in 1977 and Lakeside’s “Fantastic Voyage” (1981) named the whole city in its invitation to the Land of Funk.26 Zapp’s Roger Troutman ran his voice through a talk box on “More Bounce to the Ounce” (1980), a sound so durable that West Coast rap would build a subgenre’s vocal signature on it.27 Oakland answered with horns: Tower of Power, working since 1968, made the section itself the star, and “What Is Hip?” (1973) remains the band’s self-interrogating signature.28 And beneath all of it sits New Orleans, prehistory and parallel track at once: the Meters, house band for Allen Toussaint’s operation, cut “Cissy Strut” in 1969 — Zigaboo Modeliste’s drumming carrying the second-line parade rhythms that the scholar Alexander Stewart has traced directly into funk’s rhythmic transformation of American pop.29

The crossover decade

Funk had already conquered the movie house: Isaac Hayes’s Shaft score and Curtis Mayfield’s Superfly made Black film scoring a funk franchise at the decade’s start, the album out-earning the picture it argued with. The harder question arrived in the late seventies: whether to follow the groove onto disco’s floors and pop radio. The answers reshaped careers. Kool & the Gang lived both halves: a Jersey City jazz-funk unit whose “Jungle Boogie” and “Hollywood Swinging” (1973–74) later fed hip hop’s crates, then — after hiring the producer Eumir Deodato and their first dedicated frontman, James “J.T.” Taylor — the makers of “Celebration”, a 1981 pop No. 1 now in the National Recording Registry.30 Earth, Wind & Fire made precision the argument: Maurice White’s “Shining Star” (1975) topped both the pop and soul charts, with the Phenix Horns supplying the brass signature.31 The handoff to disco’s floors is Disco’s story; funk’s own ledger kept the harder edge — Rick James called his wing punk funk, and Street Songs (1981) earned the label’s crossover joke in one datum: “Super Freak” drew its Grammy nomination in a rock category.32

The genome

Funk’s second life began the moment hip hop needed something to stand on. On WhoSampled’s ledgers James Brown carries roughly nineteen thousand logged samples, the largest figure for any artist in the database, with the “Funky Drummer” break alone appearing in more than two thousand tracks.33 The P-Funk catalog became hip hop’s most-sampled band book: “Atomic Dog” logged in 381 songs, “Flash Light” in 229, “(Not Just) Knee Deep” in 216 — and G-funk assembled itself from them wholesale, Snoop Dogg’s “Who Am I (What’s My Name)?” stacking “Atomic Dog” and “Knee Deep” in a single record while Dr. Dre’s “Let Me Ride” lifted the “Mothership Connection” chant itself.34 Vincent’s history argues the point at book length: hip hop — G-funk most of all — is funk’s continuation, a fifth dynasty rather than a eulogy.35 In the gap between the eras, Prince carried the practice almost alone: Dirty Mind (1980), cut in his home studio with Prince playing nearly everything, made a fusion that AllMusic credits with setting “the style for much of the urban soul and funk of the early ’80s.”36 And the practice never stopped: in 2019 Vulfpeck, a funk band with no manager, no major label, and no hit single, sold out Madison Square Garden on an Instagram post.37 The One still holds.

Foundational records

  • “Papa’s Got a Brand New Bag” (1965, James Brown) — the downbeat flip, cut in one take between shows; the founding
  • “Cold Sweat” (1967, James Brown) — the near-one-chord groove; Pee Wee Ellis’s jazz line inside Brown’s rhythm engine
  • “Cissy Strut” (1969, the Meters) — New Orleans second line as instrumental funk, parallel track to Brown’s founding
  • “Thank You (Falettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)” (1970, Sly and the Family Stone) — Graham’s thumb at No. 1; slap bass enters the record
  • “Get Up (I Feel Like Being a) Sex Machine” (1970, James Brown) — the J.B.’s arrive; the One as a young band’s whole curriculum
  • “Maggot Brain” (1971, Funkadelic) — Hazel’s one-take elegy; funk’s guitar wing at full reach
  • There’s a Riot Goin’ On (1971, Sly and the Family Stone) — the turn inward: overdubs, a rhythm box, and the first drum-machine No. 1 in “Family Affair”
  • “Flash Light” (1978, Parliament) — Worrell’s stacked Minimoogs; the synthesizer becomes funk’s bass player
  • “One Nation Under a Groove” (1978, Funkadelic) — the movement’s anthem and its platinum peak
  • “Atomic Dog” (1982, George Clinton) — the last R&B No. 1 of the P-Funk era and the genome’s single richest source

Key artists

  • James Brown — the founder; the One as law, the band as drum kit, the most-sampled artist on record
  • Sly and the Family Stone — the integrated, mixed-gender band as utopian argument, then Riot’s dark reversal
  • Larry Graham — inventor of slap bass in a drummerless lounge trio; the technique every later bassist inherits
  • George Clinton — the impresario of the twin-band empire and its Afrofuturist cosmology
  • Bootsy Collins — Brown’s teenage bassist turned P-Funk’s star; the One carried between the two great camps
  • The Meters — New Orleans’s house band; second-line rhythm as funk’s deepest root
  • Roger Troutman — Dayton’s talk-box voice; the sound G-funk chose for its hooks

Borders

Soul is the voice pole to funk’s groove pole — R&B’s two-engine frame — and the border is porous in both directions: James Brown belongs to both histories. Disco took funk’s rhythm section onto the four-on-the-floor and returned the favor as influence; the exchange is committed on both notes. Jazz-funk is the border Herbie Hancock crossed with Head Hunters (1973), a story that belongs to Jazz as much as here. Go-go, funk rock, funk metal, and the Minneapolis sound each carry the groove into their own rooms — futures for their own tellings — and P-Funk’s empire is dense enough to earn a note of its own in time.
