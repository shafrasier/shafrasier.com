Genre

Electro-disco

Periodc. 1977 – c. 1984
Namedretroactively, by the collectors — no period usage has surfaced; the records were sold as disco
LocationMunich; Paris; San Francisco
Influences
DiscoKraftwerk
LinksRYM

In July 1977 a disco album arrived with a manufacturer’s disclosure printed on the back cover: “Only electronic keyboards were used on this recording.” The album was From Here to Eternity, by a producer billing himself simply as Giorgio, and the sleeve line was a boast dressed as a credit.1 Electro-disco is the strand it announced: disco with the musicians designed out, sequencers holding the groove, synthesizers playing the parts an orchestra had played the year before. The strand began in a Munich basement in the spring of 1977, spread through a handful of European studio auteurs, and was answered from San Francisco. Then it dissolved into nearly everything, which is why its records anchor so many origin stories — house and techno’s included.

The second basement

Giorgio Moroder built the room before the method. He lived in the Arabella-Haus, a Munich high-rise of hotels and restaurants, and put the studio underneath: “I found a basement, like this room, basically. Second basement. And I built a studio there.”2 Musicland Studios ran as a two-man line — Moroder writing the music, Pete Bellotte the lyrics, the production split between them — and by 1977 the line had already made Donna Summer a star.3

The record that opens the genre was the closing track of a concept album. I Remember Yesterday gave each song its own musical era, and the finale was supposed to sound like what came next; for it, Moroder borrowed a Moog modular from the Munich composer Eberhard Schoener, and the machine came with its own operator, Schoener’s assistant Robby Wedel.4 Wedel laid a reference pulse onto track sixteen of the studio’s Studer tape machine and used it to trigger the Moog’s sequencer, locking the synthesizer to the tape so that parts could be stacked in perfect time — a capability that, in the session’s telling, even Bob Moog did not know his machine had. “He was the true innovator,” Bellotte said of Wedel, “and he deserves so much credit.”5

The backing track for “I Feel Love” went down in two or three hours. Moroder fed the bassline into the sequencer and transposed it live from the keyboard, and at the mix he asked the engineer, Jürgen Koppers, for a short delay on it, doubling every note into the gallop that became the record’s signature.6 The percussion was Moog too: simulated hi-hat and snare, a low end that landed, in Bellotte’s phrase, “like a giant’s hammer on a wall.” One chair stays in dispute. Moroder has always said the machine could not make a kick — “it would give me ‘oomph’ instead of ‘dum’” — so their session drummer Keith Forsey played it live, alone, take after take; Bellotte remembers Forsey absent that day and the finished kick as synthetic as everything else.7

Munich heard an album track; Los Angeles heard a single. “Neil Bogart called back straight away and said, ‘The single is “I Feel Love”, it needs three edits and these are the edits,’” Bellotte remembered — the excised sections would have been sliced from the quarter-inch master and thrown on the floor.8 The single spent four weeks at No. 1 in Britain in the summer of 1977.9 The rest is Donna Summer’s story: the wordless line she floated over the pulse, and Brian Eno bursting into David Bowie’s Berlin sessions proclaiming the sound of the future.

The production line

The rest of the basement’s 1977 output arrived in a rush around the single. Munich Machine, the session crew billed as a band, led off in May with an album whose entire first side was “Get On the Funk Train”, a fifteen-minute suite. From Here to Eternity followed on July 22, recorded at Musicland in June, its “From Here to Eternity” title track carrying a vocoded vocal into the British top twenty while the back cover stated the method outright.10

Then a rock band came asking. Sparks, the Mael brothers’ Los Angeles art rock duo, heard the Summer single and went to the source: “We heard ‘I Feel Love’ by Donna Summer and thought it was an incredible mixture of electronics with a voice that had real warmth to it…. So we approached Giorgio Moroder,” Ron Mael said.11 No. 1 in Heaven (1979) swapped the band for the basement — Moroder producing, Forsey drumming — and put the duo back on the British charts, “The Number One Song in Heaven” reaching No. 14 and “Beat the Clock” No. 10.12 Mael measured the distance from even Kraftwerk: “It was a pretty lonely area to be. Kraftwerk were working in electronics, but we felt different, using electronics as part of a true band.”13

The studio auteurs

France produced the genre’s other auteurs, and their records crossed the Atlantic without a band or a tour. Cerrone, a drummer from the Paris suburbs who released his records through his own Malligator label, broke in America by clerical error. An employee at the Paris shop Champs Disques mis-shipped a box of his 1976 debut to a New York wholesaler. A DJ there, intrigued by the cover, tried the sixteen-minute title track, and “Love in C Minor” became a club hit with its maker unaware — the New York radio DJ Frankie Crocker even cut a competing version for Casablanca. Cerrone learned all of it at the MIDEM conference in Cannes. Sent to New York with his masters, he met Ahmet Ertegun, who offered him an Atlantic contract on the spot, and the single crossed onto the Hot 100 that February.14

“Love in C Minor” was orchestral disco, strings and all. The electro-disco record came when Cerrone decided his imitators had caught up: “I really wanted to stay original. As my first two albums had been hits, many productions had a sound close to mine.”15 At Trident Studios in Soho in 1977 he built Supernature around a machine he barely understood — ARP had given him an Odyssey, and he and his engineer stumbled into its sequencer: “we couldn’t stop that fucking dut-dut-dut-dut-dut, so I changed the chord and then it went dat-dat-dat-dat-dat.” They taped the pattern and layered the record over it. The lyric came off the street: during a session break Cerrone spotted Lene Lovich dancing with Hare Krishnas in Soho, invited her in, and asked her for words, and she handed him an environmental parable in which chemically swollen harvests wake something underneath that comes up for revenge.16 “Supernature” topped Billboard’s disco listing in early 1978 and reached the UK top ten that August after heavy play on The Kenny Everett Video Show — a lead single Atlantic had initially resisted, preferring the orchestral Cerrone it had signed.17

The other Paris auteur wore a helmet. Didier Marouani, a singer under contract to Polydor, wrote “Magic Fly” for an astrology television program that never aired, on an ARP Axxe bought in admiration of Tangerine Dream: “When I got the right sound, it took me five minutes to compose ‘Magic Fly’.” The producer Jean-Philippe Iliesco doubled the tempo and put a four-on-the-floor kick under it, and the drummer Joe Hammer spent four days locked to a bass sequence triggered by a Korg Mini Pops 120.18 Marouani, still bound to his label, hid inside his own record: the songwriting went out under “Ecama,” an alias built from his wife’s name and his surname, and the group — christened Space — performed in cosmonaut helmets so no one could recognize him. “Magic Fly” reached No. 2 in Britain that September, kept from the top, in the band’s telling, by the death of Elvis Presley. It reached America the crooked way, when Kebekelektrik, a Québécois studio act, put out an unauthorized cover that beat the original onto US floors.19

The helmets set a fashion. Space disco, the music’s science-fiction dress — laser zaps and cosmonaut staging — even supplied Sarah Brightman’s recording debut: “I Lost My Heart to a Starship Trooper”, cut with the dance troupe Hot Gossip, went to No. 6 in Britain in late 1978, its lyric besotted with a spaceman over laser effects and borrowed film themes.20 Under the costumes sat a shared working model. The producer was the act: Cerrone arrived with the record finished in his head (“When I go into the studio, everything is already decided in my head”), and Space was a construct three times over — a pseudonymous composer’s tune, reshaped by a producer, performed by hired hands.21 No band existed to tour; the records moved by import box and license instead.

The answer from San Francisco

America’s contribution began as a remix nobody had commissioned. In 1978 Patrick Cowley, then running the light show at San Francisco’s City Disco and recording on his own synthesizers, built a private extension of “I Feel Love” that ran nearly sixteen minutes, his own lines crowding over the Munich pulse. It circulated among DJs as a bootleg acetate.22 Vinyl came in June 1980 through Disconet, a subscription service whose mix programs went out to working DJs, never to stores; the sanctioned version waited until 1982, when Casablanca released “I Feel Love (Patrick Cowley Mega Mix)”, fifteen minutes and forty-five seconds of it, as a twelve-inch of its own.23 By then Cowley had a label of his own, Megatone Records, pressing the faster strain he and Sylvester were building for the city’s gay clubs; he died that November. That strain is Hi-NRG.24

The name nobody used

While it was happening, the music had every name except its own. The records charted as disco, unmarked; when period writing wanted the Munich end specifically, the word it reached for was Eurodisco.25 “Electro-disco” has no traceable birth — no coiner, no trade-paper usage from the era has surfaced — and the term lives instead in the collector taxonomies assembled decades later, which cannot even agree on it: one major reference tree makes it the formal parent of Hi-NRG, space disco, and Italo-disco, while the other leading database catalogs those three children and skips the parent entirely.26 Boogie’s name at least left a paper trail, a London DJ’s catchphrase surfacing in a 1981 advertisement; this one left none. It is a word applied backward to a strand that period listeners heard simply as disco’s European end. Only after the wing dissolved did anyone need a way to say where the machines had come from.

Year Zero

Munich never claimed the future it had made. “We did it just as an album track,” Moroder has said. “Donna finished in 10 minutes. Neither of us thought it would be as big as it’s been.”27 The testimony accumulated anyway, decade by decade. Fatboy Slim drew the house lineage: “‘I Feel Love’ was the first disco record I allowed myself to like, and obviously, that was a sort of pivotal thing because it was almost the first prototype house record.”28 New Order lifted the drum pattern of “Blue Monday” from Summer’s “Our Love” — “ripped off from a Donna Summer B-side,” as the band’s bassist put it.29 In Detroit, Juan Atkins and the DJs who founded techno kept “I Feel Love” in their sets, and Atkins’s own genealogy of the era runs straight across this wing’s floor: “funk became disco, then disco became new wave, then new wave and disco became house and techno.”30 The gay clubs that broke the record kept their own testimony. “Millions of gay people love Donna, and some say ‘I was liberated by that song’…. Jimmy [Somerville of Bronski Beat] told me he became a singer because of ‘I Feel Love,’” Moroder said.31

The wing ended by winning. By the middle of the eighties the method had no border left to defend — the machines were simply how dance records got made, from Italy to the top of the pop charts — and the strand stopped being separable from its children. By 1984 the sleeve line had nothing left to disclose.

Borders

Hi-NRG is one child: the same machines run faster in San Francisco’s gay clubs, with Cowley’s story continuing there. Italo-disco is another, the Italian industrialization of the template, with its German naming story told in its own note. Space disco is the third, the strand that dressed the arpeggios in costume for the Star Wars year. Euro-disco is the orchestral wing the 1977 split left behind — the same Munich rooms still on a string section’s payroll. Synthpop is the border with Electronic: the same machines writing three-minute radio songs while this wing built floor mixes, a merger told from that family’s side. And Boogie is the opposite answer to the same post-crash question — New York kept its players and hired the machines as section members; Munich fired the band.

Foundational records

  • “Get On the Funk Train” (1977, Munich Machine) — the basement crew billed as a band: a side-long suite from the Moroder–Bellotte line, on the street before “I Feel Love” was a single
  • “Magic Fly” (1977, Space) — the five-minute composition, the doubled tempo, the helmets; a UK No. 2 by an act that did not officially exist
  • “I Feel Love” (1977, Donna Summer) — the founding document: a sequenced Moog locked to tape, a voice, and four weeks at No. 1 in Britain
  • “From Here to Eternity” (1977, Giorgio) — the manifesto record, its sleeve declaring the all-electronic method in print
  • “Supernature” (1977, Cerrone) — the accidental sequencer riff that turned France’s orchestral auteur into a machine one
  • “The Number One Song in Heaven” (1979, Sparks) — the method applied to a rock band, by the band’s own request
  • “I Feel Love (Patrick Cowley Mega Mix)” (1982, Donna Summer) — the Munich original re-engineered from the dance floor, four years after its bootleg birth; the crate holds the object

Key artists

  • Giorgio Moroder — the architect: the basement studio and the sync trick, then the sleeve that told everyone
  • Donna Summer — the voice over the pulse; the career the machines orbited
  • Cerrone — the Paris drummer-auteur: drums up front, label of his own, and a sequencer discovered by accident
  • Space — the helmeted proof that the act could be a construct
  • Sparks — the first rock band to hand itself to the method whole
  • Patrick Cowley — the bootleg remixer whose fifteen minutes carried the sound into Hi-NRG
