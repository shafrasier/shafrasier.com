Italo-disco

Periodc. 1980 – c. 1988
Namedin Germany, 1983 — the ZYX label’s Best of Italo-Disco compilations put a sticker on the exports; the coinage is credited to Bernhard Mikulski, and no documentation of it has ever surfaced
LocationMilan; Bologna; sold everywhere else
Influences
Electro-discoEuro-discoSynthpop

Italo-disco wears a sticker its buyers printed. The records were made in Milan and Bologna by producers who sold them simply as dance music. The name came from Germany, where an import label began packaging the catalog as The Best of Italo-Disco in 1983 — and in Italy itself the word barely existed. “Until a few years ago nobody in Italy used the word Italo,” the producer Bottin said in 2013. “They called it ’80s revival.”1 Like Boogie, another strand christened from the buyer’s side of the counter, the genre is a catalog remembered under its buyers’ name. After euro-disco’s orchestras closed, Italian studios full of cheap synthesizers made records in deliberately broken English for Europe’s charts and, without noticing, sold Chicago’s DJs the parts kit for house music.2

Via Mecenate

The economics were the aesthetic. Imported vinyl was “prohibitively expensive” in Italy, as one genre history puts it: “Far more economical to have small ‘library’ runs made, or start independent labels and get it distributed directly.”3 So the industry grew its own labels. Discomagic, founded in 1979 by Severo Lombardoni, a conservatory-trained musician who had run a record shop near Bergamo and then a Milan wholesale business, grew into the country’s biggest dance label and distributor, its Via Mecenate headquarters anchoring the street that became northern Italy’s wholesale district.4 Il Discotto, which grew into its most serious competitor, ran from 1982 until the money gave out in 1987. A third operation, Baby Records, predated the name entirely, equally at home pitching a synth single at the discotheques and a ballad at the Sanremo festival; Gazebo’s international career started there.5 The labels pressed a producer’s music with a hired face and a borrowed tongue. Vocals were “sung in English, rarely in Italian” — or in Bottin’s blunter telling, the records were “Italian producers pretending to be English or American by singing in ‘English’ and using English-sounding monikers.”6 Nobody inside pretended otherwise. “It was cheap, mass-produced music,” Bottin said. “Quantity over quality and all that.”7

The routes out

Kano’s “I’m Ready” (1980), built in Milan by Luciano Ninzatti, Stefano Pulga, and Matteo Bonsanto from synthesizers, handclaps, and vocoder, went to No. 1 on Billboard’s disco chart and No. 21 on the R&B side — an Italian construct on Black American radio. Thirteen years later its groove resurfaced inside Tag Team’s “Whoomp! (There It Is).”8 Pino D’Angiò’s “Ma quale idea” (1980) took the domestic route in Italian: a talk-sung strut arranged by the jazz musician Enrico Intra, reportedly two million copies sold.9 Its bassline ran so close to McFadden & Whitehead’s “Ain’t No Stoppin’ Us Now” that D’Angiò spent years insisting he and his bassist had composed it independently. Bologna ran a stranger version of the export logic: Gaznevada, a band out of the city’s 1977 punk underground, drifted into electronic dance singles like “I.C. Love Affair” (1982) on a hometown label that called itself Italian Records to court the foreign market, then spent the following decades disclaiming the genre tag.10 Ryan Paris’s “Dolce Vita” (1983), written by Gazebo with the producer Pierluigi Giombini and pressed by Discomagic, went top five in Britain and No. 1 in five European countries.11 America had a door in from the start: Emergency Records, founded in 1979, “specialized in products of the Italian disco sound,” carried Kano’s American releases, and lived until 1988 on the international success of Shannon’s “Let the Music Play”, winding down at the decade’s end with its catalog sold to Unidisc.12

The German sticker

Germany printed the sticker. ZYX, a German label that had “specialised in prog-rock” until 1983, released a proto-Italo double LP titled Electric Dance Music, then the compilation series that stuck, with an Italo Boot Mix megamix series running behind it.13 The coinage is credited to the label’s founder Bernhard Mikulski — credited, and no more than that: “reliable third-party documentation has not been found to support whether ZYX label boss Mikulski himself named it, or whether ZYX was even the first to publish the term.”14 The sticker worked. In 1984, “thanks to the efforts of ZYX and returning hordes of holidaying teenagers, Italo started having major international success,” and “the hitherto faceless producers suddenly needed front people to cope with TV and live performance demands and thus enlisted models to perform live playback.”15 Baltimora, a Milan project active from 1984 to 1987, ran that arrangement at its purest. The Milan producer Maurizio Bassi met Jimmy McShane, a Derry-born singer and dancer who had toured in Dee D. Jackson’s stage show and supplied the robot voice on space disco’s “Automatic Lover,” and built “Tarzan Boy” (1985) around his face; by a later claim from inside the scene, the studio lead was Bassi’s own voice, as he had supplied for another act before, with McShane fronting the cameras. The single went to No. 3 in Britain and No. 13 in America. McShane died of an AIDS-related illness in 1995.16

The Chicago purchase

Importes Etc., a store tucked into a garage at 711 South Plymouth Court in Chicago’s Printer’s Row, is where the imports landed — British, Canadian, Italian, “stuff you couldn’t buy anywhere else” — under an owner, Paul Weisberg, who had started out spinning at the Playboy Club in 1977. Frankie Knuckles shopped it weekly, and the store’s signage is where “Warehouse music” got shortened to “house music,” a coinage claimed by the employee Dick Guenther and by Chip E. both.17 The radio bought in too. “There’s no denying that Italo disco served as a major influence to Chicago producers,” writes Jacob Arnold, the historian of the city’s house roots, whose inventories of the records that turned up in radio mixes, such as Farley’s, run through Doctor’s Cat’s “Watch Out” on Il Discotto and Klein + M.B.O.’s “Dirty Talk” on Zanza.18 At the Music Box, Ron Hardy’s sets folded the Italo imports in between Philadelphia disco and new wave.19 “Dirty Talk” (1982), a production by Mario Boncaldo and Tony Carrasco, reached No. 14 on the American club chart, and New Order took parts of “Blue Monday” from it, by Bernard Sumner’s own account.20 Detroit got its dose over the same air: Derrick May “listened to mix shows on WBMX and WGCI where DJs were spinning Italo cuts like ‘Feel the Drive’ by Doctor’s Cat,” and the staff at Importes Etc. pointed him to the Power Plant and the Music Box, where Ron Hardy’s sets left him saying “It changed my life.”21 The Italians themselves heard about their part late. “I don’t know if Chicago guys lifted from Italo,” Bottin shrugged in 2013. The man asking him was Mike Simonetti, whose label Italians Do It Better had made the revival its business.22

Dissolution

The market that made the genre unmade it. By the late eighties the discotheques were “engulfed in the pop dance trash typified by Stock, Aitken and Waterman and the flood of house and techno,” as the RBMA history has it; around 1987 the style hardened, on heavier beats, into Italo house, and in the early nineties the catalog split into Eurobeat, Italo house, and Italo dance.23 The same history traces the sound’s print forward onto New Order, the Pet Shop Boys, Carl Craig, and La Roux. The sticker survived. The crates keep getting bought, and the export outlived every studio that supplied it.

Key records

  • “I’m Ready” (1980, Kano) — three producers, a vocoder, and a groove hip hop was still sampling in the nineties
  • “Ma quale idea” (1980, Pino D’Angiò) — the rare one that stayed in Italian, jazz hands on the arrangement
  • “Dirty Talk” (1982, Klein + M.B.O.) — the record Chicago radio and Manchester both borrowed
  • “Dolce Vita” (1983, Ryan Paris) — the Gazebo–Giombini co-write on Discomagic’s own pressing
  • “Vamos a la playa” (1983, Righeira) — the Spanish-language summer single, issued country by country across Europe
  • “Tarzan Boy” (1985, Baltimora) — a Milan producer’s voice behind a Derry dancer’s face, the catalog’s pop-facing edge
