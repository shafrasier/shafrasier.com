Genre

Electronic Dance Music

Periodc. 1977–present
Namedthree times over — a London sleeve joke in 1980, the academy’s umbrella by the 2000s, the American industry’s rebrand by 2011
LocationMunich; Chicago and Detroit; then anywhere the grid reaches
Influences
Disco (through electro-disco)KraftwerkFunk
LinksRYM

In March 1980 a London band called Landscape released a synthesizer single, “European Man”, with a catalog number their label suggested as a piece of packaging wit: EDM 1. The back sleeve spelled it out — “Electronic Dance Music … EDM; computer programmed to perfection for your listening pleasure.”1 The joke took thirty years to become an industry. The phrase resurfaced on a New York awards night in 1995, settled into academia during the 2000s as the sober umbrella for everything with a programmed pulse, and then, in the early 2010s, was picked up by the American music business as the brand name for a festival boom.2 Underneath all three coinages sits the same music: the mainline that begins when disco hands its rhythm section to the machines, runs through Chicago and Detroit, and detonates in Britain. It goes underground in nineties America and comes back the size of a stadium.

Two senses of one name

Electronic dance music is the lineage. Dance is the floor’s history, the rooms and the nights; Electronic is the laboratory and the listening wing, ambient and IDM and the armchair avant-garde. Between them runs this branch: every genre built on a sequenced groove, from electro-disco and Hi-NRG through house, techno, trance, jungle and drum & bass, UK garage, and dubstep. The classifiers read it the same way, rooting the whole tree in disco’s machine wing and running it forward to the present.3 The name is the complicated part. To a civilian “EDM” means the drop-heavy main-stage sound of the 2010s; to the classifiers it means four decades of records, most of them made by people who never used the word.

The inheritance

The machines arrived before the genres did. In a Munich second basement, Giorgio Moroder and Robby Wedel synced a Moog to tape and built “I Feel Love” for Donna Summer, and Cerrone stumbled onto his sequencer’s pulse at Trident — the strand the collectors later named electro-disco, released across 1977, and the reason the branch’s clock starts there.4 The children came fast: Italo-disco, space disco, and the faster San Francisco strain Patrick Cowley built into Hi-NRG. The other inheritance was German. Kraftwerk’s synthesized pop crossed into Black American radio, and the splice of Kraftwerk over a beat built for a floor became electro, the bridge between the disco machines and what the Midwest made next. By 1983 the parts were on the table.

Birth certificates

Chicago named the first genre after a room. Frankie Knuckles played the Warehouse from 1977, and his own account of what the place was doubles as a definition of everything the lineage built afterward: “For most of the people that went there, it was church for them. It only happened one day a week: Saturday night, Sunday morning, Sunday afternoon.”5 The music the scene began to make in the club’s image reached vinyl when Jesse Saunders pressed “On and On” at the start of 1984 on Jes Say, the vanity label he and his partners set up to release it — Resident Advisor’s verdict on it is the consensus one, “made in 1983 and released at the beginning of 1984, kicked off a global movement,” and its description of the sound, “hollowed-out hi-NRG, reduced Italo,” is the inheritance heard plainly.6 Three years later Phuture pressed “Acid Tracks” and house gained its first fully machine-born dialect.7

Detroit’s certificate was issued in London. When Neil Rushton and Derrick May assembled a compilation of the music Juan Atkins, May, and Kevin Saunderson were making for Virgin’s 10 imprint in 1988, the working title was The House Sound of Detroit. The three voted it down: their music was not a regional house variant, and they favored a term they were already using — techno. The retitled Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit named the genre, Inner City’s “Big Fun” became its fall-1988 crossover, and the album itself failed to recoup — a fair summary of Detroit’s whole commercial arc.8

The export

Britain turned the imports into a mass culture. Chicago’s acid records and Detroit’s compilation landed on a club scene remade by MDMA, and out of the Second Summer of Love of 1988 and 1989 the American imports became the founding documents of a national youth movement.9 That story, the rave civilization and its descent through hardcore, jungle, and garage, is Energy Flash’s ground. Europe is where the branch first scaled: in the 1990s the music was massively popular there while America kept it underground, and the continental genres of that decade, hardcore, Eurodance, and trance, grew from the exported American seed.10

The American rave decade

The country that invented the music spent the nineties throwing parties for it in secret. Frankie Bones, a Brooklyn DJ who had played a British hangar rave to 25,000 people in 1989, brought the scale home: his Storm Raves began on May 11, 1991 in a Flatbush brickyard with 200 people, and by June 1992 were drawing over 3,000 to lineups that put Sven Väth and Richie Hawtin on American concrete.11 The scene’s four-letter creed came from the same crew — Bones’s brother Adam X painted “Peace Love Unity” on a train car on July 4, 1990, and the phrase grew its R and became PLUR.12 The archipelago spread. In rural Wisconsin the Furthur campouts ran from April 1994 on a Hixton motocross track, grew toward 5,000 a weekend, and put Daft Punk on the 1996 bill; in Los Angeles, Insomniac debuted the Electric Daisy Carnival at the Shrine Expo Hall in 1997; and the industry tried its first umbrella brand, “electronica,” on the UK acts of that year and watched it miss.13 Washington ended the decade’s run. The RAVE Act’s language, folded into the PROTECT Act and signed in April 2003, extended the crack-house statute to event promoters, with civil penalties of $250,000 or twice the gross, a law aimed squarely at the people who rented the warehouses.14

The brand

The boom began in a tent. On April 29, 2006, Daft Punk played Coachella’s Sahara stage inside an LED pyramid, 40,000 people trying to get into a 10,000-capacity tent, and by several accounts — among them that of Michaelangelo Matos, whose The Underground Is Massive is the boom’s standard history — the pyramid “lit the fuse for the LED arms race that would fuel the rise of the EDM genre.”15 David Guetta’s production of the Black Eyed Peas’ “I Gotta Feeling” (2009) then parked dance production at No. 1 on the Hot 100 for fourteen weeks, and the money followed the sound.16 The Electric Daisy Carnival, pushed out of Los Angeles after a fifteen-year-old attendee’s ecstasy death at the 2010 edition, moved to Las Vegas and drew a reported 230,000 in its first year there.17 Robert F. X. Sillerman founded SFX Entertainment in June 2012 to roll the boom up as a public company: he expected to spend close to $1 billion on acquisitions in the first year, bought Beatport and a majority of Tomorrowland’s parent ID&T, and took it onto the Nasdaq in October 2013.18

The name was part of the acquisition. “EDM” was pushed by the American industry and press in the early 2010s as a rebrand of rave culture, and Simon Reynolds called the maneuver plainly that August: the phenomenon “isn’t so much deja vu as a rebranding coup,” in which “EDM is what we used to know by the name of techno.”19 The musicians the brand advertised kept refusing it. deadmau5, the boom’s own arena act, told Billboard in November 2012 that “EDM is like, ‘Event-Driven Marketing’ is the acronym there,” and summarized the business as “minimal work for maximum profit, really.”20 From the underground, Seth Troxler called the term “a bit soulless and kind of an American-defined term for mass media and big business” — a word coined by publicists that “doesn’t really have anything to do with our culture.”21

The name outlives the boom

The brand crashed on schedule. SFX filed for Chapter 11 on February 1, 2016, and re-emerged under a different name entirely; by 2017 the Chicago DJ then performing as the Black Madonna, now The Blessed Madonna, could describe the scene as a horror film: “We’re definitely in the zombie moment of EDM. It’s dead, but the lumbering corpse is still walking around and fucking up FM radio.”22 Everything except the marketing survived the crash. The floors refilled around house and techno while the festivals kept selling out, and the taxonomies kept the phrase the industry had burned — as the name for the whole machine lineage, 1977 to now, most of it made before and after the boom the civilian sense of “EDM” remembers. The sleeve joke won: electronic dance music turned out to be a durable description and a disposable brand.

Further reading

  • The Underground Is Massive: How Electronic Dance Music Conquered America (2015) — the America-first telling: eighteen parties from the Chicago beginnings to the festival boom, and the argument that “EDM” names a format rather than a genre
