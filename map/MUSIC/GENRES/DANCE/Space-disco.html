Space disco

Periodc. 1977 – c. 1979
Namedretroactively, by the collectors — in the period the records were sold as disco with rockets on the sleeves
LocationParis; New York; Munich; London
Influences

On the afternoon of May 25, 1977, a session trombonist named Meco Monardo walked into the first showing of a new 20th Century-Fox picture; by the following night he had seen it four more times. “I was convinced that John Williams’s themes were recordable and danceable,” he said, and by October his conviction was the No. 1 record in America.1 Around records like his — electro-disco’s arpeggios in cosmonaut costume and borrowed film-score bombast — the record business built a boom it never bothered to name. The chronology runs backwards from the legend, though: the sound predates the movie. Space’s “Magic Fly”, cut in 1976, reached stores on Disques Vogue a month before Star Wars opened.2 The film supplied the market. For roughly two years anything wearing a helmet could chart, and a strand of European synthesizer disco went worldwide in costume.

The Star Wars economy

Meco’s record was an act of fan conversion run through session-industry professionalism. Born in Johnsonburg, Pennsylvania, to a musician father who started him on valve trombone, Domenico Monardo won a scholarship to the Eastman School of Music at seventeen. He spent 1965 to 1974 in the New York studios, playing dates for Tommy James and Diana Ross and arranging horns for Coke jingles. By 1974 he had turned producer, cutting Gloria Gaynor’s “Never Can Say Goodbye” in a partnership called the Disco Corporation of America.3 He pitched a danceable Star Wars to label after label and was told, by at least one executive, to get lost; Neil Bogart of Casablanca agreed to see the movie first, then steered the idea to Millennium, a Casablanca-partnered label run by Jimmy Ienner.4

The making was as improvised as the pitch. The soundtrack album was not yet out, so Meco talked a receptionist in Fox’s publishing department into handing him a copy of the score; he spent eight hours coaxing R2-D2’s chirps from early synthesizers and cut the record in three weeks with a seventy-piece orchestra, Tony Bongiovi producing alongside and Harold Wheeler arranging. Lucasfilm had supplied nothing: “I didn’t get the sound effects,” he remembered, and “George Lucas didn’t know I existed.”5 “Star Wars Theme/Cantina Band” hit No. 1 on the Hot 100 on October 1, 1977, held it for two weeks, and went platinum on two million American copies and four million worldwide.6 By the Guinness ledger it is the best-selling instrumental single ever released, and the album carrying it, Star Wars and Other Galactic Funk, outsold the film’s own soundtrack LP. Williams answered with a plaque carrying a handwritten note — “Dear Meco, thank you so much for this marvelous version. Sincerely, John Williams” — and then with the only rebuke that counted: at the Grammys, Meco’s nomination lost to Williams’s original.7 The market kept paying anyway, and Meco kept converting: Close Encounters, The Wizard of Oz, and The Empire Strikes Back all got the treatment and all reached the Top 40, and Christmas in the Stars (1980), a Star Wars Christmas album, carried the professional debut of a teenage Jon Bon Jovi.8

The costume economy

Europe had arrived at the costumes for its own reasons. Space wore cosmonaut helmets because their composer was hiding. Still under contract to a label that had rejected the track, Didier Marouani shot the London promo in disguise: “we dressed as cosmonauts, with helmets on our heads, so nobody would recognise me.” The dodge became the act. “Let’s forget all about Didier Marouani,” he decided; “Let’s make a band” — and Space stayed a construct, faceless by design.9 “Magic Fly” went to No. 2 in Britain, kept from the top, as the band’s chroniclers tell it, by the death of Elvis Presley, and to No. 1 in Switzerland and West Germany; “Just Blue” carried the band through 1978.10 British television built its own franchise. “I Lost My Heart to a Starship Trooper” (1978) paired the eighteen-year-old Sarah Brightman with Hot Gossip, Arlene Phillips’s risqué resident dance troupe from The Kenny Everett Video Show. The lyric name-checks Darth Vader and Flash Gordon over quotations from Star Wars, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, and Close Encounters; it reached No. 6 in Britain and went gold on half a million copies for Ariola’s Hansa operation.11 The singer at the microphone would originate Christine in The Phantom of the Opera eight years later — a West End career that began as space-disco novelty on a comedy show. By then the dress code ran continent-wide: Boney M., Euro-disco’s flagship act, titled their 1978 album Nightflight to Venus; I Signori della Galassia, an Italian act, worked in glam sci-fi costume. The sleeves did as much of the selling as the grooves.12

Munich’s robot romance

The strand’s Munich entry was sung by an English expatriate to a machine. Dee D. Jackson, Oxford-born and gigging wherever Germany would pay her, cut “Automatic Lover” (1978) for Munich’s Jupiter label, with the Munich bassist Gary Unwin writing and producing alongside Patty Unwin.13 The record is a dance-floor duet between a woman and a robotic suitor; the machine’s answering vocals came from Jimmy McShane, a Derry-born dancer who afterward toured Europe in her stage show. The single went to No. 4 in Britain, No. 1 in Brazil and Iceland, No. 2 in Sweden, No. 3 in Ireland, and No. 5 in West Germany, selling an estimated six to eight million copies across Europe.14 The concept album behind it, Cosmic Curves, played the theme across a side of galaxy police and meteor men, and the follow-up “Meteor Man” went to No. 1 in Italy. Jackson’s later itinerary doubles as the genre’s: when the space boom cooled she relocated to Italy, cut Italo singles, and opened a studio in Rome — the space-disco-to-Italo-disco handoff performed by one career.15

What space disco is not

“Cosmic disco” names a different thing: Daniele Baldelli’s practice. Baldelli came up at Baia degli Angeli under its American residents Bob Day and Tom Sison, and in April 1979 he took the opening-night decks at Cosmic, on Lake Garda, where he played 45s at 33 and the reverse — “you put it on at 45 but think ‘This is shit, it’s much better at 33’” — and fired a Korg 55 drum machine and a Yamaha CS10 synthesizer over records that ran from Pink Floyd and Frank Zappa to Ravi Shankar.16 Space disco was a product; cosmic was a technique, named after a venue, and two Italian DJs, Baldelli and Tosi Brandi Claudio, still independently claim its invention.17

Re-entry

The style burned out with the iconography that fueled it. By decade’s end the market had moved on, and the machinery dispersed into the strands beside it: the Italian one-person studios and Hi-NRG’s faster floors, with a mid-eighties spacesynth strain later blending the two.18 The revival came from Norway. Lindstrøm cut “I Feel Space” (2005) for his own Feedelity imprint, later licensing it to Playhouse, with Patrick Cowley’s remix of “I Feel Love” in his ears; it restarted the orbit. “That record was huge, it was absolutely everywhere. It kickstarted a new vibe,” the historian Bill Brewster said, and the record helped introduce Lindstrøm’s friends Prins Thomas and Todd Terje to the same floors.19 The costumes dated; the arpeggios didn’t.

Key records

  • “Magic Fly” (1977, Space) — Disques Vogue’s helmeted original, by a band that was a construct from the first photo shoot
  • “Star Wars Theme/Cantina Band” (1977, Meco) — two weeks at No. 1 on the Hot 100, platinum twice over
  • “Just Blue” (1978, Space) — the follow-up that kept the construct charting
  • “Automatic Lover” (1978, Dee D. Jackson) — Munich’s robot duet, UK No. 4
  • “I Lost My Heart to a Starship Trooper” (1978, Sarah Brightman & Hot Gossip) — a West End career’s first record, cut with a comedy show’s dance troupe
  • “I Feel Space” (2005, Lindstrøm) — the self-pressed Feedelity single that pulled the sound back into orbit
