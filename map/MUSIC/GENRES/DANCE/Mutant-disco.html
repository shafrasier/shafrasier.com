Genre

Mutant disco

Periodc. 1978 – c. 1984
NamedJune 1981, by the label — ZE’s compilation Mutant Disco: A Subtle Discolation of the Norm
Locationdowntown New York
Influences
DiscoNo waveFunkDub
LinksRYM

The genre began as a decision about tempo. “The first album I put out was James White and the Blacks, which was the Contortions slowed down to a disco beat, because they didn’t wanna sign to an unknown label and I wanted a dance band,” Michael Zilkha remembered of his label’s first year. “Then August Darnell from Kid Creole and the Coconuts slowed down Contort Yourself. That really became the basis for mutant disco.”1 What came out of that adjustment was a music that kept disco’s floor and broke the rest on purpose: angular guitars over danceable basslines, screaming and deadpan recitation where the diva should be.2 The name arrived in June 1981 on a compilation whose subtitle carried its own diagnosis, and it stuck to a scene one of its labels later described as “a small clubhouse of brash intellectuals, avant-garde crackpots, and underground flotsam calculatedly engineering a disco insurrection.”3

The repertory company

ZE Records was founded in 1978 by two men with no obvious business being partners: Zilkha, a British heir to the Mothercare retail fortune who had come through Oxford and the Village Voice, and Michel Esteban, a Parisian who had run the import shop Harry Cover, published the early punk magazine Rock News, and put out Patti Smith’s books in France.4 Zilkha’s roster logic was simple: “Basically, I signed all of downtown because no one else wanted it. I was building a repertory company.”5 The company’s range was the point. Cristina, a Harvard dropout writing theater criticism, sang the label’s first release, “Disco Clone,” produced by John Cale. Her 1980 remake of “Is That All There Is?” carried new, sexually caustic lyrics that so offended the song’s writers that Leiber and Stoller sued and had the record suppressed for years. Melody Maker had already called it “the most remarkable single of the month.”6 James Chance arrived from no wave’s ground zero with a James Brown obsession and released Buy (1979) as the Contortions, the same year the disco-tempo alias records went out as James White and the Blacks.7 August Darnell came from the opposite shore — Dr. Buzzard’s Original Savannah Band, his Bronx big band project that had already put swing, calypso, and disco in one gold record — and built Kid Creole and the Coconuts into the genre’s traveling theater, whose Tropical Gangsters went to No. 3 in Britain in 1982 with three Top 10 singles behind it.8 Lizzy Mercier Descloux, Esteban’s partner from the Paris rock press, made Press Color (1979) in a week of downtown studio time, a record that turned the Crazy World of Arthur Brown’s “Fire” into alien funk, and Was (Not Was) mailed the sound in from Detroit.9 The label even released Suicide’s second album, cut at the Power Station in 1980.10

The shop on MacDougal

The other label was a record store. 99 Records grew out of a corner of Gina Franklyn’s punk boutique at 99 MacDougal Street, where her boyfriend Ed Bahlman sold imported singles to a clientele that included Thurston Moore and Rick Rubin; it became a label in March 1980 when Glenn Branca needed someone to release Lesson No. 1.11 Two of its records rebuilt dance music’s future. ESG were four sisters from the South Bronx — Renee, Valerie, Deborah, and Marie Scroggins — whose mother bought them instruments for Christmas 1978 to keep them off streets the arson years were emptying. “Getting those instruments and that distraction was like, wow,” Renee Scroggins remembered. “It was like the window opened up and the sun came in.”12 Spotted by Tony Wilson at a Manhattan gig, they were recording with Martin Hannett in New Jersey within the week, and the song hip hop would mine for decades almost never happened: with three minutes of tape left at the session’s end, Hannett asked whether they had anything short. The answer was “UFO”: a track whose eerie sliding riff has since been sampled several hundred times, by Public Enemy and the Notorious B.I.G. among them.13 The money mostly never came back. “People were outright using our music and getting paid for it,” Scroggins said. “And I’m sitting there in a project apartment with my two kids basically hustling just to feed them.” ESG’s own 1992 single answered the situation in its title: “Sample Credits Don’t Pay Our Bills.”14

Liquid Liquid cut Optimo (1983) in a studio on the top floor of Radio City Music Hall, and its lead track “Cavern” — a Bo Diddley beat crossed with the rototoms of Curtis Mayfield’s Superfly — became a New York radio and club fixture.15 Within the year, “White Lines (Don’t Don’t Do It)” appeared on Sugar Hill, credited to Grandmaster and Melle Mel, with “Cavern“‘s bassline replayed nearly note for note by the label’s house bassist and its vocal hook reworded. Sugar Hill never cleared it. The lawsuit consumed more than $60,000 of the shop’s money over a year and a half, and 99 won a $600,000 judgment ($660,000 with costs). Sugar Hill declared bankruptcy two weeks after losing the appeal. “We never got anything,” the 99 staffer Terry Tolkin said. “And the store basically went bankrupt because of that.” Bahlman sold off the inventory in early 1986 and left the music business entirely. “People don’t normally turn their back on their art side as completely as Ed,” one musician from the label’s own roster marveled.16

The Russell wing

Arthur Russell belonged to the avant-garde the rest of the scene was defecting from, and to the floor in equal measure. The cellist and composer who had run the Kitchen’s music programming in the mid-seventies cut “Kiss Me Again” (1978) as Dinosaur, with David Byrne on guitar, and made “Is It All Over My Face?” (1980) as Loose Joints; a Larry Levan remix turned that record into a Paradise Garage anthem. As Dinosaur L he released 24→24 Music (1981) on Sleeping Bag, the label he co-founded, and its single “Go Bang! #5” became, in François Kevorkian’s mix, the record the rooms saved for the night’s height — cut-up horns and Julius Eastman’s organ over the Ingram brothers’ bass, Russell’s cello buried in the churn. The night the acetate reached The Loft, David Mancuso gave it four plays.17

The floors

The records needed rooms that would play a scream and a groove in the same hour, and the downtown scene had built them. At the Paradise Garage, Levan folded ESG’s “Moody” into Saturday Mass rotation; the group would eventually be invited to play the room’s 1987 closing party. At Danceteria, the resident DJs Mark Kamins and Sean Cassette worked twelve-hour shifts across the divide: “he would play PiL and I would go into a James Brown record, and it would work,” Kamins said, calling the club “an anti-Studio 54… It was real.”18 The circuit ran wider than any one address — one account of ZE’s reach lists the Peppermint Lounge, Club 57, Danceteria, the Pyramid, Dreamland, and the Saint — and it crossed the Atlantic: ESG played the opening night of Manchester’s Haçienda in May 1982, a room still under construction. “People are always asking what it was like and I always say ‘dusty,’” Scroggins remembered.19

The afterlife

The genre’s second life began in the same crates it had died into. “When I was DJing, playing Can, Liquid Liquid, ESG, all that kind of stuff, I became kind of cool for a moment, which was a total anomaly,” James Murphy said of the years before “Losing My Edge”, and when LCD Soundsystem played its 2011 farewell at Madison Square Garden, Liquid Liquid was the support act.20 The Rapture’s “House of Jealous Lovers” (2002) carried the sound back to rock clubs, and Soul Jazz’s 2003 compilation New York Noise — subtitled Dance Music From the New York Underground 1978–1982, running “no wave to mutant disco to hip-hop to art funk” — gave the era its standard reissue anthology the same year Esteban relaunched ZE from Paris.21 Through the floors of Levan, Frankie Knuckles, and the young Carl Craig, the catalog fed house and techno directly.22 Every generation of dance producers since has made the same discovery: a small body of records, made fast and mostly unpaid, that dance music keeps going back to.

Key artists

  • ESG — the Scroggins sisters: four South Bronx grooves that hip hop and house both mined
  • Liquid Liquid — percussion-first minimalism; “Cavern” is the sound’s rhythmic thesis
  • James Chance & the Contortions — no wave’s James Brown, the collision’s abrasive edge
  • Kid Creole and the Coconuts — Darnell’s mutant big band: the genre’s showman wing, and its British hits
  • Lizzy Mercier Descloux — the Paris transplant whose records got funkier and stranger with every city they were cut in
  • Cristina — the label’s satirist; disco sung with a raised eyebrow and a court injunction
  • Was (Not Was) — the Detroit outpost: funk, beat poetry, and studio players from both coasts of pop
  • Arthur Russell — the avant-garde’s ambassador to the floor, as Dinosaur, Loose Joints, and Dinosaur L
  • Material — Bill Laswell’s studio unit, the genre’s downtown session engine
  • Bush Tetras — 99’s other charting act; “Too Many Creeps” put no wave’s suspicion on the club chart

Foundational records

  • Contortions, Buy (1979) — no wave taught to dance; the record whose slowed-down alias versions became the idea’s basis
  • Lizzy Mercier Descloux, Press Color (1979) — the collision at its most playful: covers dismembered and rebuilt as funk
  • ESG, ESG (1981) — the Hannett session and its aftermath: six songs that fed three decades of samples
  • Various, Mutant Disco: A Subtle Discolation of the Norm (1981) — the christening: the label anthologizing its own idea
  • Dinosaur L, 24→24 Music (1981) — Russell’s recombinant disco; “Go Bang! #5” is the genre’s ecstatic peak
  • Liquid Liquid, Optimo (1983) — four rhythm workouts cut above Radio City; the bassline hip hop couldn’t leave alone
