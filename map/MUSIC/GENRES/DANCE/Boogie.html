Genre

Boogie

Periodc. 1979 – c. 1986
Namedretroactively, in London — in print by September 1981, a genre name by the Kiss FM era
LocationNew York (the records); London (the name)
Influences
DiscoFunkDub
LinksRYM

In early 1982 the record every New York floor wanted was D-Train’s “You’re the One for Me”: a gospel-sized baritone riding a bassline that came from a synthesizer, a groove set well below disco tempo, on a twelve-inch from a midtown independent label.1 Nothing about the record was called disco, because by 1982 almost nothing was; disco had become a word the industry had stopped saying. Boogie is the name for what the music did next — the R&B mainline of disco’s afterlife, made in New York between the crash and the rise of house, with drum machines beside live players, synthesizer bass where the string section used to be, and the tempo settled near 110 beats per minute. And the name is its own story: nobody making these records called them boogie. That word was applied years later, an ocean away, by the London DJs and collectors who refused to stop playing them.

The word goes radioactive

The crash arrived in the summer of 1979, and Disco Demolition was only its loudest night; the fall itself is Disco’s story to tell. What mattered downstream was the asymmetry of the damage. The majors fled the word, dropping rosters and scraping “disco” off the marketing, but the floors never emptied, and the audience that had always been the music’s core, Black and Latin and gay New York, kept buying twelve-inches.2 The records themselves changed name defensively. Radio and trade language slid toward “dance” and “club”; the music kept coming out of the same studios for the same rooms, pressed by independents small enough to live on club play. Downtown, the same post-crash years ran disco through no wave’s guitars; that wing is mutant disco’s story. The mainline stayed closer to the church of the groove: R&B singers, working rhythm sections, and a new machine or two, making dance records that no longer had a genre name anyone would say out loud.3

The sound

The clearest way to hear boogie is to notice what is missing. Disco’s signature had been the orchestra — the string dates and thirty-player sessions disco inherited from Philadelphia soul — and after the crash the budgets that paid for orchestras were gone. What replaced them was one musician at a keyboard. Kashif, a Brooklyn prodigy who had toured in B.T. Express as a teenager, built Evelyn “Champagne” King’s “I’m in Love” (1981) around a synthesizer bass line that did the work a bass player and half an arrangement used to do, and the record went to No. 1 on the R&B chart; his “Love Come Down” for King repeated the trick the next summer and crossed to No. 17 on the Hot 100.4 The tempo dropped from disco’s gallop to a lope. Leroy Burgess, who sang and arranged more of this canon than anyone, drew the line precisely: “Disco has more of a rigid type of beat to it and it’s faster than Boogie.” What boogie kept was the pulse — “The bass drum just goes like that… and then you get a body rock that moves along with it.”5

The machines arrived as section players, one more chair in the band. The Peech Boys’ “Don’t Make Me Wait” (1982) set LinnDrum beats inside a live band and then dubbed the whole arrangement out into open space: a record whose gaps and dropouts became as influential as its groove.6 Much of the canon passed through the same few rooms, above all Bob Blank’s Blank Tape Studios on West 20th Street, a 1,200-square-foot independent shop where the tape was always ready: “if you walked in at ten o’clock, a minute later you could record.”7 The result was a music of small means and large low end — handclaps, synth bass, a singer close to the microphone — built by session players who had survived the crash and were now working faster and cheaper than the orchestras ever had.

The labels

The economy that carried boogie was the one the majors abandoned. Prelude Records ran the model at its cleanest: Marvin Schlachter had founded the label in 1976 to chase the club sound, and his system moved records from New York floors outward: test pressings into the clubs first, acetates to the influential DJs, then radio and, if the record earned it, national distribution.8 The label’s house craft belonged to François Kevorkian, the A&R hire who became its mixer; his stripped, dubbed twelve-inch versions of the D-Train and Sharon Redd records are boogie’s production signature.9 West End Records, founded the same year by Mel Cheren, wired the economy directly into the Paradise Garage: Cheren was the financial backer of his former partner Michael Brody’s club, and West End’s records — “Don’t Make Me Wait” first among them — moved between the label’s office and Larry Levan’s booth like interoffice mail.10 SAM Records, Sam Weiss’s Long Island City operation, pressed more of its catalog in the eighties than the seventies — a disco label that became a boogie label by simply not stopping.11 None of these companies could afford a string section, and none of their records missed it.

The king from Harlem

If boogie has an auteur, it is Leroy Burgess, and his career contains the genre’s whole shape. He spent the first half of the seventies as the falsetto lead of Black Ivory, the Harlem sweet-soul group that Patrick Adams discovered and produced — “Patrick listened to us over the phone and liked us,” Burgess remembered. “That was my first opportunity to work on something serious.”12 He left in 1977, chafing at the format: “Black Ivory was largely typecast as a slow jam group, and to continue my growth as an artist in a changing musical landscape, I needed to discover who Leroy Burgess was.”13 The answer arrived in his sleep. “The groove for ‘Weekend’ came to me in a dream, which immediately woke me up and sent me to my Fender Rhodes piano” — and “Weekend”, cut in 1978 by Adams’s studio group Phreek, became a club standard durable enough that Sleeping Bag Records revived it five years later in a Levan mix.14

What followed was a one-crew genre. With James Calloway on bass and Sonny T. Davenport on drums, Burgess wrote, arranged, played, and sang across a run of records released under whatever name the label needed. Convertion’s “Let’s Do It” (1980) went out on SAM, and Frankie Crocker’s heavy rotation on WBLS carried it to the Garage. Logg’s 1981 album for Salsoul was produced by Greg Carmichael and mixed by Levan. “Get Down Friday Nite” (1982) came with the Aleem twins, neighbors in his own Harlem building, and Fonda Rae’s “Over Like a Fat Rat” (1982) started as a demo at Blank Tape.15 His own description of the method doubles as a definition of the genre: “With James on bass, Sonny on drums and myself on Fender Rhodes, we developed and created our own sound and style using our jazz and gospel training, combined with a generally slower than disco rhythm.”16 The crown came later, from the diggers: “Before we knew it, boogie became a genre all its own,” Burgess said, “and I was being heralded as its ‘King’ thanks to records like the LOGG album and ‘Let’s Do It’.”17

Named from London

The name reached the genre backward, through London, and its paper trail is short enough to quote. In the late seventies, the DJ George Power worked the West End club Crackers and called his best dancers “boogie boys” over the microphone; by September 1981 the word was in print, in a Blues & Soul ad promising “Jazz On Top! Soul, Funk ‘n’ Boogie Down Below.”18 At the time, British scenes filed these imports under blanket terms — soul, disco funk, or electro-funk in the North — and the records circulated through import shops and all-dayers as the specialist end of the soul scene.19 The word hardened into a genre name in the mid-eighties, when Power co-founded the pirate station Kiss FM and its DJs began using “boogie” for the early-eighties New York records they were still playing years after the American industry had moved on.20 The name carried an older American echo: boogie woogie, the piano-blues strain that made “boogie” a dance verb in the first place.21 Boogie was named the way extinct species are named, by the people cataloging what they loved after the market had declared it over. The catalog turned out to be alive.

Borders

To the electronic side sit the poles boogie never crossed into: Hi-NRG, which kept the pulse but dropped the funk and ran the tempo up, and electro-disco, the Munich-born wing that handed the whole arrangement to machines and passed the template on to italo-disco — adjacent strains sharing the same years. Electro is the nearer border: strip boogie’s live players out entirely, keep the drum machine, and the music slides toward the electro-funk that fed hip hop’s early records — a crossing point the British scenes heard so clearly they filed the two sounds side by side. To the pop side, the majors eventually took boogie’s synth-and-voice template back inside: what the industry sold as contemporary R&B through the mid-eighties was substantially the Kashif method with major-label budgets. And on the floor itself, the dubbed-out wing fed garage house, the strain that kept the Garage’s name and carried the sound into house’s era.

Legacy and influence

Boogie’s afterlife ran through the crate. When hip hop built its founding myths a decade later, the shelf it reached for was this one: the origin story the Notorious B.I.G. told in “Juicy” (1994) plays over Mtume’s “Juicy Fruit” (1983), the debt exact down to the instrumental mix the producers looped.22 The lifts run everywhere: Pete Rock built “Take the D-Train” on “You’re the One for Me,” the Prodigy lifted the same record for “Girls”, Eric B. & Rakim ran “Eric B. Is President” on the “Over Like a Fat Rat” bassline, and Daft Punk folded Vaughan Mason’s roller-rink anthem “Bounce, Rock, Skate, Roll” (1979) into “Da Funk” — the same shelf feeding French house.23 The ledger of those debts is Sampling’s. House owed a different debt. The dubbed-out Peech Boys template fed straight into what Chicago and New Jersey built next, and garage house carries the connection in its name, which is the Paradise Garage’s.24

Britain, having named the genre, made its own: Brit-funk, the homegrown sibling that moved from the jazz-funk clubs toward the charts, with Imagination — slower, synth-drenched, closest of the UK acts to the New York template — taking “Body Talk” into the top five in late 1981.25 Dâm-Funk built the modern-funk scene on Stones Throw around exactly this canon, spinning Prelude-era records at his Funkmosphere parties and refusing the genre’s kitsch memory: “I’m trying to change the cornball effect funk used to have.”26 Labels like Peoples Potential Unlimited turned the afterlife into an institution, reissuing private-press boogie twelve-inches that sold nothing in 1982 and trade as grails now.27 The second life has already outrun the first.

Foundational records

  • “Bounce, Rock, Skate, Roll” (1979, Vaughan Mason & Crew) — the roller-rink hinge between disco and boogie: the tempo already dropped, the bass already the hook
  • “Let’s Do It” (1980, Convertion) — the Burgess crew’s founding side for SAM; WBLS rotation to Garage anthem
  • “I’m in Love” (1981, Evelyn “Champagne” King) — Kashif’s synthesizer bass replaces the rhythm section’s bottom half; the synth-R&B template at No. 1
  • “You’re the One for Me” (1981, D-Train) — Prelude’s signature record: Hubert Eaves III’s keyboards, James Williams’s testifying lead, a mix shared by Eaves and Kevorkian
  • “Don’t Make Me Wait” (1982, Peech Boys) — Larry Levan’s dubbed-out West End production; the gaps that taught house its space
  • “Love Come Down” (1982, Evelyn “Champagne” King) — the crossover proof: No. 1 R&B, No. 17 pop, boogie passing as pop without surrendering the groove
  • “Over Like a Fat Rat” (1982, Fonda Rae) — the Blank Tape demo that became a classic and, a decade on, rap raw material
  • “Juicy Fruit” (1983, Mtume) — eight weeks at No. 1 R&B; the slow-burn record hip hop would build “Juicy” on

Key artists

  • Leroy Burgess — the genre’s voice, writer, and arranger; Black Ivory’s falsetto rebuilt as a one-crew canon under a half-dozen names
  • D-Train — James Williams’s gospel baritone over Hubert Eaves III’s synthesizers; Prelude’s flagship act
  • Evelyn “Champagne” King — the crossover voice of the Kashif era, from teenage disco hit to synth-R&B royalty
  • Kashif — the producer-writer whose synthesizer bass defined the post-orchestra sound
  • Mtume — the jazz-bred group that slowed boogie to a simmer and cut its most-sampled record
  • Peech Boys — the Paradise Garage’s house band; dub space and LinnDrum claps as a production philosophy
