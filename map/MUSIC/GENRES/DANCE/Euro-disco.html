Genre

Euro-disco

Periodc. 1975 – c. 1984
Namedin the period press — the UK magazine Blues & Soul was applying “Eurodisco” to Boney M. while the records were current; the collectors tightened the borders later
LocationMunich; Frankfurt; Paris
Influences
DiscoSchlagerEuropop
LinksRYM

The lyric sheet for the record that took Munich to No. 1 in America held six words. “Fly Robin Fly” had been called “Run, Rabbit, Run” until minutes before the session, when its writers changed the words. The hired session singers who cut it were not the women pictured on the sleeve or mimed in the videos. The finished record spent three weeks on top of the American chart in the winter of 1975 and won a Grammy, filed as Best R&B Instrumental Performance.1 Euro-disco is the industry behind that record: disco rebuilt as a Continental manufacturing operation, producer-owned, salaried orchestras behind interchangeable and often anonymous voices, American floor music sold back across the Atlantic with the R&B taken off the parts list. Munich, Frankfurt, and Paris each ran a version of the factory, and for a few years the factories out-charted most of what they imitated — an American No. 1 out of Munich in 1975, and a Paris drummer pressing five thousand copies of a sixteen-minute suite on his own money when his label flinched.

The Munich office

Silver Convention was two men and whoever was available. The two were Michael Kunze, a lyricist who had come up writing German protest songs, and Sylvester Levay, a composer who had arrived in Munich from Yugoslavia in 1972; they had charted in Britain with “Save Me,” a No. 30 hit, before the act had a face, its singers session hires the operation credited only as Ingrid, Wilma, and Monica. For the television bookings the hits demanded, they hired a separate trio — Penny McLean, Ramona Wulf, and Linda G. Thompson — to be the group the public saw.2 The follow-up “Get Up and Boogie” held No. 2 in America for three weeks in June 1976 and reached No. 1 in Canada on the same method, and by 1977 the operation was respectable enough to represent Germany at Eurovision, finishing eighth with “Telegram.” The act dissolved in 1979 with the American boom that had made it; the two writers went on to other manufacture, Kunze eventually to German musical theater.3 The rooms themselves ran on a shared labor pool: the imported British and Nordic session players who worked Munich’s disco dates were the same players Giorgio Moroder would soon put on a sleeve of their own as Munich Machine.4

The border inside Munich runs at the payroll. The same rooms and often the same players served Moroder’s operation, and the founding credit for euro-disco lands partly on him, alongside Farian and Kunze; the split came in 1977, when the sequencer began replacing the string section. After that, the orchestras were euro-disco and the machines were Electro-disco, Donna Summer’s Moog sides among them.5

The Frankfurt hoax

Frank Farian ran the model to its logical end: the act existed before the band did. In 1974 he cut a dance track called “Baby Do You Wanna Bump” alone, singing both the bass line and the falsetto, and released it in early 1975 credited to “Boney M.” — a name with no people in it yet.6 When television wanted the group, he hired one: on record, Boney M. was Liz Mitchell, Marcia Barrett, and Farian’s own studio-layered voice, while the dancer Bobby Farrell performed to full playback, miming vocals that were mostly Farian’s.7 The uncredited voices multiplied as the hits did; the spoken gangster prologue on “Ma Baker” was cut by a woman whose name never reached a label.7 “Daddy Cool” (1976) went to No. 1 in West Germany; “Ma Baker” (1977), rebuilt from a Tunisian folk song Farian’s assistant heard on holiday, reached No. 2 in Britain with only “I Feel Love” above it — the two poles of European disco stacked on one chart.8 “Rivers of Babylon” (1978), Psalm 137 by way of the Melodians’ 1970 rocksteady setting, sold more than two million copies in Britain; only six other singles have matched that on pure sales. The album behind it carried “Rasputin,” and the same year the group made a much-publicized promotional visit to the Soviet Union; only a handful of Western acts were allowed in, Elton John the other name usually cited. “Rasputin” itself stayed unreleased there for its subject matter.9 Farian would later run the same playbook to disgrace with Milli Vanilli; he died in 2024 with both acts on the ledger.10

The Paris opus

France’s contribution was scale. Cerrone was a drummer out of the percussion band Kongas, spotted playing in St. Tropez by the label owner Eddie Barclay; for his solo debut he flew to London, to Trident Studios, with Alec R. Costandinos writing alongside and London session professionals, Alan Hawkshaw and Snowy White among them, in the chairs. The result was “Love in C Minor” (1976), sixteen minutes of orchestral disco that gave the genre its template.11 His label doubted it: “Barclay was unsure of the record’s commerciality, so Cerrone started his own label, Malligator and pressed 5,000 copies of the LP to distribute around France.”12 Costandinos, Cairo-born of Armenian and Greek parents, spun off a run of orchestral concept albums under names like Love & Kisses and the Syncophonic Orchestra — sixteen albums in three years, by one count.13 His disco Romeo & Juliet took the top of Billboard’s disco chart in 1978 and is credited with bringing the concept album to dance music. Meanwhile, Munich’s Ariola machine added Amanda Lear. Her “Follow Me” (1978), composed by her producer Anthony Monn, went top ten in six European countries, sold several million copies worldwide, and finished as West Germany’s sixth-biggest single of the year; the album carrying it, Sweet Revenge, remains her best-seller.14 Forty-five years later a perfume campaign gave the single a second life, sending it to a million Shazam searches in a single month of 2023.

The end of the orchestra

By the turn of the decade the model had franchised across the Continent: the Netherlands ran Luv’ and Teach-In, Spain answered with Baccara, and Germany’s festival-pop wing stretched from Arabesque to Dschinghis Khan.15 That sprawl is why the period press never held the term tight. Retrospect has been harder on the records than the floors were: even an admiring reappraisal of “Fly Robin Fly” concedes that away from the dancefloor “a sound that starts out hypnotic and electrifying can gradually become exhausting.”16

The payroll was the genre, and the payroll became optional. By 1983 the E-mu Drumulator had put a programmable rhythm section on sale for $995, and a producer who had needed a string date and a chorus could make a dance record alone; the one-person studios of Italy and Hi-NRG’s faster gay-club strain took the floor, and the session-orchestra economy wound down around 1984.17 The name outlived the orchestras the way “disco” outlived the boom — the German press hung “Eurodisco” on the mid-eighties machine-pop duos that followed, Modern Talking the most successful of the line, and in the nineties French house arrived as a deliberate return to the seventies records, digging the Cerrone and Munich catalogs back out of the crates.18 The factories closed; the product became source material.

Key records

  • “Fly Robin Fly” (1975, Silver Convention) — six words, three weeks at US No. 1, and nobody on the sleeve sang a note
  • “Daddy Cool” (1976, Boney M.) — the Frankfurt method’s first West German No. 1
  • “Love in C Minor” (1976, Cerrone) — the sixteen-minute Paris-London opus the genre is measured against
  • “Ma Baker” (1977, Boney M.) — a Tunisian folk melody at UK No. 2, behind only “I Feel Love”
  • “Follow Me” (1978, Amanda Lear) — Ariola’s disco voice, top ten across six countries
  • “Rivers of Babylon” (1978, Boney M.) — Psalm 137 as one of the seven two-million UK singles
  • “D.I.S.C.O.” (1979, Ottawan) — the formula spelling its own name at the decade’s edge
