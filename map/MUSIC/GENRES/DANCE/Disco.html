Disco

Periodc. 1972 – c. 1982
Namedc. 1973–74 — from Vince Aletti’s first report on the discotheque scene to Billboard’s first disco chart
LocationPhiladelphia and New York, then Miami and Munich
Influences
Philadelphia soulFunkGospelLatin and African percussion

Disco begins with a drummer who wanted to dance. In September 1973, Philadelphia International released “The Love I Lost,” a Gamble and Huff song written as a ballad for Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes, and it came out the other end of the session at a rolling uptempo stride because the man at the kit pushed it there. “The Bluenotes were not a disco group, they did mostly ballads,” Earl Young remembered. “I said ‘Look man, let’s pick this up because I like to dance.‘”1 What he played became the genre’s signature and then its definition: the bass drum striking all four beats, the hi-hat ticking sixteenths with the cymbals sighing open on the offbeats, a pulse a DJ could find in the dark. Young built it that way on purpose — “everything that I cut is at 120 speed,” he said. “I made it that way so that the disc jockeys could mix my music into anything that they’re playing.”2 The record went to No. 7 on the pop chart, sold a million copies, and is conventionally cited as the first disco single. The claim matters less than what it marks: the order of events. The sound came first, built inside Philadelphia soul by the company’s session musicians, and the scene it needed was already waiting.

The engine room

The sound had an address. Joe Tarsia opened Sigma Sound Studios at 212 North 12th Street in August 1968 (“I hocked everything, even my house,” he said of financing it), and Gamble and Huff moved their operation in at once: “everything from that point that Kenny and Thom did in Philadelphia, they did at Sigma.”3 The house players who cut the label’s rhythm dates worked under the name MFSB, an orchestra with strings and horns on staff, and the arranging economy that resulted — Thom Bell’s charts, strings voiced like weather — put a symphony inside the dance record as a standard production line. Their own instrumentals proved the engine could run without a star: “Love Is the Message” (1973), and “TSOP” (1974), the label’s Soul Train theme, which put the house band itself at No. 1.4 The city even exported the apparatus whole: in 1974 the Cayre brothers financed Vince Montana Jr., MFSB’s vibraphonist, to staff the Salsoul Orchestra from the same pool of Philadelphia players, sections that could run to eighteen violins: one studio’s payroll becoming a genre’s sound.5

The press and the charts arrived while the records were still warm. Vince Aletti filed the first report from the scene in Rolling Stone in September 1973 — “Discotheque Rock ‘72: Paaaaarty!” — and described a culture already complete: “The best discotheque DJs are underground stars, discovering previously ignored albums, foreign imports, album cuts and obscure singles.”6 The following summer the floor’s sound took consecutive weeks at No. 1 on the pop chart — the Hues Corporation’s “Rock the Boat,” then “Rock Your Baby,” cut in Hialeah, Florida for TK Records — and that October Billboard began printing a disco chart; the first record to top it was Gloria Gaynor’s “Never Can Say Goodbye.”7 The industry had found the floor.

The mix and the twelve inches

Disco’s second invention was a format, and it happened twice by accident. Tom Moulton, watching Fire Island dancers lose the thread every time a three-minute 45 ended, built a continuous mixed tape for the Sandpiper — “I made one mixed side of 45 minutes and it took me 80 hours,” he said — and carried the idea onto vinyl: side one of Gaynor’s Never Can Say Goodbye (1975) ran three songs as one unbroken nineteen-minute suite, commonly described as the first of its kind on a commercial album.8 Then, in a mastering room out of 7-inch blanks, the engineer José Rodriguez cut a Moulton acetate on a 12-inch disc; to fill the surface they spread the grooves, and the record came out louder and deeper than any single had sounded. “I know what we’ll do: we’ll spread the grooves and make it louder,” as Moulton told the story.9 The club had its format. Salsoul commercialized it in May 1976: Walter Gibbons’s mix of Double Exposure’s “Ten Percent,” a DJ stretching a three-minute song toward ten by running two copies of the drum breaks against each other, became the first 12-inch single the public could buy. “When we put out ‘Ten Percent’ as a 7” single, the song really didn’t take off,” Ken Cayre said. The Gibbons mix took off; Cayre’s first reaction to his remixer had been “Walter, you’re crazy and you’re gonna get me killed by the producers!”10 The consequences outran the format. A record company had put a DJ’s version on sale over the producer’s, and the remix became a form of authorship: an argument that runs through Dance’s whole history, and a craft Sampling later turned into an engine.11

The boom

The second half of the decade belongs to the producers. In Munich, Giorgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte built Donna Summer’s records at Musicland like engineering projects: Love to Love You Baby (1975), whose title track was stretched to nearly seventeen minutes at Casablanca’s request so it could fill an album side and a floor at once, and “I Feel Love” (1977), built on a Moog borrowed from the composer Eberhard Schoener, whose assistant showed Moroder how to lock the machine to a click track. Everything on it is synthesized except Summer’s voice and, by Moroder’s account, the kick drum. “The Moog was totally… what’s the word? Out of tune, all the time,” Moroder said. “I had to go back and go ‘dum,’ ‘dum,’ ‘dum,’ tune it again.”12 The tuning trouble was worth it; the record is the hinge between this genre and everything electronic that danced after it. The American boom ran on showmanship: Casablanca, Neil Bogart’s label, staged disco as spectacle from Sunset Boulevard offices decorated like the film set (“Everybody got a leased Mercedes,” in the label history’s telling), while Chic ran the opposite temperament in New York — Nile Rodgers’s clipped rhythm guitar locking against Bernard Edwards’s melodic bass until the space between them was the hook, on “Le Freak” and Risqué.13 Behind the stars ran a voice economy the credits barely acknowledged: First Choice on the Philadelphia labels, Loleatta Holloway belting “Love Sensation” (1980) into permanent sample-bank service — singers from gospel backgrounds whose performances outlived their contracts, and who, in Holloway’s later case, saw nothing when new hits were built from her vocal.14

Then the boom went total. The Saturday Night Fever soundtrack, released November 15, 1977, sat atop the album chart for twenty-four straight weeks and became the best-selling album in history to that point. WKTU in New York flipped to the format in July 1978 and rode a 0.9 share to the top of the market inside months — a whole industry reading the same ratings book.15

The glut and the crash

What died in 1979 was inventory. The majors had flooded the format until nearly every roster carried a disco record, and the novelty shelf reached its terminus in The Ethel Merman Disco Album, Broadway standards redone four-on-the-floor by a seventy-one-year-old belter just as the market turned.16 The industry’s own accounting mechanism turned the correction into a rout: records shipped with a return privilege, so a “gold” shipment was a wager, and when the wager failed the records came home. Casablanca had learned the joke early — its 1974 Johnny Carson album, in the comedian Robert Klein’s line, “shipped gold, and came back platinum” — and in 1979 the joke became the business, returns arriving at a scale that turned the boom’s paper triumphs into losses.17 Casablanca itself, reportedly grossing enormously while sinking deeper into debt, was absorbed by PolyGram, which found the books tens of millions of dollars underwater and put Bogart out in early 1980.18 Radio flipped back as fast as it had flipped in; Chicago’s WDAI, whose rock-to-disco switch had put Steve Dahl out of a job, was a rock station again by May 1980, and Disco Demolition, the night Dahl staged at Comiskey Park in July 1979, had already given the collapse its lasting image.19 The correction fell hardest on the people the music came from — the gospel-schooled voices, the Philadelphia players, the Black, Latino, and gay floors that had built the culture the majors briefly rented — and the same crossover decade had quietly pushed the veteran soul singers off the majors and into Soul blues’s company town. Billboard closed the era the way industries do, by renaming the paperwork: from the issue of March 27, 1982, the disco chart became “Dance/Disco,” and the first word slowly ate the second.20

The borders and the children

The genre’s descendants split by geography and voltage. Boogie, the name British DJs later gave the slower, synth-warmed records New York labels like Prelude pressed after the crash, carried the sound into the early eighties at funk tempo. The European strains went electronic. Euro-disco sped the tempo and traded the American rhythm section for Europop melody; Italo-disco, the name Bernhard Mikulski’s ZYX label stuck on its Italian-import compilations in 1983, pushed all the way into drum machines and vocoder chill.21 In downtown New York, mutant disco ran the beat through no wave’s angular guitars on the ZE and 99 labels. Hi-NRG kept the pulse, dropped the funk, and ran the tempo up in the gay clubs of San Francisco, while electro-disco names the wing where Moroder’s machines replaced the band outright; nu-disco, the 2000s revival strain, applied modern production to the old grammar and earned its own bin at Beatport by 2008.22 The nearest non-descendants matter as much: dance-pop absorbed disco’s beat into Pop’s factory system and, with contemporary R&B and synthpop, eclipsed the genre it borrowed from, while house was built in Chicago partly from disco’s own crates (Frankie Knuckles called it “disco’s revenge”), its founding chapter opening at the Warehouse inside dance’s second act.23 The revival that began in the late 2010s brought the sound back at pop scale: Random Access Memories had already put Rodgers and Moroder on the same album in 2013, and Future Nostalgia and Renaissance carried the four-on-the-floor back to the center of pop.24 In 2025, Philadelphia renamed a stretch of street in the drummer’s honor: Earl Young Way, the city’s name for the corner the beat came from.25

Key artists

  • Donna Summer — the voice the genre answered to, and the Munich records that electrified it
  • Giorgio Moroder — the producer as author: the Munich apparatus behind Summer, and the synthesizer future
  • Chic — Rodgers and Edwards playing disco as rhythm-section discipline
  • Gloria Gaynor — the first chart’s first name, and the survivor’s anthem that outlived the backlash
  • MFSB — the Sigma Sound engine room: the house orchestra the genre was built inside
  • The Trammps — Earl Young’s own group, and “Disco Inferno’s” gospel-fired peak

Foundational records

  • “The Love I Lost” (1973, Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes) — the beat arrives: conventionally the first disco single
  • “Love Is the Message” (1973, MFSB) — the engine room’s instrumental, the floors’ private anthem
  • “Rock Your Baby” (1974, George McCrae) — TK’s Miami hit at No. 1, the boom’s starting gun
  • “Never Can Say Goodbye” (1974, Gloria Gaynor) — the first chart’s first No. 1; the 1975 album it titled carries Moulton’s side-long mix
  • Love to Love You Baby (1975, Donna Summer) — Munich stretches the song to the length of desire
  • “Ten Percent” (1976, Double Exposure) — the first commercial 12-inch: the DJ’s version goes on sale
  • “I Feel Love” (1977, Donna Summer) — the almost-all-synthesizer pulse the future ran on
  • Saturday Night Fever (1977) — the boom made an album: twenty-four weeks at No. 1
  • Risqué (1979, Chic) — the craft at the cliff’s edge, “Good Times” already funding the next genre
