Artist

Robert Johnson

Erac. 1929–1938
InstrumentVoice, guitar
Genres
Delta bluesAcoustic blues
ListenRYM·Apple Music

Keith Richards first heard Robert Johnson at Brian Jones’s flat, on the one record player, and asked the obvious question: “Who is the other guy playing with him?” There was no other guy. “I was hearing two guitars, and it took a long time to actually realize he was doing it all by himself,” Richards said later. “Robert Johnson was like an orchestra all by himself.”1 The man behind that sound had been dead almost a quarter century by then, and his whole recorded life amounts to five days: twenty-nine songs cut in a San Antonio hotel room and a Dallas office building in 1936 and 1937. Exactly one became a hit, selling perhaps ten thousand copies. He was poisoned at a Mississippi juke joint in 1938, dead at twenty-seven, and no newspaper reported it.2 From that sliver of a career grew the most contested reputation in American music: to Eric Clapton the most important blues musician who ever lived, to his sharpest revisionist a minor figure whose absence would have changed little.3 Between the two verdicts stands the documented man — a guarded, ambitious professional who practiced in graveyards because they were quiet — and his story is stranger than the devil invented to explain him.

The kid with the racket

Robert Leroy Johnson was born May 8, 1911, in Hazlehurst, Mississippi, spent much of his childhood in Memphis, and landed around 1919 in the Delta cotton country near Robinsonville, where his stepfather beat him for playing guitar instead of picking cotton.4 At seventeen he married fourteen-year-old Virginia Travis; she and their newborn died in childbirth while he was on the road, and relatives blamed the music he would not put down.5 What the neighbors heard in those years was not promise. Son House, newly arrived in Robinsonville and playing Saturday-night dances with Willie Brown, remembered the teenager who grabbed the guitar between sets: “And such a racket you never heard! It’d make the people mad, you know. They’d come out and say, ‘Why don’t y’all go in and get that guitar away from that boy! He’s running people crazy with it!‘”6

Then Johnson vanished south to the Hazlehurst country, and the year that made him is the part the legend erased. His teacher was Ike Zimmerman, an Alabama-born guitarist who never made a record, took Johnson into his house for about a year, and drilled him nightly in the local cemeteries — as Zimmerman’s daughter remembered it, “I can remember hearing that music. ‘Cause it sounded just so good just like they was competing, he was teaching him then.”7 The graveyard was a practice room, chosen for the plainest reason the biography could document: it was quiet, it was isolated, and nobody interrupted.8 Greil Marcus caught what the neighbors made of the arrangement: “Zimmerman liked to tutor unformed guitar players in a graveyard at midnight; some called him the devil for his ability to turn apparently unremarkable young men and women into masters.”9 When Johnson walked back into Robinsonville with a technique nobody could account for, House was flattened: “he showed us something, and we didn’t believe what we saw… I said to old Bill: ‘That boy’s good.‘”10

The gap between the racket and the mastery is the hole the myth poured into. The sentence every retelling leans on — House saying Johnson “sold his soul to the devil to play like that” — reached print in a 1966 Down Beat piece by Pete Welding, who never shared a recording of the interview, and the fuller House interview published the year before tells the transformation story with no devil in it.11 The crossroads bargain itself belonged to another Delta blues man entirely, Tommy Johnson, and migrated to Robert after his death.12 His traveling partner Johnny Shines, asked whether Johnson ever told him the story: “No, he never told me that lie. If he would’ve, I would’ve called him a liar right to his face. You have no control over your soul.” His stepsister Annye Anderson: “The stories about his dealing with the devil took away from his real talent.”13

The working professional

The talent worked for tips. Through the 1930s Johnson lived on the road — juke joints, street corners, levee camps, one failed run at the Major Bowes Amateur Hour in New York — at times with Shines, who remembered a closed, watchful partner: “He didn’t talk very much. He thought all the time.”14 A tip crowd paid for what it wanted, and the working songbook ran far past the blues: hillbilly hoedowns, pop tunes, whatever Bing Crosby had on the radio. That is the Johnson recovered in Elijah Wald’s Escaping the Delta: “a brilliant and savvy pop musician.”15 Peter Guralnick heard the pop instincts inside the delivery itself, a singer who “croons like the Bing Crosby records that he evidently admired, but with a sexual intensity that makes it seem as if he is crooning obscenities.”16 What that combination did to a room, only Shines really recorded: in St. Louis, after a slow “Come On in My Kitchen”, “I noticed no one was saying anything. Then I realized they were crying — both women and men.”17

Five days in Texas

The recordings happened because the race-record business sent a mobile unit south. In November 1936 Johnson reached San Antonio, where Don Law, recording for the American Record Corporation’s Vocalion label, had set up equipment in room 414 of the Gunter Hotel. Johnson cut eight sides on November 23 — including “Terraplane Blues” and “I Believe I’ll Dust My Broom” — spent the gap between dates in the Bexar County jail, and returned on the 26th and 27th, cutting “Cross Road Blues” among the final sides.18 Vocalion issued “Terraplane Blues” backed with “Kind Hearted Woman Blues” in March 1937, and it became his one hit: a jukebox record across the South that sold, by his biographers’ estimate, up to ten thousand copies.19 That June, Law recorded him again in Dallas, thirteen more sides across a June weekend, among them “Hellhound on My Trail” and “Me and the Devil Blues”. He was paid cash per song and no royalties; twenty-nine songs and forty-two takes survive.20

Law told a story that Johnson recorded facing the wall, and for decades it read as country-boy shyness — Clapton: “I immediately identified with that, because I was paralyzed with shyness as a kid.” The biography reads it colder: Johnson habitually played with his back to other guitarists, guarding his fingerings and his unconventional tunings from rivals.21 The takes themselves back the professional. Wald: “Robert Johnson certainly was very conscious of what a hit record sounded like.” The sessions opened with songs modeled on recent hits, and the arrangements were built tight to the 78’s two and a half minutes.22 Inside those limits sat the technique Richards mistook for a duet: a thumbed boogie bass, a figure that had lived on piano, walking under stabbed rhythm chords while a slide answered the voice.23 Over it all sat the singing, which could crack into falsetto mid-line like a man interrupting himself: on “Hellhound on My Trail” the voice thins and frays at the top of its range while the bass keeps walking underneath, and on “Come On in My Kitchen” the slide tracks the vocal so closely the guitar seems to finish his sentences.

Three Forks

In August 1938 Johnson was playing a juke joint at the Three Forks crossing outside Greenwood when he took sick mid-performance; he died on August 16, at twenty-seven, with no doctor attending and no autopsy. The account that survived came down through Honeyboy Edwards, who was there and told it all his life: whiskey laced by a jealous husband, days of agony.24 The death certificate surfaced only when the researcher Gayle Dean Wardlow dug it out of the Mississippi records three decades later, and the fullest modern reconstruction, in Conforth and Wardlow’s biography Up Jumped the Devil, concludes he was poisoned: possibly mothballs dissolved in the corn whiskey, possibly never meant to kill.25 So little was settled that three Leflore County churchyards now hold markers for him; the accepted grave, at Little Zion Missionary Baptist Church north of Greenwood, was corroborated by the widow of the man who dug it.26

The resurrection

The first man to canonize him never got to meet him. John Hammond had praised Johnson in New Masses in March 1937 — “the greatest Negro blues singer who has cropped up in recent years… Johnson makes Leadbelly sound like an accomplished poseur” — and wanted him for the “From Spirituals to Swing” concert at Carnegie Hall in December 1938. Word came back that Johnson was dead. Big Bill Broonzy took the slot, and Hammond played two of Johnson’s records, “Preachin’ Blues” and “Walkin’ Blues”, from the Carnegie stage.27

The records then waited a generation. In 1961, Columbia’s Frank Driggs assembled sixteen sides from Law’s masters as King of the Delta Blues Singers; it never charted, and it made the reputation anyway.28 Hammond passed an advance copy to Bob Dylan, who remembered his hair standing up from the first note and hearing “perfected pieces” where he expected customary blues, and who later named the working debt: “If I hadn’t heard the Robert Johnson record when I did, there probably would have been hundreds of lines of mine that would have been shut down.”29 The LP sits in the record clutter on the cover of Bringing It All Back Home.28 In England the same album hit the young British blues guitarists hardest. Clapton found it almost unlistenable in the best sense — “Robert Johnson wasn’t entertainment. When I first heard him I had a very hard time listening to him, because it demanded something of me” — and in 1968 Cream straightened “Cross Road Blues” into regular twelve-bar rock as the live “Crossroads” on Wheels of Fire.30 The Rolling Stones learned “Love in Vain” from a bootleg of the unreissued sides and cut it country-style on Let It Bleed, crediting it to “Woody Payne,” one of Johnson’s pseudonyms; Led Zeppelin taped a “Traveling Riverside Blues” of their own for the BBC in 1969.31 When the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inducted its first class in 1986, Johnson entered with it, as an Early Influence.32

The strangest chapter came last. In 1990 Columbia issued The Complete Recordings: forty-one takes, one alternate short of everything that survives, with booklet essays by Clapton and Richards. A box of 1930s hotel-room 78s reached No. 80 on the Billboard 200, won the 1991 Grammy for Best Historical Album, and sold over a million copies.33 Clapton’s essay carried the line the reputation now rests on — “the most important blues musician who ever lived” — and in 2004 he recorded Me and Mr. Johnson, fourteen of the twenty-nine songs: “It is a remarkable thing to have been driven and influenced all of my life by the work of one man.”34 The scarcity fed the aura. Through the entire revival no photograph of him was public at all. A dime-store self-portrait surfaced in Rolling Stone in 1986 and a Hooks Bros. studio portrait in 1989, and those two stood alone until Anderson’s 2020 memoir Brother Robert added an accepted third.35 The estate followed the same arc from neglect to windfall: a Mississippi court ruled Claud Johnson, a retired gravel-truck driver, the singer’s son and sole heir, and royalties that never existed in 1938 paid him over a million dollars.36

The argument

Every generation of writers has needed a different Robert Johnson. Greil Marcus opened rock criticism’s account in Mystery Train (1975), hearing “a world without salvation, redemption, or rest”; Guralnick’s Searching for Robert Johnson (1989) conceded the vanishing point (“No one seems to have any idea where the music came from”) and defended the myth as devotion: “the soul-selling legend is the highest tribute that could possibly be paid to Johnson.”37 The revision answered hard. Wald’s verdict that in the evolution of Black music Johnson was “an extremely minor figure” relocated the story to the white revival that built him. Robert Johnson: Lost and Found (2003) went further: the familiar images of Johnson were “the product of ignorance and economics,” invented to sell a dead man’s music to the white fans who arrived decades later. And Up Jumped the Devil (2019) set out to return him “to his human particulars.”38 All of them were listening to the same forty-two takes: five days of work carrying ninety years of argument. Richards’s question is still the best one asked of them — who is the other guy playing? — and the answer is still that there was never another guy.
