Artist

Arthur Russell

Era1973–1992 (posthumous canon from 2004)
InstrumentCello, voice
Genres
Avant-folkArt popMutant discoAmbient popSinger-songwriterChamber music
ListenRYM·Credits·Apple Music

In the spring of 1979, the Kitchen’s former music director booked two nights of his own music into the room that minimalism had made its sanctum, and brought a drummer and two percussionists. The run was called 24 to 24 Music: Arthur Russell’s cello with organ, saxophone, and trombone over a groove, played to an audience of composers. His friend Steven Hall called the booking “a careful, well-staged affront to the Kitchen people and their sensibilities,” and the composer Arnold Dreyblatt remembered the room’s verdict: “nobody knew what the hell to make of it.”1 In the back, a few people danced — among them the DJ Nicky Siano, whose club had taught Russell what a dance floor was. That night holds the whole career: one musician insisting, against the certainties of every scene he belonged to, that the composers’ loft and the disco were the same room. “He was way ahead of other people in understanding that the walls between concert music and popular music and avant-garde music were illusory,” Philip Glass said of him. “He lived in a world in which those walls weren’t there.”2

Iowa, the commune, the school

Charles Arthur Russell Jr. was born May 21, 1951, in Oskaloosa, Iowa, the son of a future mayor, and took up the cello as a boy; his mother Emily, a cellist herself, had started him on piano at six.3 Severe acne arrived in adolescence and never fully left the story — “Many people perceived Arthur as a mystery,” his partner Tom Lee wrote later, “by virtue of his demeanor, his acne-scarred face, his sometimes playful and often serious responses to others.”4 At sixteen he ran away; arrested in San Francisco around 1969 on a marijuana charge, he was released to the custody of the Kailas Shugendo commune, a Buddhist community under the teacher known as Ajari Warwick, rather than sent to juvenile detention, and there he practiced and played cello. He studied North Indian classical music at Ali Akbar Khan’s college and composition at the San Francisco Conservatory, and began accompanying Allen Ginsberg — the start of a lifelong alliance in which the poet supplied readings to score, a floor to sleep on when Russell reached New York, and, in the lean years, electricity through an extension cord run to his apartment.5

He arrived in New York in the autumn of 1973 to study at the Manhattan School of Music, clashed with the serialist Charles Wuorinen over a minimalist piece, and settled into the building where he would live the rest of his life: 437 East 12th Street, the East Village tenement called the Poets’ Building, with Ginsberg and Richard Hell among the neighbors.6

The Kitchen and the almost-band

At twenty-three Russell became music director of the Kitchen, downtown’s experimental headquarters, for the 1974–75 season. He kept the minimalists on the calendar and then bent it: in March 1975 he booked the Modern Lovers, Jonathan Richman’s proto-punk band, for a four-night “Rock and Roll Show.” “The Kitchen walls were going to fall apart,” the venue’s director Robert Stearns remembered thinking. “But frankly, I loved it.”7 Russell persuaded his successor to book an early Talking Heads, and in 1976 was in talks to join the band, then a trio; he declined, by his biographer’s account preferring a more explicitly sincere kind of pop with his own band, the Flying Hearts, and his own explanation kept the art-school cool at arm’s length: “They were all from art school and were into looking severe and cool. I was never into that. I was from music school and I had long hair at the time.”8 He recorded cello on an early acoustic take of “Psycho Killer” anyway, cut demos for the Columbia executive John Hammond, who rated him a possible successor to Bob Dylan, and recorded “Ballad of the Lights” with Ginsberg’s voice over the Flying Hearts.9

The floor

The dance floor claimed him at the Gallery and The Loft, the private parties where, as the critic Philip Sherburne writes, gay men like Russell “were forging their own community, their own rituals, their own methods of experiencing time.”10 Out of those rooms came the aliases. “Kiss Me Again”, cut in late 1977 and released as Dinosaur with Siano co-producing, put David Byrne’s rhythm guitar and the Fluxus violinist Henry Flynt into a disco band running dueling drummers and bassists; released on Sire, it sold more than 300,000 copies.11 In February 1979, “on the night of the full moon,” Russell and the DJ Steve D’Acquisto — who had met him at the Loft — tracked the songs that became Loose Joints, with the Ingram brothers, five Philadelphia siblings, as the rhythm section; the backup singers were recruited straight off the Loft’s floor, and David Mancuso came with them. “Is It All Over My Face?” (1980) struck the writer Lucy Sante as “the most amazingly explicit disco song I’d ever heard,” and it broke at the Paradise Garage in Larry Levan’s remix of the female-vocal take.12

The sessions that became 24→24 Music (1981, as Dinosaur L) ran on graphic scores and trust. “He’d put some music in front of me and I’d say, ‘Where do you want me to play from?’” the trombonist Peter Zummo recalled. “He’d say, ‘Just play.‘” Russell then recombined the tapes across two 24-track machines with the engineer Bob Blank, a process the album title names, and released the result on Sleeping Bag Records, the label he co-founded with Will Socolov.13 The album’s single became his dance-floor monument. François Kevorkian, hired to find the record inside tapes he called “an absolute, utter, and total mess” (“there were at least 20 songs that could have been put together from those tapes”), delivered the “Go Bang! #5” mix; the night Socolov carried the acetate to the Loft, Mancuso played it four times, lifting the needle at the end of the song and dropping it back to the start, while at the Garage Levan timed its organ climax to the confetti guns. Russell’s own review was in character: “François fucked up the EQ of the drums — we’re ruined,” he groused.14

The echo

World of Echo (1986), recorded over two years of solo sessions, is cello, voice, and echo and almost nothing else — the dance records’ skeletons played alone. It is the record his cult now rests on. Melody Maker heard “a fuzz, a blur, a rich, throbbing pulse, a signal in space”; Q heard “just another art bore.” Russell himself called it unfinished on principle: “World Of Echo isn’t a complete version of echo, it’s a sketch version of echo. I want to do the full version, which will have brass bands and orchestras playing outdoors in parks with those bandstands that project echoes.”15 Around it sat the rest of the sprawl: Tower of Meaning (1983), orchestral pieces conducted by Julius Eastman and released on the Chatham Square label, and the country songs he never stopped writing.16 He finished almost none of it. More than a thousand tapes survived him, forty of them different mixes of a single song; the bassist Ernie Brooks said he “never arrived at a completed version of anything,” and Zummo drew the character underneath: “He was funny, serious, driven, meticulous and never tame. He was reserved and had a sense of propriety that was sometimes almost Victorian.”17 Tom Lee paid the rent from a steady job and lay awake to the sound of work: “he would have his headphones on and there would be this constant plunk plunk tapping of the keyboards.”18

A sudden chill

Russell was diagnosed HIV-positive shortly after World of Echo appeared; one of the songs he wrote after the diagnosis is called “A Sudden Chill.” He died of AIDS-related illness on April 4, 1992, in New York, at forty — Ginsberg was the last, apart from Lee, to sit at his bedside.19 The obituary record is its own measure of the obscurity: the closest thing to a notice was a few paragraphs at the end of a Village Voice column chiefly about other composers, which observed that “his inability to finish his music to his satisfaction hindered dissemination of his work” and listed the entire catalog then in print — four records.20

The return

The audience arrived a decade late and has not stopped growing. Glass’s Point label assembled Another Thought in 1994; the true revival came in 2004, when Soul Jazz’s The World of Arthur Russell compiled the dance sides and Audika — the label Steve Knutson founded on an exclusive license from the estate — began releasing the archive Tom Lee had kept in his apartment for a decade, starting with Calling Out of Context.21 Matt Wolf’s documentary Wild Combination followed in 2008, “a tender, fascinating documentary,” the New York Times wrote, “that will delight the Russell cult and instantly convert new members,” and Tim Lawrence’s biography Hold On to Your Dreams (2009) gave the life its scholarly telling.22 The devotion turned practical: James Murphy and Tim Goldsworthy of DFA completed Russell’s unfinished “Springfield” from his masters in 2006, Kanye West sampled “Answers Me” on “30 Hours” in 2016, and the New York Public Library acquired the archive (some 1,500 reels, plus cassettes) the same year.23 Fact named World of Echo the best album of the 1980s; Pitchfork placed it twenty-fifth on its own eighties list. The man who could not finish became, in the archive’s hands, endlessly releasable. The one who had kept the tapes held on to a plainer version: “But to me,” Tom Lee wrote, “he was just Arthur, whom I loved.”24

Key records

  • Dinosaur, “Kiss Me Again” (1978) — the first alias: downtown art stars inside a working disco band, 300,000 copies on Sire
  • Loose Joints, “Is It All Over My Face?” (1980) — the full-moon session; Levan’s female-vocal mix became the Garage’s anthem
  • Dinosaur L, 24→24 Music (1981) — the recombinant disco album; “Go Bang! #5” is its ecstatic dispatch
  • Arthur Russell, Tower of Meaning (1983) — the orchestral wing: pieces meant for the theater, Eastman conducting
  • Arthur Russell, World of Echo (1986) — cello, voice, and echo; the solo record at the center of the posthumous canon
  • Arthur Russell, Calling Out of Context (2004) — the revival’s opening act: Audika’s first release from the tape archive

Legacy and influence

The career that confused every room it entered now reads as a map of where the rooms connected. The dance aliases run through Mutant disco and forward into house. The posthumous model itself — a devoted partner’s archive, a dedicated label, a biography, a film — became a template for how lost figures get found. Lawrence’s book makes the larger claim plainly. The boundary-crossing was itself the work; follow this one life, and the whole scene comes into view.
