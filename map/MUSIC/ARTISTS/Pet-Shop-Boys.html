Artist

Pet Shop Boys

Erac. 1981–present (primary period: 1984–1993)
MembersNeil Tennant (voice), Chris Lowe (keyboards)
Genres
SynthpopHi-NRGDance-pop
ListenRYM·Credits·Apple Music·Spotify

Introspective (1988) holds six songs, none under six minutes and two past nine. Pop convention kept the radio versions on the album and exiled the long mixes to club vinyl; the Pet Shop Boys ran the hierarchy backward, sequencing the full-length dance constructions as the album itself and carving the seven-inch edits out of them afterward. By Neil Tennant’s own account it outsold their other albums internationally, though he has also allowed that the songs’ twelve-inch scale may have put some fans off.1 The inversion only made official how the duo had always worked. A pop journalist and an architecture student who met in a Chelsea record shop, they apprenticed themselves to a New York Hi-NRG producer, and a Stephen Hague rebuild turned the song that apprenticeship produced into a number one on both sides of the Atlantic. At every altitude of fame that followed, the club record stayed their unit of thought. Their singles catalog became the meeting ground of dance music’s remix lineages, and the twelve-inch held the primary text of their music, with everything shorter an abridgment.

The dumper and the demos

Tennant and Chris Lowe met on August 19, 1981, at Chelsea Record Centre on King’s Road, where Tennant was buying a connector for a Korg MS-10 synthesizer, and discovered they both loved disco.2 What Tennant did for a living turned out to matter as much as what he bought. At Smash Hits, where he edited, the staff kept a box for unwanted promos they called “the dumper” — Tennant spun it into the pop-press verdict “down the dumper” — and he and Lowe rescued the Bobby O imports other writers threw away.3 Another coinage reported as his, “pervy synth duo,” was minted for the Eurythmics and has since been aimed at the Pet Shop Boys themselves. Records like The Flirts’ “Passion” (1982), produced by Bobby Orlando, shaped what the two were writing, so when a Smash Hits assignment put Tennant in New York in August 1983, he sought Orlando out and played him the demos the pair had cut in a small London studio.4

Orlando bit. Across late 1983 and early 1984 the trio recorded thirteen of the duo’s early songs in New York; only four were ever officially released.5 On the first “West End Girls,” taped at Unique Recording, Orlando played nearly everything himself — Lowe contributed “one chord and the bassline” — over a drum part lifted from “Billie Jean” and what Tennant called “Barry White chords,” chasing his stated aim “to make a rap record in a British accent.”6 Released in April 1984 on Orlando’s Bobcat imprint, it became a club hit in Los Angeles and San Francisco and little else. In March 1985, after long negotiations, the duo cut their ties with Orlando and signed with EMI under the manager Tom Watkins.7

Imperial

The Hague rebuild of “West End Girls,” cut at Advision in London in June 1985, kept the Orlando blueprint and refined the machine: the engineer counted “only four basic rhythmical patterns” in the whole construction, strings blended from two E-mu Emulators, and a trumpet solo Hague played on the Emulator’s factory horn sound, lifting five notes straight from Orlando’s original.8 Released that October, it entered the chart at eighty and took nine weeks to climb to number one, arriving there in January 1986; America followed that May. What came next was the run that would put a new term into pop criticism. Please (1986) established the duo; Actually (September 1987) perfected them, with Julian Mendelsohn producing and engineering half the record, including the second UK number one “It’s a Sin,” and Tennant reading the album loosely as a critique of Thatcherism.9 A synthpop “Always on My Mind,” recorded for an ITV special marking ten years since Elvis Presley’s death, held off “Fairytale of New York” for the 1987 Christmas number one and stayed on top four weeks; a remixed “Heart” made it four UK number ones in under three years.10

Then, in September 1988, driving through Sussex, Tennant heard the new single “Domino Dancing” — a Miami production by Exposé’s Lewis A. Martinée — come onto the chart below the peak they expected: “it entered the charts at number nine and I thought, ‘that’s that, then – it’s all over’. I knew then that our imperial phase of number one hits was over.”11 The coinage outgrew its coiner. A Blur biography borrowed “imperial phase” in 1999, crediting Tennant, and by the mid-2010s critics were using it for any artist’s can-do-no-wrong stretch without feeling the need to cite anyone.12 Tennant’s addendum is the sharper thought: “What’s interesting is what you do after.”

The twelve-inch as the text

Between those peaks the duo published their aesthetic in catalog form. Disco (November 1986) collected the Please-era twelve-inch mixes as an album, and the labels of the LP printed a BPM for every track — 122, 128, 122, 121, 111, 114 — DJ metadata stamped onto a mainstream pop release.13 The roster on that one record maps the era’s remix craft: Arthur Baker’s extended “In the Night,” Mendelsohn’s eight-minute “Suburbia (The Full Horror),” Ron Dean Miller and the Latin Rascals’ “Version Latina” of “Opportunities,” and Shep Pettibone twice, on “Love Comes Quickly” and the nine-minute “West End Girls” Mastermix first issued on the Parlophone twelve-inch of the hit.13

The commissions kept sorting themselves into lineages. Pettibone, the KISS-FM editor whose craft was radio surgery, delivered the Mastermix line; François Kevorkian, the dub-schooled club mixer, took “Rent” and returned an Extended Mix and a Dub on one 1987 twelve-inch, the New York tradition’s two halves working the same duo’s masters.14 Ian Levine, Heaven’s first resident and the producer who gave Hi-NRG its anthem, remixed “It’s a Sin” the same year.15 And on Introspective, produced in its grandest stretches by Trevor Horn and Stephen Lipson, Frankie Knuckles took additional production on “I Want a Dog” while the album closed on “It’s Alright,” a nine-minute reading of a Chicago house song — house arriving inside a British pop album while the genre was still club property.1 The scale ran to parody: “Left to My Own Devices” exists at 8:16 on the album, as a 4:43 edit, and as an eleven-and-a-half-minute Disco Mix on its own twelve-inch, orchestra included.16 One song, three sizes, and the album carries the long one.

Written for others

The same years produced a songwriting-and-production shop whose clients received the same twelve-inch treatment the duo gave itself. Dusty Springfield came first: Watkins suggested writing with Allee Willis, and the song they produced, “What Have I Done to Deserve This?”, sat through the Please sessions while the duo waited on Springfield’s answer to the duet invitation; it reached number two in both Britain and America and restarted her career.17 They kept writing for her. “Nothing Has Been Proved,” a song Tennant had drafted years before the duo existed, was rebuilt for Scandal, the Profumo affair film, with an Angelo Badalamenti orchestration, Courtney Pine’s soprano saxophone over the fade, and a Marshall Jefferson dance mix on the twelve-inch — a house pioneer remixing a torch song about 1963.18 The duo went on to produce half of her 1990 comeback album Reputation, and Pettibone gave “In Private” a twelve-inch of its own — remix, dub, and bonus beats across one record.19

For Eighth Wonder they wrote “I’m Not Scared” (1988), a number one in Italy whose twelve-inch “disco mix” welds the English vocal to its French-language adaptation and whose American pressing carried an early Louie Vega remix; they liked the song enough to reclaim it on Introspective with Tennant singing.20 The furthest reach was Liza Minnelli. She had heard “Rent” and loved it, and on Results (Epic, 1989) she cut daytime vocals against finished backing tracks, pushed to registers below her stage range, with Badalamenti’s orchestrations following the team from the Dusty sessions; “Losing My Mind” gave her a UK number six, and AllMusic’s verdict — “more of a Pet Shop Boys album than a Liza Minnelli disc” — named the method.21

What you do after

The answer to Tennant’s addendum was Behaviour (1990) and Very (1993), opposite responses to the same ending. For Behaviour the duo went to Munich because Harold Faltermeyer was a master of the analog synthesizers they missed — he started at five each morning arranging the Moogs and modulars before they arrived — and because, as Tennant put it, “sad songs are more interesting to write.”22 The saddest, “Being Boring,” reaches from teenage party invitations to the AIDS death, at thirty-four, of his childhood friend Christopher Dowell.23

Very, their only UK number one album, swung back to euphoria — “Can You Forgive Her?” opens it — and was read as their coming-out record; Tennant came out publicly the next year in Attitude.24 The cover that anchored the era began as a favor: Derek Jarman asked them to play a 1992 AIDS benefit at the Haçienda, Lowe picked the Village People’s “Go West,” and Tennant forgot the words on the night. Recorded properly, with the Pachelbel canon progression under the original pulled to the surface, it reached UK number two in 1993, a 1979 gay disco anthem re-sung as elegy and defiance at the epidemic’s height.25

The decades since have been steady rather than imperial: Horn returned for Fundamental (2006), Stuart Price produced a trilogy across the 2010s, and James Ford produced Nonetheless (2024), whose “A New Bohemia” took its cue from an exhibition on Les Petits Bon-Bons, the seventies queer art troupe that ran with Lou Reed and Sylvester. Tennant introduced the album with “I think this is our queer album” — Lowe’s reply: “Really?! I’m taking my copy back…”26 The 1999 Guinness Book of Records listed them as “the most successful duo in British music history,” a standing since carried past 100 million records sold.27 When the singles box Smash arrived in 2023, it collected 55 singles as “the 7-inch or CD version that was serviced to British radio,” a career of edits in one place while the primary texts stayed out on the albums and the old club vinyl.28 Asked in 2024 which era they are in, Tennant closed the question: “Pet Shop Boys are always in the era of now.”26
