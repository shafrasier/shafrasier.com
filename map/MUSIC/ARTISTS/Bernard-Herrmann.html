Artist

Bernard Herrmann

Era1941–1975
InstrumentComposition, conducting
Genres
Cinematic classicalModern classicalOrchestral musicFilm score
ListenRYM·Credits·Apple Music

Alfred Hitchcock wanted the shower scene in Psycho to play in silence. Bernard Herrmann scored it anyway — violins, violas, and cellos shrieking in downward stabs — and when Hitchcock heard the cue against the picture he reversed himself on the spot and nearly doubled the composer’s salary.1 Asked years later what he had meant the shrieking strings to convey, Herrmann answered with one word: “Terror.”2 The episode is Herrmann twice over. The sound comes from an orchestra deliberately starved of everything except its strings, and it exists because a composer overruled his director about the director’s own picture. Herrmann refused the title of film composer altogether, treating every score as a symphonic argument that happened to run under images.3 Across thirty-five years, from Orson Welles’s first film to Martin Scorsese’s breakthrough, he wrote some of the most psychologically exact orchestral music the cinema has, nearly all of it made by leaving things out.

What he came from

Born in 1911 to a Ukrainian-Jewish immigrant family — his optometrist father pushed the violin and took him to the opera — he won a composition prize at thirteen, and around the same age discovered Hector Berlioz’s Treatise on Instrumentation, the nineteenth-century manual that treats orchestral color as a subject in itself.4 Timbre-first thinking became the core of his craft. He studied under the composer-pianist Percy Grainger at NYU, moved on to Juilliard, and at twenty founded his own New Chamber Orchestra to play the repertoire nobody else would program.5 The decisive inheritance was Charles Ives. Herrmann found Ives’s privately printed scores as a student, and in 1932, at twenty-one, published an advocacy essay in the little magazine Trend declaring the Fourth Symphony, then unperformed in full, “one of the greatest symphonies ever penned,” about a composer whose name, as Herrmann noted in the same piece, was unknown even in musical circles.6 The friendship lasted until Ives’s death in 1954; Herrmann premiered Ives works over the CBS airwaves and dedicated his own cantata Moby Dick to him.7 What Ives supplied was permission: an American proof that dissonance and cussed independence could carry the deepest feeling. What the Romantics supplied was the feeling itself. “My feelings and yearnings are those of a composer of the 19th century,” Herrmann wrote his wife in 1947. “I am completely out of step with the present.”8 Out of step, and yet armed with Ives’s permission: a nineteenth-century heart looking for a medium that would take both.

The radio workshop

The medium turned out to be broadcasting. Herrmann joined CBS in 1934 as a staff arranger and conductor, was running the experimental Columbia Workshop drama series within two years of its 1936 launch, and by 1940 was chief conductor of the CBS Symphony Orchestra, where his programming of rarities and unheard living composers earned a Peabody citation “for bringing to the radio compositions and composers who deserve but might not otherwise have received a hearing.”9 The other half of the job built his method. By the composer David Raksin’s count Herrmann scored some 1,200 radio dramas: seconds-long cues for odd pickup ensembles, written fast, timed to dialogue.10 That workshop is where he forged the anti-leitmotif credo he preached for the rest of his life: “I don’t like the leitmotif system. The short phrase is easier to follow for an audience, who listen with only half an ear… a tune has to have eight or sixteen bars, which limits you as a composer. Once you start, you’ve got to finish… It’s putting handcuffs on yourself.”11 The refusal — aimed at the leitmotif system the Hollywood score was built on — began as radio pragmatism and hardened into doctrine.

At CBS he fell in with Orson Welles’s Mercury Theatre, and on October 30, 1938, he was in the studio for the War of the Worlds broadcast — conducting no music of his own. The panic’s engine was a fake dance-remote: “From the Meridian Room in the Park Plaza in New York City, we bring you the music of Ramón Raquello and his orchestra.” Raquello’s orchestra was Herrmann’s CBS players, gamely swinging “La Cumparsita” between ever-worsening bulletins from Grovers Mill.12 When Welles went to Hollywood, he took his conductor with him.

Citizen Kane

Welles gave Herrmann conditions no Hollywood composer had: twelve weeks, where the Hollywood norm was a frantic few, working while the film was still being made. “I worked on the film reel by reel, as it was being shot and cut,” Herrmann told the New York Times in 1941. “Many sequences were actually tailored to match the music.”13 The scoring itself announced a new palette — “I avoided, as much as possible, the realistic sound of a large symphony orchestra,” he wrote; “a simple bass flute solo, the pulsing of a bass drum, or the sound of muted horns, can often be more effective than half a hundred musicians sawing away.”14 His wit was as exact as his color sense. For Susan Alexander’s disastrous opera debut he composed a genuine pseudo-French grand-opera aria and pitched it to climb to a high D far beyond the character’s modest voice, so the audience would hear the quicksand swallowing her; soprano Jean Forward dubbed the singing, engineered to sound strained rather than incompetent.15 And the “News on the March” newsreel carries no Herrmann at all — it was cut with stock RKO library cues at Welles’s preference, because a real newsreel would sound canned.16 Welles later put the score at half the film’s success.17 At the Academy Awards the following winter Herrmann was nominated twice in the scoring category and won — for the other film, The Devil and Daniel Webster (released as All That Money Can Buy), whose devil he conjured from four superimposed violin tracks and the recorded hum of telephone wires at four in the morning. It beat his own Kane, and it was the only Oscar he ever won.18

The Hitchcock decade

The defining partnership ran 1955 to 1964: seven scores, from The Trouble with Harry through Marnie, plus the electronic bird-shrieks he supervised as “sound consultant” on The Birds. Herrmann’s own account of the division of labor was characteristically modest: “Hitchcock only finishes a picture 60 per cent. I have to finish it for him.”19 He even appears on screen once, conducting the Storm Clouds cantata at the Royal Albert Hall in The Man Who Knew Too Much — a piece he pointedly did not replace, judging that no one could better what Arthur Benjamin had written for the 1934 original; he re-orchestrated it instead.20

Vertigo (1958) is the masterwork. An American musicians’ strike kept Herrmann off the podium (the conductor Muir Mathieson recorded the score in London and Vienna, to Herrmann’s lasting irritation), but the music itself circles and rises without ever settling.21 Its love music, the “Scène d’amour”, takes the harmonic yearning of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde and refuses it any arrival; as Alex Ross writes, the citations are audible while “the main melodic contour is his own; the harmony is still his idiosyncratic construction.”22 Against it Herrmann sets Carlotta’s habanera, a dance rhythm fixed obsessively on a single repeating D: memory as a needle stuck in a groove.23 For North by Northwest (1959) the studio wanted something Gershwinesque for the Manhattan bustle; Herrmann opened instead with a driving fandango overture, his musical reading of what he called “a crazy dance between Cary Grant and the world.”24 And Psycho (1960) pushed his lifelong reduction to its limit. Hitchcock reportedly wanted jazz; Herrmann turned the film’s low music budget into an aesthetic and scored for strings alone, what he called in biographer Steven C. Smith’s account a “black and white sound” to match the photography.25 The only electronics in the murder cue were microphones placed close to the instruments to catch the harshest possible bowing.26

The break

By the mid-sixties the industry had rebuilt itself around the soundtrack single, and the pressure — the same machine The song in film maps — reached even Hitchcock. On November 4, 1965, he wired Herrmann that the old approach was “out of step with the times and old fashioned” and demanded a score with a beat for Torn Curtain; Herrmann protested that Hitchcock “was not the kind of film maker who made pictures for children,” then cabled that he would be delighted to write a vigorous score.27 What he delivered was the opposite of a pop theme: an orchestra of twelve flutes, sixteen French horns, nine trombones, two tubas, timpani, eight cellos, and eight basses, and no violins at all. At the Goldwyn soundstage session in March 1966 the studio musicians burst into applause after the main title. Hitchcock arrived unannounced, pronounced the music exactly what he did not want, and dismissed orchestra and composer mid-session. In the phone call that followed, Hitchcock accused Herrmann of stabbing him in the back; Herrmann shouted that Hitchcock had abandoned his integrity and sold out. Years later, when Herrmann came by with his new wife to introduce her, Hitchcock refused to see them.28

The concert composer he insisted he was

“As a composer I might class myself as a Neo-Romantic, inasmuch as I have always regarded music as a highly personal and emotional form of expression,” his printed credo begins.3 He backed it with a catalog: Moby Dick, premiered by John Barbirolli and the New York Philharmonic in April 1940, then a Symphony played by the same orchestra in 1942. The work he regarded as his central achievement was the opera Wuthering Heights, composed 1943–1951 to a libretto by his first wife, the suspense writer Lucille Fletcher. No company staged it in his lifetime. He recorded it complete in 1966 at his own expense — film money paying the concert composer’s debts — and Portland Opera finally staged it in 1982, seven years after his death.29

His working terms were absolute — “I have the final say, or I don’t do the music,” he told an audience in 1973 — and he orchestrated every note himself: “To orchestrate is like a thumbprint… It would be like someone putting color to your paintings.”30 Yet the man who considered himself a nineteenth-century symphonist also insisted that the motion picture was “the first truly original art form of the twentieth century” and that “any composer who disdains writing music for films, radio or television, or any other medium, is doomed to oblivion.”31

The disciples

Exile made him a legend. Relocated to London after the Hitchcock break, conducting LP re-recordings of his own catalog and scoring British pictures — among them Twisted Nerve (1968), with its softly whistled theme — Herrmann was rediscovered by directors young enough to have grown up on him.32 François Truffaut, asked why he wanted Herrmann and none of his avant-garde friends, answered: “They’ll give me music of the twentieth century, but you’ll give me music of the twenty-first.” For Fahrenheit 451 Herrmann repaid the compliment by refusing electronics entirely: strings, harps, and tuned percussion for the future, reasoning that “the music of the next century would revert to a great lyrical simplicity.”33

Brian De Palma’s editor Paul Hirsch, temp-tracking Sisters with Psycho cues, talked the director into summoning the man himself; Herrmann came from London and scored the film with a Moog synthesizer carrying its main themes, then wrote Obsession, his Vertigo-haunted music for De Palma’s Vertigo-haunted film.34 Scorsese’s Taxi Driver he initially waved off — “I don’t know anything about cabdrivers” — until the scene where Travis Bickle pours peach Schnapps over torn-up white bread intrigued him; he took the job on his usual terms of total control.35 The score sets a mournful alto-saxophone main title (played in the film by Ronny Lang; Tom Scott plays it on the album’s re-recorded arrangements) against harps and muted brass that curdle as Travis does, and its final gesture was Herrmann’s last compositional decision anywhere: told a closing sting was too obvious, he said “Play it backwards” and left the room.36 He finished recording on December 23, 1975, and died in his sleep in Los Angeles hours later, at sixty-four. The film went out dedicated to him, its closing card offering “our gratitude and respect.” The Taxi Driver score won him the BAFTA. Fifteen months later, at the March 1977 Academy Awards, came two posthumous nominations, for Taxi Driver and Obsession, his first since the forties; both lost to Jerry Goldsmith’s The Omen.37

The afterlife

Smith’s biography A Heart at Fire’s Center (still the standard life) calls the shower cue “probably the most famous (and most imitated) cue in film music,” and the imitation is the point: his short repeating cells and pared-down ensembles are simply how screen suspense is scored now.38 The inheritors say so themselves. Danny Elfman: “…I became a film music fan because of The Day the Earth Stood Still. It was the first time I noticed a composer’s name: Bernard Herrmann” — and it was Elfman who adapted Herrmann’s own Psycho score for the 1998 remake.39 John Williams has named the Hitchcock–Herrmann films among his earliest strong impressions.40 It was Elmer Bernstein who gave the rejected Torn Curtain score its first recording, in his mail-order Film Music Collection, and adapted Herrmann’s 1962 Cape Fear music for Scorsese’s 1991 remake.41

The afterlife long ago escaped the concert hall. It reached pop within Herrmann’s lifetime: for “Eleanor Rigby”, George Martin scored a string octet in biting close-miked staccato drawn from the Psycho score, planting the Herrmann sound in the middle of Revolver.42 Quentin Tarantino resurrected the Twisted Nerve whistle for Kill Bill Vol. 1, Daryl Hannah’s Elle Driver whistling it down a hospital corridor, and Busta Rhymes’s “Gimme Some More” (1998) runs on the Psycho prelude’s stabbing violin figure — the concert-hall refusenik become breakbeat, via Sampling.43 Alex Ross’s verdict, “the greatest of all Hollywood film composers,” is the consensus now.44 It rests on a career built almost entirely of refusals: of the leitmotif, of the tune, of the Gershwinesque, of the pop single, of anyone else’s orchestration. Every refusal named a convention the movies didn’t actually need, and what was left after the subtractions is still what dread sounds like.
