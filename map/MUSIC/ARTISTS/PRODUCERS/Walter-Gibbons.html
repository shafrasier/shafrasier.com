Producer

Walter Gibbons

Erac. 1975–1994
RoleRemixer, DJ
Genres

In May 1977, reviewing a twelve-inch for Billboard, Tom Moulton reached the edge of his own categories. “Many of the breaks on this record are unpredictable, and convey the impression that the mixing deejay was working with a full floor of dancers and was going out of his way to ‘do a number’ on the audience,” he wrote of Loleatta Holloway’s “Hit and Run.” “This version is really so different from the original that it must be classified as a new record.”1 The mixing deejay was Walter Gibbons, and the review named the thing he had just invented: the remix as a work of its own. Tim Lawrence’s summary runs, “in the second half of the seventies and the first half of the eighties there were only three DJ remixers who really mattered: Gibbons, Kevorkian and Levan. Gibbons was the first.”2 Where François Kevorkian worked the console like an engineer, Gibbons worked it like a mystic — a DJ who heard records as raw percussion and, eventually, as scripture.

Two copies of everything

Gibbons was born in Brooklyn on April 2, 1954, and came up through the city’s record economy: a job at a record shop from the spring of 1973, then DJ dates at a gay bar in Jackson Heights by the end of that year. With his partner Rich Flores he pressed his own edits as acetates — two-sided seven-inches, twenty of them, on a private label they called Melting Pot.3 Working two copies of a record, he could seize a thirty-second drum break and hold it as long as the floor could stand.

Galaxy 21

Galaxy 21 was an after-hours club in a five-story Chelsea brownstone at 256 West 23rd Street, owned by George Freeman, with sound by Alex Rosner. It opened at four-forty in the morning, when nearly every other discotheque in New York was closing, and Gibbons ruled it through 1975 and 1976.4 Other DJs finished their shifts and came to study him. “Walter was so innovative,” remembered Kenny Carpenter, who ran the lights. “He would buy two copies of a record like ‘Happy Song’ and he would loop the thirty-second conga section.”5 “Everyone was going to hear Walter,” the DJ Tony Smith said. “Most DJs finished at four so we could hear Walter from five until ten.” Kevorkian, then a French drummer newly arrived in New York — the club’s owner had hired him to play along with the records — put it flatly: “But there were just DJs, and then there was Walter.”6 Lawrence pairs him with Herc: “Paralleling Herc, Gibbons started to mix between breaks when he DJed at Galaxy 21,” a turntablist working disco in the same years the Bronx DJs were working funk, the two scenes inventing break-work in parallel.7

Freeman, tired of losing volume arguments, had Rosner install a secret control in his office; Rosner remembered: “There was a hidden volume control in his office. When Walter found out he quit.” Freeman relented within about a week, the control came out, and Gibbons came back. “You can’t take the gas pedal away from the driver,” Rosner concluded.8 The second betrayal ended the residency. In late 1976 Gibbons discovered his reel-to-reel edits “were being lifted from his booth and taken to Sunshine Sound, where they were being pressed up and sold on the black market” — a wire ran from the DJ booth to the office — and he left for good. The club went dark within months.9

The Salsoul run

The studio came to the booth. Ken Cayre, the Salsoul co-owner, was brought to Galaxy 21 by the label’s promotions staffer Denise Chatman and watched Gibbons work two promotional copies of Double Exposure’s “Ten Percent.” “He did this fantastic edit and the reaction in the club was phenomenal,” Cayre remembered. “I said, ‘Can you do that in the studio?’ He said he could.”10 The session, engineered by Bob Blank, ran three hours. Gibbons stretched the album cut from six minutes fifty to nearly ten, and was paid $185 in total. The result — released in May 1976, the first commercially sold twelve-inch single, a story Disco tells — outsold the seven-inch two to one.11 “The session was very intuitive,” Blank said. “Walter was a real genius.”12

“Hit and Run” made Gibbons the first DJ handed the multitrack tapes themselves; he stripped the string section and most of the horns, then cut Holloway’s opening verses and stretched her closing vamp from three minutes to five, over the objections of the record’s producer Norman Harris — until some 300,000 in sales settled the argument.13 In 1978 he built Love Committee’s “Law and Order” around an insistent bongo-driven percussion track and mixed Bettye LaVette’s “Doin’ the Best That I Can” for West End. In March 1979 Salsoul collected the strangest of this work on Disco Madness — by Cayre’s account the first album of mixes a label ever built around a single remixer — including the stripped, dubbed mix of First Choice’s “Let No Man Put Asunder” that Frankie Knuckles would ride at the Warehouse.14 Moulton, asked about the album, kept his distance: “If you want to know anything about that album, ask Walter.”15

The conversion

The faith turned on his own material. Gibbons had been drawn to Bible study since the mid-seventies, and by 1978 the devotion had hardened into refusal.16 Handed Instant Funk’s “I Got My Mind Made Up,” he built most of a mix, balked at the lyrics, and said he would finish only if new vocals were cut; Cayre gave the record to Larry Levan, whose version became the hit, and Gibbons refused the credit for the foundation he had laid. Chatman pleaded with him: “You’ve created the foundation for it. Just stick with it.”17 The refusals multiplied. He blotted out profanities on his records with a marker pen and drew hearts on songs containing the word “love”; he pulled records whose titles he judged ungodly from his sets, Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes’ “Bad Luck” among them.18 Tony Smith got him a booth at the midtown club Xenon and watched it collapse in three weeks: “the owners complained because he only played gospel and Salsoul.” A radio spot ended the same way — “all of the sudden I heard Walter’s voice reading scriptures over the records,” a listener remembered. “They took it right off the air.”19 The industry moved on to remixers who would play what they were given. “Larry became hot,” Blank said, “and Walter didn’t have a base.”20

The Russell wing

Arthur Russell found him anyway. “Walter could discuss the different textures of music for days,” said Will Socolov, co-founder of Sleeping Bag Records. “The only other person who discussed sound in that kind of detail was Arthur.”21 Gibbons had already cut a mix of Dinosaur L’s “Go Bang!” that stayed on a shelf for more than twenty years — the version story lives in “Go Bang!“‘s crate — and in 1986 he and Russell released “School Bell / Treehouse” on Sleeping Bag under the name Indian Ocean.22 The guitarist Gary Lucas watched them make “Let’s Go Swimming” that year: “Arthur was shrieking and tearing his hair out, raging around the studio like a psychotic bat, while Walter was calmly snipping and pasting the tape as if it was macramé.” Russell would shout, “You’re ruining my fucking vision!”; Gibbons would answer, “Arthur, Arthur, calm down!” Lucas’s line on the finished mix: “He took the song out to the stratosphere.”23

The last decade

By then Gibbons was working the counter at Rock and Soul on Seventh Avenue, selling records and sermons in equal measure, and assembling a collection of some five thousand gospel records.24 There was one more flare. His 1984 mix of Strafe’s “Set It Off” cleared floors before it filled them — “I played ‘Set It Off’ for ten minutes and it cleared the floor,” Smith remembered — and then the early New York garage rooms adopted it as an anthem.25 His health failed through the late eighties; losing his sight, he developed a notch system that let him recognize his records by touch, and depended on the shop’s owner, who recalled, “I used to send him $200 every two weeks.”26 In September 1992 a Tokyo booking put him in the same city as Kevorkian and Levan, two months before Levan’s death; Kevorkian thought the reunion left him “very, very deeply touched… because he was able to find there a reaffirmation of everything he believed in.”27 Gibbons died of AIDS-related complications on September 23, 1994, at forty, his final weeks spent alone in a YMCA. The memorial on October 11 was far quieter than the one held for Levan two years earlier, and Billboard ran a brief obituary at the bottom of its dance column.28 After his death, Kevorkian went to the apartment: “the executors of his estate were going to trash everything and throw it in the garbage,” he remembered. “I looked through and what I thought was unique and needed to be preserved I put into this big bag and the bag’s been closed ever since. It’s not my property.”29

Influence and legacy

The rediscovery came in waves: the Mixed With Love anthology of his Salsoul work in 2004 and Lawrence’s scholarly account four years later, then Jungle Music in 2010, the career retrospective that collected the shelved “Go Bang!” mix, first released the year before.30 Lawrence’s essay carries the complaint the reissues answer: Gibbons rarely features alongside the canonical seventies spinners, though the DJs themselves never forgot — Nicky Siano’s verdict on the “Hit and Run” mix is simply, “It’s perfection.”31 Kevorkian ranked him differently: of the three remixers who mattered, Gibbons was the most abstract and the least concerned with whether anyone danced — the lonely genius to Levan’s hitmaker.32
