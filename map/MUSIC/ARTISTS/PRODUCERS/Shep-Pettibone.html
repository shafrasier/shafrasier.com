Producer

Shep Pettibone

Era1980–c. 1996
RoleRemixer, producer, DJ
Genres

The best-selling single of 1990 was cut for five thousand dollars in somebody’s basement on West 56th Street, in a vocal booth that had been a closet — bi-fold doors off, sliding glass door on — through a secondhand 24-track with only a few working cards.1 Craig Kostich, Warner’s head of dance music, had asked Shep Pettibone “to come up with a track for her” as a possible B-side for Madonna’s “Keep It Together.” Pettibone sent a groove built from the Philadelphia-Salsoul feel he had loved since his radio days; within two weeks she flew in, announced “I hope you don’t mind, but I’m going to call it ‘Vogue,’” and sang it in first takes.2 When the label heard the finished mix, “the attitude was like, ‘This isn’t gonna be a b-side,’” and the throwaway went out through the Dick Tracy album and became her eighth No. 1.3 “Vogue” finished a ten-year climb that had begun with a razor blade and a reel of radio tape: the editor who built hits for the airwaves ended the decade as co-writer and co-producer of one.

Sixteen hours of radio

Pettibone’s first mastermix was a rework of MFSB’s “Love Is the Message,” made at WKTU in 1980, and when the new dance station WRKS — 98.7 KISS-FM — launched the following year, he became its signature editor: “legendary for his Mastermixes, exclusive re-edits of hit tracks that used clever tape splicing, delay effects, and TR-909 drum fills.”4 The job was industrial. He “had to fill 16 hours of weekly music programming with new mixes,” and recruited a young Tony Humphries to help; across town, editors like Tony Moran of the Latin Rascals were running the same race, cutting reel-to-reel remixes and dropping them straight on air.5 This was a second remix lineage, running beside the club line of the Garage booths: radio surgery, built to seize a listener in seconds. Prelude, the label whose records he was mixing in-house, put the radio work on sale: 98.7 Kiss FM Presents Shep Pettibone’s Mastermixes in 1982, a second volume in 1983 with Humphries, and a Cleveland-branded WZAK variant of the franchise.6 The same stretch put him next to Arthur Baker on Afrika Bambaataa and the Jazzy 5’s “Jazzy Sensation,” the start of a long association.7

The Salsoul chair

The radio editor then took the seat three club legends had held. At Salsoul Records Pettibone would, in the DJ-historian Greg Wilson’s words, “follow in the footsteps of a trio of fabled figures, Tom Moulton, Walter Gibbons and Larry Levan, as Salsoul’s favoured remix exponent.”8 The chair’s defining record is First Choice’s “Let No Man Put Asunder”: Walter Gibbons had cut a spectral 1979 treatment that withheld the bass until the final 45 seconds. The 1983 twelve-inch paired a Pettibone side with a Frankie Knuckles flip, his mix spare and rhythm-forward — stop-start drums, dubbed-out percussion, newly recorded vocal passages. From there the record traveled into house’s bloodstream by way of the “Jack Your Body” sample.9 His other Salsoul reworks included Instant Funk and Skyy, and the Salsoul Orchestra’s “Ooh, I Love It (Love Break)” — a 1983 remix that would come back into his life through a courtroom.10

Mastermix Productions

Through the late eighties his sleeves rarely said “Shep Pettibone.” They said “Additional Production and Remix by Shep Pettibone for Mastermix Productions,” the company named for the radio slot, usually with “Edited by Shep Pettibone and Junior Vasquez” beneath it; Vasquez, the future Sound Factory headliner, was his cutting-room partner for the decade.11 Under that credit the client list became a cross-section of the pop apparatus: the Pet Shop Boys’ “West End Girls (The Shep Pettibone Mastermix)” and “What Have I Done to Deserve This?” with Dusty Springfield, New Order’s “Bizarre Love Triangle” and “True Faith,” Depeche Mode’s “Behind the Wheel,” and George Michael’s nine-minute “Hard Day.” Janet Jackson’s “Miss You Much” and “The Pleasure Principle” ran through the same shop, and Dusty’s “In Private” twelve-inch carried a complete DJ toolkit — vocal remix, dub, bonus beats — on one record. Prince’s “Strange Relationship,” a 1987 club mix, stayed in the vault until an official release in 2020.12 Arthur Baker, introducing the 2021 box of this work, named what set it apart: “What Shep Pettibone had that most other mixers did not, was a true radio sensibility. He inherently knew what would work on the airwaves, but was equally comfortable in a club setting, as a DJ and a fan, so he knew what worked both on the dance floor and on radio. That was a deadly combination.”13

The Madonna decade

The partnership that defined him began before her records did, a friendship from his WBLS and KISS-FM days.14 On True Blue in 1986 he was one hired hand among several, trusted with “True Blue (The Color Mix)” while other singles went to other specialists.15 By You Can Dance in 1987 — his “Into the Groove” and “Where’s the Party” reworks on the album, his mixes running on her tour — he owned the job: “I was kind of, I guess at that point, Madonna’s favorite remixer, so I was remixing all of her songs.”16 The 1989 singles made the method explicit. His “Express Yourself” “was a total reproduction and I totally rewrote the music”; Warner synced the video to his version, leaving the carousel horn players miming parts he had deleted, and his “Like a Prayer” suite became the versions she chose to lead with.17 He claimed the method’s paternity outright: before him, he said, a remix meant some percussion added onto a track; he was re-recording all the music.18 After “Vogue” the relationship crossed fully into writing: “Rescue Me,” the QSound remixing of The Immaculate Collection, a second No. 1 in “This Used to Be My Playground,” and the bulk of Erotica (1992), co-written and co-produced from his instrumentals with Tony Shimkin programming. “Deeper and Deeper” quotes “Vogue” because “we got to that point and we’re like, ‘What the hell. Let’s have fun with it.‘”19 The run ended quietly with “Bad Girl” and “Rain” in 1993. Sessions for the next album started and stalled: “We wrote some songs for what became Bedtime Stories. But, halfway through, she changed her direction and wanted to go more hip-hop. … She’s always moving on.”20

The hotelier

He left at the top. “It was like mid-’90s,” he said of the exit; everything had gone hip-hop, and he was not a hip-hop person: “I didn’t have it in me to write it or produce it… I’d been doing it for 15 years.”21 In 1998 he bought the Empress Hotel in Asbury Park, a 1961 building abandoned for years in the next town over from his Ocean Grove birthplace, and in May 1999 opened the Paradise nightclub inside it: “I put together a club with the idea that if you build it with a great sound system — they will come. And that’s what I did. And they did.”22 When the town later threatened to seize the property unless the hotel rooms were finished within six months, he “went to Amazon.com and got every book there was on hotel management” and delivered a hundred rooms — the self-taught hotelier who had been “kind of the running joke in town.”23 Paradise became an engine of Asbury Park’s revival as a gay resort town, and he still plays it a few nights a year, holidays and tea dances by the pool.24 The courts eventually paid him back for the music too. The claim that “Vogue” sampled a 0.23-second horn from his own “Love Break” remix was ruled trivial, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed; his own verdict was “frivolous. Just lies.”25 In 2019 an appeals court ordered Warner to release the “Vogue” royalties it had withheld, calling the contract “tortured, garbled and ambiguous.”26 In 2015 an interviewer asked whether he would ever cut another remix. “I don’t think so, but you never know what can happen.”27

Key mixes

  • “Let No Man Put Asunder (Shep Pettibone 12” Mix)” (1983, First Choice) — the mix Chicago later sampled into “Jack Your Body”
  • “West End Girls (The Shep Pettibone Mastermix)” (1986, Pet Shop Boys) — the 8:37 rework collected on Disco
  • “Behind the Wheel (Shep Pettibone Mix)” (1987, Depeche Mode) — from the Music for the Masses twelve-inch campaign
  • “Express Yourself (Non-Stop Express Mix)” (1989, Madonna) — rebuilt around her voice, both versions mixed inside fifteen hours
  • “Like a Prayer (12” Dance Mix)” (1989, Madonna) — five versions on one twelve-inch, Churchapella included
  • “Vogue” (1990, Madonna) — five thousand dollars and two weeks, closet booth to No. 1
  • “Fever (Hot Sweat 12” Mix)” (1993, Madonna) — the far end: remixing a record he co-produced himself
