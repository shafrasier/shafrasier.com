Frankie Knuckles

Era1972–2014
RoleDJ, producer, remixer

Frankie Knuckles came to production through scarcity. From around 1980, with new dance records drying up, the Warehouse’s resident began rebuilding the ones he had — “different records like ‘Walk The Night’ by the Skatt Bros. or stuff like ‘A Little Bit Of Jazz’ by Nick Straker,” as he inventoried the practice — re-cutting them “to make them work better for my dancefloor,” all of it on reel-to-reel tape.1 Four years later a drum machine was pounding live beneath the records at his own club, and the teenagers on his old floor were pressing what they heard onto vinyl.2 The rest of the career ran on that discovery. From New York, after 1987, he remixed house’s own singers and Michael Jackson from the same desk; in 1998 the Recording Academy gave him the first Grammy it ever awarded a remixer. When he died in 2014, Time Out New York’s obituary headline used the title the culture had settled on long before: “The Godfather of House.”3

Balloons, then a bathhouse

Francis Warren Nicholls Jr. was born in the Bronx on January 18, 1955, and came up studying textile design at the Fashion Institute of Technology while working the earliest downtown party circuit with his friend Larry Levan — two teenagers blowing up balloons at Nicky Siano’s Gallery, the club built on the Loft’s template.4 In July 1972 he started spinning at the Continental Baths, the gay bathhouse complex whose amenities he could still inventory decades later: an Olympic-size pool, a sauna, boutiques, restaurants, private apartments. Levan held the prime nights. “I was scheduled to play Mondays and Tuesdays, and Larry played from Wednesday to Sunday,” Knuckles said, “and on the nights he played I found myself playing at the beginning of the evening or playing before he woke up… If he woke up.”5 The Baths went bankrupt in 1976, and the two apprentices went looking for rooms of their own — Levan toward what became the Paradise Garage, Knuckles toward an offer from the Midwest.6

Five years, renewable

Chicago asked for Levan first. “Originally they wanted Larry in Chicago, but Larry didn’t want to leave New York,” Knuckles remembered of the Warehouse job; when it passed to him it came with the thing he had wanted since the Baths folded: “They offered me a piece of the business.” He moved in 1977 with an exit clause of his own devising — “I gave myself five years, and if I couldn’t make it in five years then I could always come back home.”7 What he found on South Jefferson Street held him past the deadline. The crowd was “predominantly black, predominantly gay, age probably between 18 and maybe 35. Very soulful and very spiritual,” a quality he found remarkable in “corn-fed” Midwesterners, and the schedule concentrated everything into one weekly rite: “For most of the people that went there, it was church for them. It only happened one day a week: Saturday night, Sunday morning, Sunday afternoon.”8 By the early eighties Chicago’s record stores were selling the sound under a clipped form of his club’s name.9 The room itself changed under him. Between 1982 and 1983, he said, “there was a lot more hard-edged straight kids that were trying to infiltrate what was going on there, and for the most part they didn’t have any respect for what was going on” — and in the spring of 1983 he walked away.10

The Power Plant and the towel

That fall he opened the Power Plant, the club he finally owned outright. Derrick May called from Detroit in 1984 to say he was driving down with two drum machines and “wanted to sell me one”; the merchandise arrived wrapped in a towel, with a pitch Knuckles never forgot: “This is a Roland 909 drum machine, and it’s going to take us to the future. It’ll be the foundation of music for the next 10 years.”11 In Detroit the sale registered as treason; Juan Atkins raged that May had given away the sound.12 Knuckles put the machine straight to work: “The first time I used it, I used it on a version of ‘Your Love’ that I did with Jamie Principle. And I would use it live in the club.”13 Jamie Principle, a bedroom songwriter a mutual friend had introduced in 1982, gave the partnership its songs: “Your Love” ran in Knuckles’s sets from tape for about a year before its first pressing under Principle’s own name in 1986; in 1987 Trax paired it with “Baby Wants to Ride” on one twelve-inch.14 He closed the Power Plant in September 1986, and a successor venture called the Power House lasted into 1987. Four months followed at Delirium!, Heaven’s Thursday-night party in London — and then he went home to New York.15

Def Mix

The city he returned to in 1987 had just built him an office. Judy Weinstein, who had been routing promos to New York’s DJs through her For the Record pool since 1978, founded Def Mix Productions that year with David Morales, and Knuckles joined soon after.16 The client list ran from pop’s upper register — Michael Jackson, Luther Vandross, Diana Ross — to the vocal house sides of the era: Lisa Stansfield’s “Change,” Electribe 101’s “Talking With Myself,” and the mix he and Morales built of Alison Limerick’s “Where Love Lives.”17 The Pet Shop Boys handed him “I Want a Dog” for Introspective in 1988, an early case of British pop hiring house at the source.18

The signature record of the period began as a cassette from Tokyo. On his first Japan tour, Knuckles met a twenty-year-old keyboardist named Satoshi Tomiie, who was organizing the Tokyo parties Knuckles played and writing their theme music; invited to New York, Tomiie brought demos including a piano figure he had named for “the way the one piano line reminded me of tears rolling down.” Weinstein heard the tape at the office and insisted there was “a song hidden in those tracks.”19 The vocal came from Chicago: Robert Owens, the voice of Fingers Inc., was performing in town when word reached him that “Frankie Knuckles is looking for you,” and he wrote the lyric in about fifteen minutes, imagining “how I would feel if I really love someone and it wasn’t reciprocated.”20 “Tears” (1989) credits all three. Tomiie defined Knuckles’s role in the room with one comparison — “he had a Quincy Jones type of role” — and Weinstein defined the record’s: “one of the songs that helped make Def Mix.”21

His own debut album, Beyond the Mix (1991), led with “The Whistle Song,” the first of his four No. 1s on the US dance chart.22 The album had sold about 8,000 copies by 2004, and the gap between renown and retail is the career in miniature: his economy was the twelve-inch and the booth. He spent 1990 to 1991 playing the Sound Factory while Junior Vasquez was away amid an ownership dispute, and ranked it “the last great room like that, of that caliber.”22 In February 1998 the Recording Academy presented its first Remixer of the Year award and gave it to Knuckles, an honor judged on a body of work with no single record named. Morales took the second a year later; the category’s first two winners shared one office.23

The letter

Chicago repaid the transplant formally. On August 25, 2004, the stretch of Jefferson Street by the old Warehouse became Frankie Knuckles Way, the designation pushed through by a state senator named Barack Obama, and the date declared Frankie Knuckles Day.24 The last decade was harder. Type II diabetes set in during the mid-2000s; after breaking his foot snowboarding he kept his touring schedule rather than treat the bone infection that followed, and eventually lost the foot. He kept releasing — a final album, A New Reality, in 2004, and late reworkings of his own classics with Eric Kupper under the alias Director’s Cut — and kept playing. He died in Chicago on March 31, 2014, at fifty-nine, of complications from the disease.25

Three weeks later, Morales posted to Facebook a letter the family had received from the President and First Lady: “Frankie’s work helped open minds and bring people together, blending genres to capture our attention and ignite our imaginations.” The Obamas called him a trailblazer.26 The crates got a public home the next year: his working record collection now lines a wall of Theaster Gates’s Stony Island Arts Bank in Grand Crossing, open to visitors since October 2015. The career that began beside a bathhouse pool ended as a public archive.27

Key records

  • “Your Love” (1986, Jamie Principle) — the tape that ran in his sets for a year, and the record where the 909 first went to work
  • “Baby Wants to Ride” (1987, Jamie Principle) — the partnership’s second Trax side, paired with “Your Love” on one twelve-inch
  • “I Want a Dog” (1988, Pet Shop Boys) — the Introspective rework that carried the Chicago sound into British pop
  • “Tears” (1989, with Satoshi Tomiie and Robert Owens) — the Def Mix cornerstone, written across Tokyo, New York, and Chicago
  • “Where Love Lives” (1990, Alison Limerick) — the club mix he built with David Morales, revived for a UK Christmas ad campaign in 2025
  • “The Whistle Song” (1991) — the debut single under his own name, and the first of his four US dance-chart No. 1s
