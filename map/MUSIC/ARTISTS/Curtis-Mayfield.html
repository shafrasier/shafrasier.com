Artist

Curtis Mayfield

Era1958–1999
InstrumentVoice (falsetto tenor), guitar, composition
Genres
Chicago soulFunkPsychedelic soul
ListenRYM·Apple Music

The sound that made Curtis Mayfield unmistakable was built from a child’s shortcut. He learned piano from his mother first and loved playing boogie-woogie in F-sharp, all black keys; when he took up the guitar as a boy, he tuned its open strings to those same black keys, and the tuning stayed with him for life. “No one else tunes that way,” he said late in his career. “So it’s a lost art now, a lost art tuning.”1 Nobody could easily copy his voicings, and the caressing hammered lines he drew from that private system became one of soul’s foundational guitar styles — the Library of Congress credits them with shaping Jimi Hendrix, and Mayfield put the relationship simply: “My guitar was like another me.”2 Out of that tuning came the gentlest major voice in soul and the most pointed catalog it carried: the songs the civil-rights movement marched to, and a blueprint for owning the business behind them.3

The church and the wager

Mayfield was born at Cook County Hospital in 1942 and grew up poor across Chicago’s North Side — at least nine grammar schools by the Sun-Times’ count, landing at twelve in a row house at 966 North Hudson in the Cabrini Homes, where he lived into his mid-twenties.4 The music came from his grandmother. The Reverend Annie Bell Mayfield ran the Traveling Souls Spiritualist Church, a storefront congregation, and Curtis absorbed her sermons, “even in my sleep,” he said, while singing tenor with his cousins in the Northern Jubilee Gospel Singers.5 The group’s older member was a teenager named Jerry Butler, who remembered the future star as an afterthought: “We used to kick Curtis to the side because… he was the little guy” — until the little guy learned guitar.6 In 1957 Butler joined the Roosters, a group newly arrived from Chattanooga with Sam Gooden and the brothers Arthur and Richard Brooks, and recruited Mayfield out of his own doo-wop group with a wager: rehearse three nights a week with each, and whichever group improved first got him. The Roosters won.7

The Impressions

At the group’s Vee-Jay audition in April 1958, A&R man Calvin Carter listened to them struggle and then gave an instruction Butler never forgot: “Sing something you’re ashamed to sing.” They sang Butler’s poem-turned-song “For Your Precious Love”, and Carter had his record.8 Released in 1958 under the group’s new name, the Impressions, it reached number 11 on the pop chart and number 3 R&B and sold 150,000 copies in two weeks — but the label’s promo copies read “JERRY BUTLER” in large type with “and the Impressions” small beneath, a billing decision that seeded a resentment the group never fully shed. Butler went solo within months.9 Mayfield, eighteen, became his old friend’s touring guitarist, and on a drive between Philadelphia and Atlantic City the two worked up a melody Butler had been humming into “He Will Break Your Heart,” which spent seven weeks atop the R&B chart in 1960.10 With his tour earnings Mayfield put $1,000 into a session that relaunched the Impressions, now with Fred Cash in Butler’s chair, and his own “Gypsy Woman” reached number 20 pop at the end of 1961.11

The classic Impressions arrived in 1963: the Brooks brothers had gone home to Chattanooga the year before, and the remaining trio — Mayfield, Gooden, Cash — teamed with the arranger Johnny Pate. Pate’s brass-forward charts carried a vocal architecture straight out of the gospel quartets: the “switch-off,” each singer taking the lead in turn while the other two answered, Mayfield’s high tenor balanced against Gooden’s bass and Cash’s lower tenor. “The group went into some high falsetto harmonic things that were really unheard of,” Pate said. “Nobody had done it before. The amazing part was, it’s all in tune, in perfect harmony…”12 Mayfield described the blend as automatic: “there was nothing to work out… through my melody would automatically come the harmonies of Fred and Sam.”13 “It’s All Right” went to number 4 pop and number 1 R&B and gold in 1963, and the group sent five 1964 singles into the pop Top 20.14

The movement’s songbook

“Keep On Pushing” (1964) began as a gospel composition. Mayfield converted it by rewriting its claim of God-given strength into a claim of his own, and the hymn became a march — number 10 pop, number 1 on Cash Box’s R&B chart in the fourteen months Billboard published none.15 The Sun-Times’ obituary reported that it “generally is regarded as the first rhythm-and-blues song to rally blacks behind the civil rights movement,” and forty years later Barack Obama walked out to it at the 2004 convention that made him famous.16 “People Get Ready” (1965) reached deeper. A train song in the Underground Railroad lineage, it sets Mayfield’s silken falsetto over pizzicato strings, his guitar marking time against low French horns while a glockenspiel chimes above — the Library of Congress registry essay reports that Martin Luther King Jr. named it the movement’s unofficial anthem, used to set marchers moving or to calm them.17 Mayfield traced it plainly to the storefront church: “That was taken from my church or from the upbringing of messages from the church… I must have been in a very deep mood of that type of religious inspiration when I wrote that song.”18 The Grammy Hall of Fame took it in 1998, the National Recording Registry in 2015, and Rolling Stone has ranked it as high as number 24 among the greatest songs ever made.19

The messages sharpened as the sixties did. “We’re a Winner” (1967), cut with a party crowd baked into the track, was among the first prominent pop records about Black pride, and Chicago’s powerhouse Top 40 station WLS would not play it. Its own program director, Clark Weber, told NPR decades later why such records died at white radio: “sponsors would call and say, that market is not the market I’m going after.”20 The record went to number 1 R&B anyway, Howard University students made it their victory song when they shut down the campus in 1968, and its title became the motto of Mayfield’s label.21 “Choice of Colors” hit number 1 R&B in 1969. Through all of it Mayfield deflected the prophet’s mantle — “I’m just an entertainer,” he insisted, one who could “say these things and yet not make a person feel as though they’re being preached at.”22 Fred Cash remembered where the songs actually came from: one or two o’clock in the morning, Mayfield sitting on the side of the bed with his guitar.23

Owning as much of himself as possible

The business education began with a wound: Mayfield’s name appears nowhere in the writing credits of “For Your Precious Love.”24 Around eighteen he went to the Library of Congress to learn how copyright worked, then founded his own publishing company with the group’s manager Eddie Thomas. In an era when R&B writers were routinely strong-armed out of their copyrights, a Black teenager owning his own songs was nearly unheard of.25 When he later bought out Butler’s silent stake, Butler asked what he had done wrong. “You didn’t do anything,” Mayfield told him. “I just want to own as much of me as possible.”26 The publishing threw off royalties from a moonlighting empire: through OKeh’s Carl Davis he wrote “The Monkey Time” (number 8, 1963) and “Um, Um, Um, Um, Um, Um” (number 5, 1964) for his old schoolmate Major Lance, plus hits for Gene Chandler and Jan Bradley, while the Impressions ran their own streak.27 After two short-lived labels he founded Curtom Records in March 1968 with Thomas — CURtis plus THOMas — distributed by Buddah, headquartered on South Stony Island, with Mayfield as owner, producer, and A&R man.28 In 1970 he left the Impressions for a solo career, installed Leroy Hutson as their new lead, and kept the group on his label.29

The solo records and the producer’s chair

Curtis (1970) opened his solo career at number 1 on the R&B album chart with the sound gone dark and psychedelic: “(Don’t Worry) If There’s a Hell Below, We’re All Going to Go” runs nearly eight minutes of fuzz, congas, and apocalyptic preaching, while “Move On Up” became the UK hit he never had at home, ten weeks in the top 50.30 Robert Christgau later judged it “cut for cut… stronger than Superfly.”31 Roots followed in 1971, and then Superfly (1972), the commissioned soundtrack that made him a landmark.32 Back to the World (1973) gave him another number 1 R&B album, and then he moved into the producer’s chair and wrote hits for other voices: Claudine (1974) for Gladys Knight & the Pips (“On and On,” number 5 pop), Sparkle (1976) for Aretha Franklin (“Something He Can Feel,” her first R&B number 1 in two years), and Let’s Do It Again (1975) for the Staple Singers, whose title song topped the Hot 100.33 Mavis Staples remembered her father balking at his romantic line until Mayfield pleaded: “Pops, come on. It’s a movie score. It’s not changing your religion. Do it for me, please?” Her father got tickled, she said, and “couldn’t say no to Curtis.”34 The empire didn’t outlast the decade. The historian Robert Pruter blames the rise of disco and the collapse of the Black film market; Curtom ceased operations in 1980, and Mayfield moved his family to Atlanta.35

Wingate Field and New World Order

On August 13, 1990, at an outdoor concert at Wingate Field in Flatbush, Brooklyn, a storm gust toppled the stage’s lighting truss as Mayfield was being introduced. A falling stage light struck the back of his neck and crushed three vertebrae; asked by his drummer if he was okay, he answered, “I think so, but I can’t move.” He was forty-eight, and paralyzed from the neck down.36 He was frank about what followed — “you can’t help but wake up every once in a while with a tear in your eye” — and defiant about the ending: “How many 54-year-old quadriplegics are putting albums out? You just have to deal with what you got, try to sustain yourself as best you can, and look to the things that you can do.”37 New World Order (1996) was recorded lying on his back, gravity pressing on his chest and lungs, the vocals captured a line or two at a time; Rolling Stone’s review called it “a triumphant return.”38 The honors arrived while he could still hear them: the Grammy Legend Award in 1994 (the year’s other recipient was Frank Sinatra), the Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 1995, and two Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inductions: with the Impressions in 1991, then solo in March 1999, the same night as Paul McCartney, making him one of the few artists inducted twice. He was too ill to attend.39 His right leg was amputated in 1998, and he died of complications from diabetes on December 26, 1999, in Roswell, Georgia, at fifty-seven.40

The claims on him kept multiplying. Hendrix, who toured behind him in 1963, said directly what the Library of Congress would later certify: “Curtis was a really good guitarist… He probably influenced me more than anyone I’d ever played with up to that time, that sweet sound of his, you know.”41 Bob Marley’s remade “One Love” carries Mayfield’s name in its credits because it folds in “People Get Ready”; Kanye West built “Touch the Sky” from the slowed horns of “Move On Up.”42 Christgau opened his posthumous guide with the summary the career earned: “Curtis Mayfield wasn’t just a genius, he was a hero.”43 The AP’s obituary heard the whole thing in the voice — “the gentle voice that sounded more like a pensive philosopher than a raging revolutionary” — and the hometown paper put it simplest: “Mr. Mayfield had the soul of a preacher.”44
