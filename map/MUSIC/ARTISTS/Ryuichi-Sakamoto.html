Artist

Ryuichi Sakamoto

Era1978–2023
InstrumentPiano, synthesizer, composition
Genres
ElectronicAmbientModern classicalSynthpopFilm score
ListenRYM·Apple Music

As a Tokyo high schooler, Ryuichi Sakamoto rode a packed commuter train twenty minutes every morning and started counting the noise. “I found more than 10 different sounds, so I started enjoying hearing these sounds,” he remembered. “Of course, the main inspiration was John Cage. Coincidentally, his famous ‘4’33”’ piece was made in the year I was born: ‘52. So I was a baby of silence — or opening ears.”1 The habit never left him, through the synthpop trio that made him a stadium star in Japan, the film scores that won him an Oscar, the glitch collaborations, and the final albums assembled from a failing body and a drowned piano. “I was born in Japan but I don’t think I’m Japanese,” he told an interviewer in 1988. “To be a stranger – I like that attitude. I don’t like nationalities and borders.”2

Yellow Magic Orchestra

He came to the band as a hired hand with elite training. The son of a famous literary editor and a hat designer, Sakamoto studied composition at the Tokyo University of the Arts (a bachelor’s in 1974, then a master’s concentrating in electronic and ethnic music), got his hands on the school’s Buchla, Moog, and ARP synthesizers, and from 1975 worked Tokyo’s session circuit for singers like Taeko Onuki; drummer Yukihiro Takahashi nicknamed him “the Professor” for his command of theory.3 In 1978 bassist Haruomi Hosono invited Sakamoto and Yukihiro Takahashi to dinner and pitched an absurdity: as the story is told, he proposed to “arrange Martin Denny’s ‘Firecracker’ as an electric chunky disco using synthesizers and sell four million copies of the single worldwide.”4 Yellow Magic Orchestra built that joke into a machine. Their sequencer, a Roland MC-8 run by programmer Hideki Matsutake, was so central that the trio treated Matsutake as a de facto fourth member, and their debut wrapped Denny’s exotica in arcade sounds lifted from Space Invaders.5 The single “Computer Game / Firecracker” sold over 400,000 copies in the United States, reached number 60 on the Hot 100 and number 17 in Britain, and climbed to number 18 on Billboard’s R&B chart.6 In November 1980 they played Soul Train, grinning through a cover of Archie Bell’s “Tighten Up” while their manager bounded onstage costumed as a stereotype Japanese tourist, camera and all.7 At home they were the biggest band in the country: their live album topped the chart on two weeks’ sales, Solid State Survivor and X∞Multiplies held Oricon’s top two spots for seven straight weeks, and a “YMO Generation” of screaming fans chased them through the streets. Sakamoto hated it. “We were very big, that’s why I hated it,” he said later. “We were always followed by paparazzi.”8 The band dissolved in 1984: refusing the words “broke up,” they called it sankai, a spreading-out.9

The records inside the band

His solo career started before YMO’s first album was even out. Thousand Knives (1978), titled from a Henri Michaux mescaline image, opens with Sakamoto reciting a Mao poem through a vocoder.10 Two years later came B-2 Unit (1980), cut partly in London with the dub producer Dennis Bovell, and its centerpiece “Riot in Lagos”, a lurching, off-grid electronic track with no straight beat to hold onto. Electro producers heard the future in it: Kurtis Mantronik remembered New York DJs “cutting up Riot in Lagos, and I thought it was the coolest thing,” and one electro history put it plainly: “In 1980, no other music sounded like Riot in Lagos. In 2016, still barely anything does.”11 “Behind the Mask”, a song Sakamoto first wrote for a Seiko watch commercial and sang through a vocoder on Solid State Survivor, ended up in Michael Jackson’s vault. Quincy Jones had brought it to him from Japan; Jackson cut a demo during the Thriller sessions with new lyrics of his own, and the track was left off the album when the royalty split couldn’t be agreed. His lyrics traveled anyway — through Greg Phillinganes’ dance-chart hit, then Eric Clapton’s 1987 UK top-twenty single — until Jackson’s own recording finally surfaced on the posthumous Michael in 2010.12

Merry Christmas, Mr. Lawrence

Nagisa Ōshima cast Sakamoto, who had never acted, as the prison-camp commandant Captain Yonoi opposite David Bowie; Sakamoto took the role on the condition that he could also write the score, his first.13 The shoot marooned the two musicians together. “I was with him for a month, every day, on a very small island in the South Pacific Ocean,” Sakamoto remembered. Did they discuss music? “No, no. I hadn’t started working on it yet, I was totally concentrated on the acting… He was very nice guy. Very straightforward.”14 The main theme he wrote afterward — music-box figures over synthesizer, produced with the early-eighties toolkit of Prophet-5 and Fairlight — became his signature melody; sung by David Sylvian as “Forbidden Colours”, it reached number 16 on the UK singles chart, and the score won the BAFTA for film music.15 Its fame puzzled him for the rest of his life: “it’s still a mystery to me why that piece alone gained such wide acceptance.”16

The Last Emperor and the screen

Bernardo Bertolucci met Sakamoto at Cannes in 1983, and producer Jeremy Thomas later called with a week’s notice to come to China, this time to act, as the Manchukuo film chief Amakasu. The scoring request came only after shooting, with a deadline to match: offered one week, Sakamoto bargained for two, wrote dozens of cues — 64 by his own later count, about half of them used — and flew the tapes to London.17 Watching the finished orchestral rush years later in the documentary Coda, he laughed: “Thank God I was young. I couldn’t do that today.”18 At the 1988 Academy Awards The Last Emperor went nine for nine, and Sakamoto, David Byrne, and Cong Su shared Best Original Score, making Sakamoto the first Japanese composer to win an Academy Award. The score took the Golden Globe and the Grammy too.19 He scored more than forty features in all, winning a second Golden Globe for Bertolucci’s The Sheltering Sky (1990).20 The late landmark was The Revenant (2015): Alejandro Iñárritu’s office called with “Come to LA as soon as possible,” and Sakamoto, a year out from cancer treatment, built its huge, groaning score with the German artist Carsten Nicolai, who records as Alva Noto, and Bryce Dessner. It drew Golden Globe, BAFTA, and Grammy nominations; the Academy’s music branch ruled the three-way collaboration ineligible for the Oscar.21

The piano, the glitch, and the placards

At the century’s turn he became, briefly, a healing-music phenomenon at home. The solo-piano album BTTB (1999) spun off “Energy Flow,” a balm of a piano piece whose maxi single topped the Oricon chart, finished among 1999’s best-selling singles in Japan, and is reported as the first instrumental number one in the chart’s history. The same year his opera LIFE premiered, with contributions from Pina Bausch, the Dalai Lama, and Salman Rushdie.22 Then he swerved the other way. With Nicolai he began trading files — Sakamoto sent piano figures, Nicolai dismantled them into clicks and micro-loops — and the duo’s Vrioon (2002) and Insen (2005) became a template for the quiet wing of 2000s electronic music.23 The same years put him at the front of Japan’s antinuclear movement: he launched the Stop Rokkasho campaign against a nuclear reprocessing plant in 2006, founded the forest-conservation group More Trees in 2007, and after Fukushima headlined the No Nukes 2012 festival he organized, where a reunited YMO covered Kraftwerk’s “Radioactivity” in English while Kraftwerk sang it in Japanese. At a Yoyogi Park rally that summer he told a crowd of tens of thousands, “Keeping silent after Fukushima is barbaric.”24

async and the last works

The throat cancer behind that Revenant convalescence, diagnosed in 2014, had stopped everything for a year.25 async (2017), his first solo album in eight years, was conceived, he said, as the soundtrack to an imaginary Tarkovsky film.26 Its instruments included a piano salvaged from a school gymnasium flooded by the 2011 tsunami, recorded warped and untuned on the track “ZURE”. “I felt as if I was playing the corpse of a piano that had drowned,” he said, and he refused to have it repaired: an instrument “detuned by nature.”27 On “fullmoon”, the voice of Paul Bowles — taped while Sakamoto was scoring The Sheltering Sky — asks how many more times you will watch the full moon rise, and the question recirculates in German, Farsi, Chinese, Russian.28 Pitchfork heard “an album bearing a sense of mortality surrounding its every sound,” with “a warmth and fragility… that makes it stand apart”; Fact named it the best album of 2017.29 Sakamoto was direct about the stakes: “after the cancer, I thought this could be my last one so I thought I should do everything that I wanted to do.”30 A second cancer, disclosed in 2021 and stage four by 2022, sharpened the frame further. “I hope to be able to make music until my last moment,” he wrote, “like Bach and Debussy whom I adore.”31

12 arrived on his seventy-first birthday, January 17, 2023: twelve sketches titled with the dates they were recorded, his labored breathing left audible in the mix. He likened it to a diary. Resident Advisor called it “the most foreboding, but also one of the most beautiful, in a towering catalogue of music.”32 That September’s Venice festival premiered Opus, a black-and-white concert film shot by his son Neo Sora over a week in an NHK studio Sakamoto considered the best-sounding room in Japan: twenty pieces, sequenced by the dying composer himself, from YMO to the film themes to 12.33 He died on March 28, 2023, at seventy-one; the announcement, held until after a private family funeral, said “He lived with music until the very end” and closed with his favorite phrase, ars longa, vita brevis.34 Six weeks later his team published the playlist he had quietly built to be played at his own funeral: two and a half hours, opening with Alva Noto and closing with Laurel Halo, with Bach, Debussy, Satie, and Ennio Morricone in between.35 The obituaries reached for him from every direction — NPR called him “a godfather of electronic pop,” the Guardian “a restless traveller, personally and musically.”36 His memoirs, the first published in Japan in 2009, carry the whole ethos in the title of their combined English edition: Music Sets You Free.37
