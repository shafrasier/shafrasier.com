Album

Like a Virgin

Madonna
ReleasedNovember 12, 1984
LabelSire
RecordedApril – May 1984
ProducerNile Rodgers
StudioPower Station (New York)
Genres
Primary
Synthpop
ListenRYM·Apple Music

The title song of Madonna’s second album was written by a man driving through his father’s vineyards. Billy Steinberg drafted the lyric in the Coachella Valley, fresh out of a bruising relationship and into a new one — “I always wrote my song lyrics in a sort of a stream of consciousness,” he said, and the hook arrived unbidden: “‘Shiny and new, like a virgin,’ I don’t know, it just popped out.”1 Michael Ostin of Warner played her the demo, and her read, recalled years later, settled the matter: “How can you be like a virgin? I liked the play on words, I thought they were clever. They’re so geeky, they’re cool.”2 The record built around the song settled in one winter how big she was going to be.

”Help me!”

The hire behind the record began as a rejection. Warner turned down Madonna’s request for full creative control of her second album and offered her a choice of producer instead; her appeal went to Seymour Stein by letter — “Here I am forced to choose a man once again – help me!” — with Nile Rodgers at the top of her list, on the strength of the record he had just made, David Bowie’s Let’s Dance.3 Rodgers came warned. He had been told she was a “totally self-centered bitch” and a “pain in the ass to work with”; on meeting her he judged her a “true professional.”4 The terms were set in one exchange. Madonna: “If you don’t love these songs we can’t work together.” Rodgers: “I don’t love them now, but I will when I’ve finished working on them!”5

Studio C

The sessions ran through the Power Station in April and May 1984, and the correction they made was a band. She and Rodgers agreed the debut had come out too slick and too synthetic; his answer was to reconvene the rhythm engine of Chic: Bernard Edwards on bass, Tony Thompson on drums, and his own guitar, cutting live to the cassette demos she had made with Stephen Bray; five of the nine tracks are hers, four of them written with him.6 The engineer Jason Corsaro learned what job he had taken from the radio: “The first day I learned that we would be working on it, I was in Bernard Edwards’ car and we heard ‘Holiday’ on the radio.”7 Corsaro fought Rodgers for the big live room — “Why can’t we record Tony in the big room?”; “No, no, R&B is in the dead room” — won, and close-miked Thompson with the one microphone that could take the punishment, a Shure SM58 with a pop shield. His summary of the rhythm section was an engineer’s shrug: “You can put up the worst mics ever and, with a great drummer and a great bass player and a great guitarist, you look like a genius.”8 He also pushed the project onto a Sony 3324 digital recorder, early conviction rather than label policy: “I was sure the digital medium was going to be the future,” and, of the exposed arrangements, “if I made a bad move you could hear it.”9 The client ran the clock. Her greeting to late musicians was “Time is money, and the money is mine!”, and when her treatment of a band member went too far, Rodgers walked out until she apologized. Corsaro’s verdict: “She was very determined for the record to be successful, and she made no bones about it.”10

The song he hated

Rodgers wanted “Material Girl” as the lead single and thought the title song “really queer,” doubting the hook could carry a hit. Four days later it was still running in his head, and he surrendered: “if it’s so catchy, it must be something. So let’s do it.”11 The song had already survived one round of doubters. Tom Kelly had first set Steinberg’s lyric as a ballad; the breakthrough came when he attacked it in falsetto over a Motown-style bass line — Steinberg: “That is it right there, that’s Like A Virgin” — and label men still rejected the demo as bizarre until Ostin carried it to her.12 Steinberg called her recording of it “the happiest moment of my life, at least of my musical life.”13 “Material Girl” came from outside too, written by Peter Brown and Robert Rans and routed through Ostin, and she took both songs on the same terms: “I liked them both because they were ironic and provocative at the same time but also unlike me. I am not a materialistic person.”14

What it inherits and what it introduces

The low end carries the debt: the record put disco’s most celebrated rhythm section back at the top of the pop chart five years after the backlash, with Rob Sabino, Chic’s keyboardist, overdubbing Prophet-5 synthesizer onto the live takes.15 The newer move was the campaign, packaging that argued as loudly as the songs. Steven Meisel shot the cover in a suite at the St. Regis, Madonna in bridal lace over a “BOY TOY” belt buckle, a look her stylist Maripol summarized as a “bride of Satan.”16 The bridal irony and the outside songs chosen for being “unlike me” added up to a pop album designed as a provocation with a dance record inside it.

The winter of 1984–85

The commercial run was a landslide by installments, and it began on a stage: at the first MTV Video Music Awards that September, she sang the title song in a wedding dress and finished it rolling on the floor.17 Released November 12, 1984, the album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 70. The single reached No. 1 on the chart of December 22 and held for six weeks, her first; the album followed on February 9, 1985, her first No. 1, for three weeks.18 “Material Girl” reached No. 2, and “Angel” and “Dress You Up” both hit No. 5.19 By July 1985 it was the first album by a woman past five million American copies; it went on to a diamond certification and roughly 21 million worldwide.20 Britain held out longest: the album reached No. 1 there only in September 1985, after an August international reissue added “Into the Groove” — the Desperately Seeking Susan song that was never an American single, went out in the US as the B-side of the “Angel” twelve-inch, and gave her a first UK No. 1 on its own.21

The reviews trailed the sales at a distance. Rolling Stone’s Debby Miller heard “her light voice bob[bing] over the heavy rhythm and synth tracks like a kid on a carnival ride” but granted “an undercurrent of ambition that makes her more than the latest Betty Boop.” Robert Christgau graded it a B, willing to take the sex fantasy “as long as the fantasy of it remains out front”; People called it a “tolerable bit of fluff.”22 Retrospect reversed the verdict: AllMusic’s Stephen Thomas Erlewine wrote that she “didn’t become a superstar, an icon, until Like a Virgin,” even while conceding the calculation “throws the record’s balance off”; Billboard’s Chuck Arnold put it flatly — she “has definitely made better albums,” but this one “changed the course of pop history.”23

Influence and legacy

The panic and the merchandise arrived together. The Parents Music Resource Center put “Dress You Up” on its 1985 Filthy Fifteen, the list that produced the Parental Advisory sticker, for lyrics that dress a lover in nothing more explicit than clothes.24 The same spring, TIME’s cover story coined a name for her imitators — “Wanna Be’s (as in ‘We wanna be like Madonna!’)” — and reported “Twelve-year-old girls, headphones blocking out the voices of reason… running around wearing T shirts labeled VIRGIN”; Macy’s opened a Madonnaland boutique and hosted a look-alike contest judged by Andy Warhol.25 Madonna’s own memory of the crowds catalogs the uniform: women would show up, she recalled, with “flap skirts on and tights cut off below their knees and lace gloves and rosaries and bows in their hair.”26 The title song’s afterlife stayed argumentative. Quentin Tarantino opened Reservoir Dogs (1992) with his own character reading the song as pure carnality, and Madonna answered by autographing a copy of Erotica to him: “To Quentin, it’s not about dick. It’s about love. Madonna.”27 The other single became the label she never shed: “My least favorite is ‘Material Girl.’ I never, ever want to hear it again!” The Tidal critic John Murph credited the album with “creat[ing] an archetype of pop stardom that the likes of Gwen Stefani, P!nk and Lady Gaga have followed.”28 The distance the record traveled is easiest to measure at the ends: the album People called fluff sits, as of 2023, in the National Recording Registry.29
