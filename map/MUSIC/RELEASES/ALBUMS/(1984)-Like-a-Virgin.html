Like a Virgin is the second studio album by Madonna, released on November 12, 1984, by Sire. Nile Rodgers produced it at the Power Station in New York in April and May 1984, after Warner Bros. refused her request for creative control of the record and offered her a choice of producer instead. Four of its nine songs came from outside writers, the title track from Billy Steinberg and Tom Kelly, “Material Girl” from Peter Brown and Robert Rans, “Dress You Up” from Andrea LaRusso and Peggy Stanziale, and the Rose Royce cover from Miles Gregory; she wrote or co-wrote the other five, four of them with Stephen Bray. The album is dance-pop, and it alternates between two rhythm sections: the Chic players on four tracks and drum machines on the other five.

It settled in one winter how big she was going to be. The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 70 and reached No. 1 on February 9, 1985, holding three weeks; the title single had already spent six weeks at No. 1, the first of her career. “Material Girl” reached No. 2, and “Angel” and “Dress You Up” both stopped at No. 5. By July 1985 it was the first album by a woman to pass five million American copies, and it now stands at a diamond certification and roughly twenty-one million worldwide. The trade press was warm from the first week and the rock press was not; the record went into the National Recording Registry in 2023.

Background

The first album had not finished happening. Madonna went gold in June 1984, eleven months after release, and Billboard’s dance columnist read the delay as the method working: her singles had built her a club audience, then radio, then the album, which he called a true case study in the platforming of sales and airplay.1 The debut did not reach its own chart peak until October 1984, the month “Lucky Star” did, so the second album was recorded and mixed by an artist whose first one was still rising.2 The trade’s year-end tally for 1984 put her twenty-sixth among album artists on the strength of that one record and third among women, behind Cyndi Lauper and Linda Ronstadt and ahead of Barbra Streisand and Tina Turner; she did not appear at all among the year’s top new pop artists.3 Cash Box, in the week the second album shipped, still called her “the new artist.”4

The hire began as a rejection. Warner turned down her request for full creative control of the second album and offered her a choice of producer, and her appeal went to Seymour Stein by letter: “Here I am forced to choose a man once again – help me!” She was blunter in public, describing her bosses as a hierarchy of old men and the situation as a chauvinistic environment in which she was treated like a sexy little girl. Nile Rodgers was at the top of her list, on the strength of the record he had just made, David Bowie’s Let’s Dance.5

She knew what she wanted changed and said so before a note was cut. The first album’s producers had come out of a different school, she told NME, and the new production would not be so slick: “I want a sound that’s mine.” The songs on the debut she called weak, and she had picked every song on this one and wanted them all to be hits with no filler.6

Reggie Lucas, who had produced that debut, gave the clearest account of the handoff, and gave it while the second album was still unreleased. “I wanted to push her in a pop direction. She was a little more oriented toward the disco thing, but I thought she had appeal to a general market,” he told Rolling Stone in November 1984. “It’s funny about that thing with Kamins. The same thing that happened to him pretty much happened to me on her second record, when they had Nile Rodgers.” In the same piece Bray answered the charge that she was being exploited: “People say that, but that’s resentment of someone who’s got the drive. She doesn’t care if she ruffles someone’s feathers.”7 That article was her first Rolling Stone cover, reported before the album shipped, and it recorded the imitators six months before Time gave them a name — the clones were already ratting their hair, putting on rosaries and baring their bellies from coast to coast. Her own line in it was a prediction: “All I said was, ‘I know this record is good, and one of these days Warner Bros. and the rest of them are going to figure it out.‘”8

Rodgers came warned. He had been told she was a “totally self-centered bitch” and difficult to work with; on meeting her he judged her a true professional.9 The terms were set in one exchange. Madonna told him that if he did not love the songs they could not work together, and he answered that he did not love them yet but would once he had finished working on them.10

Recording

The sessions ran through the Power Station in April and May 1984, and the correction they set out to make was a band. She and Rodgers agreed the debut had come out too slick and too synthetic, and his answer was to reconvene the rhythm engine of Chic: Bernard Edwards on bass, Tony Thompson on drums and his own guitar, cutting live against the cassette demos she had made with Bray.11

The engineer Jason Corsaro learned what job he had taken from the radio, hearing “Holiday” in Edwards’s car on the day he was told.12 He fought Rodgers for the big live room, arguing that R&B did not have to be cut in the dead one, won, and close-miked Thompson with the only microphone he trusted to survive it, a Shure SM58 behind a pop shield. His summary of the rhythm section was an engineer’s shrug about looking like a genius when the players are that good.13 He also pushed the project onto a Sony 3324 digital recorder out of conviction rather than label policy, believing digital was the future, and noted that the arrangements were exposed enough that a bad move could be heard.14 The sleeve turned the decision into a boast: “Fanatically recorded digitally from start to finish on Sony equipment.”15

The client ran the clock. Her greeting to late musicians was that time was money and the money was hers, and when her treatment of a band member went too far Rodgers walked out until she apologized. Corsaro’s verdict was that she was very determined for the record to succeed and made no bones about it.16

The song Rodgers hated

Rodgers wanted “Material Girl” as the lead single and thought the title song “really queer,” doubting the hook could carry a hit. Four days later it was still running in his head and he surrendered: if it was that catchy, it must be something.17 The song had already survived one round of doubters. Kelly first set Steinberg’s lyric as a ballad; the breakthrough came when he attacked it in falsetto over a Motown-style bass line, which Steinberg heard immediately as the record, and label men still rejected the demo as bizarre until Michael Ostin carried it to Madonna.18 Steinberg had drafted the lyric driving through his father’s vineyards in the Coachella Valley, and said the hook simply popped out.19 Her own reading settled it: you cannot be like a virgin, she liked the play on words, and she thought the writers so geeky they were cool.20

Music

Like a Virgin is dance-pop with synthpop and new wave in it, and the received account of it as a Chic record is only half right. The sleeve credits Edwards and Thompson on four of the nine tracks: “Material Girl,” the title song, the Rose Royce cover “Love Don’t Live Here Anymore” and the doo-wop pastiche “Shoo-Bee-Doo.” The other five are drum-machine records, programmed by Jimmy Bralower on a LinnDrum and Simmons pads. So the album alternates, and it alternates by idiom: the live band plays the songs that lean on an older music, and the machines play the ones critics call new wave. Rodgers plays guitar on all nine, which is why it still sounds like one record, and Robert Sabino’s bass synthesizer runs under seven of them, doubling Edwards on the title track.21

Stephen Holden heard the seams and named them one by one in the New York Times. Rodgers, he wrote, “has adapted some of the elements of Chic’s sound — a scrubby guitar, glistening electric keyboards and light, strutting dance-pop rhythms — to enshrine Madonna’s personality,” and the album spends that adaptation across idioms: Philadelphia soul on “Love Don’t Live Here Anymore,” orchestrated doo-wop on “Shoo-Bee-Doo,” frilly pop-soul on “Dress You Up” and “Angel,” and a reggae lilt he alone heard in “Material Girl.” His favorite was the one nobody remembers: “Pretender” is “as elegant a piece of disco fluff as Chic at its airiest.”22

The title track is built on two borrowed hooks. The three-note bass motif reworks the Four Tops’ “I Can’t Help Myself,” a lift Billboard’s own singles reviewer clocked in the week it shipped, and the chordal guitar stab comes out of Chuck Berry. The record takes its time: it bops on the opening chord through the whole introduction and the first four bars of the verse, close to twenty seconds before anything changes, and Rodgers enters with four sharp strums of that same chord spread over two bars, which is what gives the song its relaxed strut while the bass prowls underneath. The falsetto release after the title phrase is withheld until the second chorus. The ad-libs at the end are not hers; they were copied faithfully off the Steinberg and Kelly demo.23 Rikky Rooksby’s verdict on why the four live tracks sound different is one sentence about the drummer: Thompson gives it far more wallop than a drum machine ever could.24

Published sheet music exists for only three of the songs, so most of the record has no key or tempo on paper. “Material Girl” is marked at 120 beats per minute in C major, with verses on C Mixolydian, and “Angel” at 133 in G major.25 The textures around them are varied deliberately: Rodgers arranged and conducted strings for “Love Don’t Live Here Anymore,” Sabino plays acoustic piano on “Shoo-Bee-Doo” and Fender Rhodes on the Rose Royce cover, and Lenny Pickett takes a saxophone solo.26

The deep cuts do more than the reputation allows. Alfred Soto called “Angel” the apex of Rodgers’s post-Chic skills, hearing a moody opening of pizzicato guitar, canned laughter and a dark keyboard under a giddy lyric, with Madonna dipping into her lower register before the vowel on eyes opens out; she tied the song to her Catholic upbringing and the calling of angels for protection.27 “Over and Over” is a resilience lyric about picking oneself up from disappointment, with what Chuck Arnold heard as some of the punk attitude of “Burning Up.” “Stay” is the album’s one departure from duple meter, a fast triple rhythm with synthesizers, the sound of someone slapping a microphone, and a spoken passage near the end. The robotic male voice repeating the hook on “Material Girl” is Frank Simms. “Shoo-Bee-Doo” opens as a stripped-down piano ballad before turning bouncy, and has kept a reputation as the track that shows her singing better than any of the hits.28

The singing is lighter and higher than the record under it, which every 1984 reviewer noticed and most held against her. Debby Miller in Rolling Stone heard her light voice bobbing over the heavy rhythm tracks like a kid on a carnival ride, and still granted an undercurrent of ambition that made her more than the latest Betty Boop.29 Holden was more precise and less kind about the instrument and more admiring of the frame around it: a “kewpie-doll voice” and “a tough-cookie voice that’s both coy and streetwise,” with the arrangements “spun around” it “like swirls of cotton candy,” which he thought made the album the year’s definitive model of danceable urban teen-pop. He also placed her in a line running back through the Shirelles and the Ronettes, and read the persona as economic: “In matters of love, she is a comparison shopper with a shrewd sense of her own market value.”22

Four of the nine songs are not hers, and three of those four are the famous ones. “Like a Virgin” is a first-person account of being made new by a lover, and its scandal was always in the simile rather than the sentiment. “Material Girl” is a comic monologue in the voice of a woman who prices her suitors, which she took on the same terms as the title song, liking both because they were ironic and provocative and unlike her.30 “Dress You Up,” by Andrea LaRusso and Peggy Stanziale, is a courtship lyric that dresses a lover in clothes, and of the man in it, as Holden noticed, we are told only that he sleeps on satin sheets and wears custom English suits. “Love Don’t Live Here Anymore,” by Miles Gregory, is the record’s one piece of grief, sung straight. What she wrote herself is the record’s other half: “Angel,” “Over and Over,” “Shoo-Bee-Doo,” “Pretender” and “Stay.”

Title and artwork

Steven Meisel shot the cover in a suite at the St. Regis: Madonna sepia-tinted on a satin bed in a wedding dress over a tight bustier, full-length gloves, heavy makeup, hair disordered, staring into the lens, with a belt buckle reading BOY TOY. Her stylist Maripol summarized the look as a bride of Satan. The sleeve credits the art direction and design to Jeri McManus and Jeffrey Kent Ayeroff, the Warner executive who also commissioned the video and was then launching the label’s home-video division, so the image, the clip and the cassette all ran through one desk.31

The album takes its title from its third song, and the bridal image is an argument about that song rather than an illustration of it. Matthew Rettenmund read the cover as communicating at once what a wedding dress is supposed to represent and what it actually does, virginity against sexual knowingness. The scholar Graham Thompson found the provocation one layer further back, in the link between her own given name, the Catholic title for Mary, and the doctrine the title plays on, and argued that the artwork presents her as a desiring subject and not only an object of desire. The Colombian critic Manolo Bellón put it as a matter of performance: this is the first cover on which she plays a character, a virgin who does not want to be one.32

What it inherits and what it introduces

The low end carries the debt. The record put disco’s most celebrated rhythm section back at the top of the pop chart five years after the backlash, with Chic’s keyboardist overdubbing a Prophet-5 onto the live takes, and it did so at a moment when saying the word disco in public was still bad business.33 The doo-wop pastiche and the Rose Royce cover reach further back than that, and the machine tracks reach sideways into the synthpop and new wave of the moment. Alexis Petridis heard the whole cocktail as post-disco with a dash of hip-hop in the rhythm of “Pretender” and a surprisingly large shot of choppy, angular new-wave rock, and caught on it “the faint scent of the Mudd Club and the Paradise Garage” — the rooms she had come out of, audible on a record that was about to take her permanently out of them. He also supplies the before and after: this was the last album on which she sounded like a product of the environment that made her.34

What it introduced was the campaign. The packaging argued as loudly as the songs, and the two outside songs at the front of the record were chosen for being unlike her, so the album is a pop record designed as a provocation with a dance record inside it. The performance that made the argument public came before the album: at the first MTV Video Music Awards in September 1984 she sang the title song in a wedding dress and finished it rolling on the floor.35

Release

The album was finished long before it came out, and the first album is why. Billboard announced it on June 30, 1984 as due in about a month; by October it had been rescheduled into the “October hot album releases” table for the twenty-ninth, billed as the month’s top non-CBS release; it finally appeared on November 12, catalog 25157, at a list price of $8.98. Rodgers explained the wait simply: the debut was still the focus, “then ‘Borderline’ hit out of the clear blue sky. We didn’t expect that.”36

It had already been heard in public once. On May 16, 1984, five days after the sessions ended, Madonna sang “Like a Virgin” and “Dress You Up” at Keith Haring’s birthday party at the Paradise Garage, wearing a leather jacket Haring had painted for her, performing from a brass bed dressed with frills and white roses in front of a downtown art crowd.37

“Like a Virgin” preceded the album by twelve days in the United States and reached most of Europe on November 11. “Material Girl” followed in January 1985, “Angel” in April, backed on the American twelve-inch with “Into the Groove”, and “Dress You Up” on July 31.38 An international reissue in August 1985 added “Into the Groove,” the Desperately Seeking Susan song that was never an American single and gave her a first British No. 1 on its own; the album reached No. 1 in Britain only that September, ten months after release.39

Mary Lambert directed the video after Ayeroff asked her for something outrageous, and shot it in Venice and New York, the palazzo interiors at Ca’ Zenobio degli Armeni. She worked from the Carnival of Venice and from things not being what they seem, which produced a lion mask and then an actual lion, brought from Rome with its trainer standing off-camera holding a rifle; in one take the animal put its head between Madonna’s legs and roared. Ayeroff put the budget between a hundred and fifty and a hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars, far more than the label had ever spent on a clip. Her own comment afterward was that in a past life she must have been a lion or a cat. No video was ever made for “Angel” — one was planned and dropped because too many of her clips were already competing for airtime — and the “Dress You Up” video is a Virgin Tour performance filmed in Detroit.40

The label misread what it had. Warner Bros. formed a home-video division that autumn, and one of its two first releases was a twenty-minute compilation of her four most recent clips, shipping December 7 at $19.95. The trade was skeptical, since the videos were the same ones already running on MTV; one distributor said publicly that if that was all it was, it would not break out of music video’s low-sales rut. The cassette then held No. 1 on Billboard’s music videocassette chart from April to November 1985, went platinum, and took the retailers’ own award.41 On December 13, 1984 she sang the title song on Top of the Pops in a pink wig and a gold metallic jacket, a performance a BBC viewers’ poll later voted the second best in the program’s history. On February 9, 1985, the night the album reached No. 1 in America, Cabaret Metro in Chicago charged five dollars for a Virgin Party, told twelve hundred people to wear white, and screened the videocassette.42

Reception

The trade press was warm from the first week, which is the opposite of the story usually told about this record. Billboard gave it a Spotlight, the designation it used for albums it expected to reach the top ten or go platinum, and the unsigned notice credited “producer Nile Rodgers’ sleek but sinewy rhythm arrangements, burnished to surgical sharpness by crisp digital sonics,” heard “an updated disco groove” firing “the anything but virginal content of these songs,” and faulted only the ballad. Cash Box made it the lead album pick of the week, a hit out of the box for radio and the clubs with a healthy dose of disco-rock. Billboard’s singles reviewer had already called the title track an out-of-the-box radio smash, and named the Four Tops bass line doing the work.43

Brian Chin’s notice in the same paper was the most attentive of any published that winter. He granted at the start that she does no searching whatsoever in her singing, then said what she does instead: she hits her notes straight on, and against a pretty enough melody sounds attractive, porcelain-like and occasionally vulnerable. He called it “her fine second album,” judged that it rarely suggests its own title, singled out “Angel” as closest to the rhythm and blues mode of “Borderline” and “Lucky Star,” found most of the rest wave-pop that was harder than the debut and softer-centered than its competition, and thought “Dress You Up” prettier than one could imagine a song at that tempo. His summary of Rodgers was light-handed, finessed production.44

The consumer press was the cool wing. Miller’s Rolling Stone notice is the representative one, and Robert Christgau graded it a B, willing to take the sex fantasy as long as the fantasy stayed out front; People called it a tolerable bit of fluff.45 Rettenmund, writing the album’s National Recording Registry essay decades later, gave the readiest explanation for the split: the record “rankled mostly male rock critics, who sneered at Madonna’s unbridled sensuality,” still basking in the glow of Disco Demolition Night and finding creative ways to review a dance record without saying that disco sucks. One epithet they reached for was Minnie Mouse on helium.46 Retrospect reversed the verdict without pretending the record was better than it is. AllMusic’s Stephen Thomas Erlewine wrote that she did not become a superstar until this album, while conceding that the calculation throws its balance off, and Billboard’s Chuck Arnold put it flatly: she has made better albums, and this one changed the course of pop history.47

The chart run was a landslide by installments. The album entered at No. 70 on December 1, 1984, and reached No. 1 ten weeks later; the title single topped the Hot 100 on December 22 and held six weeks. Its American chart life ran a hundred and eight consecutive weeks, and its British life a hundred and thirty-four. The Official Charts page has “Like a Virgin” entering Britain at No. 51 rather than the No. 5 usually reported.48 By May 1985 Time could report four and a half million American copies sold, and the diamond certification the record carries now was awarded more than a decade after that.49

Legacy

The panic arrived six months late, and it arrived through a child. The Parents Music Resource Center did not exist when the album came out; it was founded in May 1985, after Tipper Gore heard her daughter playing a record, and it put “Dress You Up” on its Filthy Fifteen under the heading of sex, for a lyric that dresses a lover in nothing more explicit than clothes.50 The list’s first entry was Prince’s “Darling Nikki,” from the album that had been at No. 1 for sixteen weeks when Like a Virgin shipped. The two records that provoked the parents’ movement were on the same chart in the same season, on the same label.51

The merchandise arrived on time. Time’s cover story that spring coined a name for the imitators and reported twelve-year-olds in T-shirts reading VIRGIN, while Macy’s opened a Madonnaland boutique and ran a look-alike contest judged by Andy Warhol. Madonna’s own memory of those crowds catalogs the uniform, down to the rosaries and the lace gloves.52 “Weird Al” Yankovic took the title song for “Like a Surgeon,” which reached No. 47, and did it at her suggestion: she had wondered aloud when he would get around to her, the only time he took a parody title from the artist he was parodying.53

The song’s afterlife stayed argumentative, and she kept correcting it. In 1987 she explained that she had been singing about how something made her feel brand-new and fresh, “and everyone else interpreted it as, ‘I don’t want to be a virgin anymore.‘”54 Quentin Tarantino opened Reservoir Dogs (1992) with a character reading it as pure carnality, and Madonna answered by autographing a copy of Erotica to him with a one-line correction.55 The other single became a label she never shed: “I hate that name,” she said in 2010. “Everyone relates that title to me because of the song I did like 300 years ago. It’s a title I got stuck with.” The Tidal critic John Murph credited the album with creating an archetype of pop stardom that Gwen Stefani, Pink and Lady Gaga followed.56 The distance the record traveled is easiest to measure at the ends: the album People called fluff entered the National Recording Registry in 2023.57
