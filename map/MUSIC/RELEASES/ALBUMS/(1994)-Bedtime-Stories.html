Bedtime Stories is the sixth studio album by Madonna, released on October 25, 1994, by Maverick and Sire. It followed Erotica (1992) and the book Sex (1992), whose reception had turned much of the press against her, and an appearance on Late Show with David Letterman in March 1994 that became the most censored episode in the history of American talk shows. She recorded it between February and August 1994 with three American R&B producers, Babyface, Dallas Austin, and Dave Hall, and the British producer Nellee Hooper, whose credits included Björk’s Debut (1993), after ending her first sessions for the record with Shep Pettibone, the producer of Erotica. The album is contemporary R&B at slow and medium tempos, sung mostly in her lower register, and five of its eleven tracks are built on samples of R&B, funk, jazz, and hip hop records. The exception in sound is “Bedtime Story,” written by Björk, Hooper, and Marius de Vries and produced by Hooper in the electronic style of his work with Björk; it is the one track on the album Madonna did not write.

The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 3, reached No. 2 in Britain, and was certified triple platinum in the United States in 2005. The two singles released in 1994 were the hits: “Secret” reached No. 3 on the Hot 100 and “Take a Bow” spent seven weeks at No. 1, her longest run at that position. The two singles of 1995, “Bedtime Story” and “Human Nature,” missed the American top 40, the first of her singles to do so since 1982. The album was nominated for the Grammy for Best Pop Album. Reviews in 1994 were mostly favorable and mostly moderate, and they disagreed over whether the record was a retreat from the material of the previous two years or a reply to its critics; Barbara O’Dair, in Rolling Stone, argued that it was both. Assessments from 2003 onward, beginning with Sal Cinquemani in Slant, have rated it higher than the contemporary press did, and its title track is the beginning of the electronic direction Madonna followed on Ray of Light (1998).

Background

Madonna released Erotica on October 20, 1992, and Sex, a book of photographs by Steven Meisel, the following day. The reception of the book became, in practice, the reception of the album, which stopped at No. 2; that story is told at Erotica. The two years that followed gave the press three more episodes to cite. The film Body of Evidence, released in January 1993, grossed $38 million against a $30 million budget and won her the Golden Raspberry for Worst Actress.1 The Girlie Show, a tour of thirty-nine dates between September 25 and December 19, 1993, grossed $70 million and drew a mixed press, although Jon Pareles wrote in The New York Times that it broke fewer taboos than her previous tour and made her seem “likeable again.”2 In the spring of 1994 Entertainment Weekly called her “shockingly passe,” and a Singapore newspaper asked in a headline whether she was “a very rich has-been.”3 The same spring, her single “I’ll Remember” (1994), written with Patrick Leonard for the film With Honors, reached No. 2 on the Hot 100 and finished the year at No. 13 on the year-end chart.4

On March 31, 1994, Madonna appeared on Late Show with David Letterman on CBS. She said “fuck” fourteen times, handed David Letterman a pair of her underwear, smoked a cigar, and refused to leave the stage; the episode became the most censored in the history of American talk shows and drew Letterman’s highest ratings since his first week on the network.5

The Letterman appearance, March 31, 1994

The segment had been arranged by phone with the show’s segment producer, Daniel Kellison, after her publicist Liz Rosenberg said Madonna wanted to give Letterman back some of the jokes he had been making about her. The plan was that she would complain about the jokes and then show three of the worst on tape. She arrived with only her makeup artist, Kevyn Aucoin, greeted Kellison with an obscenity he later described as delivered “like a petulant 8-year-old child challenging a parent,” and told him she could not remember the plan.6 A minute into the interview she called Letterman “a sick fuck,” then told the audience she had given him her underwear and asked him to smell it. When she would not leave at the end of her segment, the executive producer, Robert Morton, told Kellison to “get rid of her,” and Kellison walked onstage during a band break, took her hand as if helping her up, and led her off.7 Ken Tucker wrote in Entertainment Weekly that for the first time in her career “the primary emotion she inspired was pity”; the New York Post headlined its front page “Stupid Madonna Tricks.”8 Whether the swearing had been cleared in advance is disputed. Madonna said in 1996 that the show’s producers had told her it would be funny if she swore and was bleeped; Kellison said in 2024 that he might have told her swearing was fine but had not encouraged her in the way she later described.9 Letterman walked her onstage at the MTV Video Music Awards on September 8, 1994, to present an award, kissed her hand, and left her with one line: “I’ll be in the car. Watch your language.”10 Madonna said in 1996 that she had regretted the appearance immediately because of the reaction, and later decided it had been the right thing to do.11

The music on American pop radio had changed since Erotica. R&B led it in 1994: Boyz II Men’s II entered the Billboard 200 at No. 1 in August and its single “I’ll Make Love to You” (1994) spent fourteen weeks at No. 1 on the Hot 100, and Toni Braxton’s debut and Janet Jackson’s janet. were among the ten best-selling albums of the year.12 Two related styles were in play. New jack swing, the fusion of R&B singing with hip hop rhythm and production that Teddy Riley and Dallas Austin had led since the late 1980s, was giving way to what Sean Combs had named hip-hop soul when he promoted Mary J. Blige’s What’s the 411? in 1992: singers performing directly over the kind of sample-built tracks found on hip hop records.13 The critics’ consensus lay elsewhere: the Village Voice poll for 1994 placed Hole, Pavement, R.E.M., and Nirvana at the top.14

Madonna described her intentions for the record in the same terms throughout its promotion. She told the trade paper Music & Media that she had set out to make “an R&B-influenced record, which is in a way going back to my roots, because my very first record was more R&B,” that the songs were “romantic and very reflective,” and that Erotica by contrast had been “much more club/house music” with “a somewhat industrial feel to it.”15 To the chart historian Fred Bronson she said the idea had been to set her singing against “a hardcore hip-hop sensibility” and still make a Madonna record.16 She told Q that she had been in “an incredibly reflective state of mind” and had written in a romantic mood because that was the mood she was in.17

Recording

The first sessions, in early 1994, were with Shep Pettibone, who had produced Erotica, and Madonna ended them. Asked that November why the album’s liner notes thanked him “for his understanding,” she said that she had found they were making the same kind of music again and that she wanted to return to rhythm and blues; Pettibone’s own account of the split is at his note.18 Two of the songs from those sessions survived in other forms. A demo called “Something’s Coming Over Me” went to Dallas Austin, who rebuilt it into “Secret”, and Pettibone kept a writing credit on the finished song.19 “Love Won’t Wait” (1997), which Madonna and Pettibone wrote and abandoned, became a British No. 1 for Gary Barlow in April 1997, and Madonna’s demo of it was released in 2025.20

The producers who replaced him were the people whose records led the R&B charts that year. Babyface was hired because Madonna admired his single “When Can I See You” (1993); he said in 2014 that “she came to me for lush ballads, so that’s where we went.”21 Through Babyface she met Dallas Austin, the Atlanta producer of TLC’s first album, and she worked with him in Atlanta for about a month.22 Dave Hall had been credited on Mary J. Blige’s What’s the 411? and had produced Mariah Carey’s “Dreamlover” (1993), a No. 1 single; no account of how he was hired has been published.23 Nellee Hooper, who had come up in the Bristol collective that became Massive Attack and had produced Björk’s Debut (1993), was flown to Los Angeles with his assistant Marius de Vries. Her biographer Lucy O’Brien wrote that Madonna’s voice was not powerful enough for the deep notes of the R&B she was courting and that she went to the British club scene for another flavor.24 The sessions ran from February to August 1994 in nine studios, among them Axis, The Hit Factory, and Soundworks in New York, Chappell in Los Angeles, Austin’s DARP in Atlanta, and Hooper’s Wild Bunch Studios in London.25

Madonna arrived at the sessions with finished lyrics. The backing singer Donna De Lory said in 2014 that “the minute you walked in the studio, she was giving you the lyric sheet,” and that the harmonies she and Niki Haris sang on “Survival” took a couple of hours and no retakes.26 Babyface wrote “Take a Bow” with her at the studio in his house in Beverly Hills, starting from a beat and a chord sequence, and the track was recorded at the Hit Factory in New York; the live strings were her suggestion and the first he had used on a record. He said in 2023 that she had been nervous “because she just wasn’t used to singing that controlled and being that vulnerable.”27 “Forbidden Love” was the second of the songs they wrote together; a third was not used.28 “I’d Rather Be Your Lover” was recorded with a rap verse by Tupac Shakur, whom Madonna was dating; the released version replaced it with a verse by Meshell Ndegeocello, the bassist and singer who had been among Maverick’s first signings, and the Shakur version remains unreleased.29

The title track, the one song on the album Madonna did not write, began when she told Hooper she admired Debut and he proposed that she and Björk write a song together. While Madonna recorded in the United States and Björk toured Europe, Björk and Hooper wrote the song in London in a day, and the tape was sent to Madonna; Hooper then came to the States, and he and Madonna rebuilt it as what she called “a whole new track that was more like a club record” and restructured the words. The finished song, “Bedtime Story,” is credited to Björk, Hooper, and de Vries, and was recorded at Chappell.30 Björk said at the MTV Video Music Awards in September 1994 that she had written it as a favor to a friend and had never met Madonna, and later that she had used it to write the things she had always wanted to hear Madonna say. Madonna misheard one line of the demo and sang a word that reversed its meaning; Björk let it stand.31

Music

The album is contemporary R&B in the American style of 1994, with one electronic track at its end. Every song is at a slow or medium tempo: the published sheet music gives 80 beats per minute for “Take a Bow,” 84 for “I’d Rather Be Your Lover,” 88 for “Human Nature,” 96 for “Secret,” and 108 for “Bedtime Story,” and two 1994 reviewers made the same observation in their own words, Chris Willman writing that none of the songs rose “too needlessly above mid-tempo” and Jim Farber that “not a single number mounts the fast track.”32 Madonna sings most of the record in her lower register, which J.D. Considine called a “husky alto” on “Secret” and O’Dair described as “downbeat restraint.”33 Pareles wrote that many of the songs have pentatonic melodies, which made them “both concise and seemingly unresolved, with a melancholy undertow”; he exempted “Human Nature.”34 The musicologist Stan Hawkins, in a 1997 analysis, described it as “a slickly produced blend of ’90s hip-hop and crossover dance styles.”35

Sampling, the method of hip hop production, underlies five of the eleven tracks. “I’d Rather Be Your Lover” is built on Lou Donaldson’s 1969 recording of “It’s Your Thing” (1969), a cover of the Isley Brothers’ hit that several hip hop producers had already sampled. “Inside of Me” samples Aaliyah’s “Back & Forth” (1994), released that April, the Gap Band’s “Outstanding” (1982), and a record by Gutter Snypes. “Human Nature” takes its bass line from Main Source’s “What You Need” (1994). “Forbidden Love” samples Grant Green’s recording of “Down Here on the Ground” (1970), and “Sanctuary” samples Herbie Hancock’s “Watermelon Man”.36 Two of the samples are Hall’s, two are Hooper’s, and one is Austin’s; the Babyface production and the Björk song carry none. Live strings, arranged and conducted by Jessie Leavey, Craig Armstrong, and Suzie Katayama, appear on “Secret” and “Take a Bow.”37 Madonna said she had paid particular attention to the sequence so that the record would play as a whole, and the end of “Sanctuary” runs directly into the beginning of “Bedtime Story,” the album’s one deliberate join.38

“Secret” is built on acoustic guitar, a drum loop, live strings, and a wah-wah guitar, in E♭ minor per the sheet music. The lyric is about a lover who shared a secret and about the realization that happiness is in one’s own hands, which Madonna described as a Hindu idea that God lives within everyone.39 “Human Nature” runs on a loop of bass and drums, with the sung lines answered by whispers and a door-slam sample used as percussion. The lyric is her answer to the press over the previous two years, and it refuses to apologize. She said in November 1994 that the song was “my answer to the media, to the press that criticizes me for dealing with subjects that are taboo and tries to punish me for it.”40 “Take a Bow” is a string-led ballad in A♭ major, with a pentatonic string figure and a chime in the arrangement, and Babyface’s backing vocals answer Madonna’s lines closely enough that the song plays almost as a duet; its title line is sung once and never repeated. The lyric is a farewell to a lover whose life was a performance, with an echo of the stage speech from As You Like It.41 “I’d Rather Be Your Lover” sits on a two-chord riff, F♯m7 to B9, at what Hawkins called “a moderately slow, grinding tempo,” with Ndegeocello on bass and an eight-bar rap. The lyric is a negotiation: the singer would rather be the lover than any other relation to the person addressed.42

“Bedtime Story” is the one track in a different genre from the rest of the album: art pop and house, ambient house and deep house in particular, with trance and downtempo elements. It sits on one chord, Gm9, with a drum machine, handclaps, organ stabs, and synthesized strings, and the lyric is about giving up words and reason for unconsciousness.43 Of the remaining songs, one is addressed to the public and the rest to lovers. “Survival,” produced by Hooper from a song Madonna wrote with Austin, is the other lyric Madonna said was aimed at the public, alongside “Human Nature”; Hawkins heard “Survival” parodying nostalgia with cheap-sounding analog synthesizer riffs and a vinyl-scratch sample against the polished production.44 “Don’t Stop” is the album’s one dance track, carried by a bass line under sustained strings. “Inside of Me,” which Madonna dedicated to her mother, sets a saxophone phrase against a smooth ballad. “Forbidden Love” is sparse, with Babyface’s backing vocals and a whispered lead. “Love Tried to Welcome Me” is arranged for strings, acoustic guitar, and wind chimes, and paraphrases George Herbert’s poem “Love (III)” in its chorus, and “Sanctuary” quotes Walt Whitman’s “Vocalism” and, in Farber’s reading, “aligns love and death.”45

Title and artwork

The title came last. Madonna said that after the Björk song was finished she thought Bedtime Stories “a good name for the record, as all songs on the album are bedtime stories, like a tale, a story to tell.”46 The cover photograph is by Patrick Demarchelier, with art direction and design by Baron & Baron, the firm of Fabien Baron, who had designed Sex; the photographs were taken at the Eden Roc hotel in Miami Beach in August 1994.47 A second 1994 session, photographed by Paolo Roversi, went unused on the album and supplied the sleeve of the “Bedtime Story” single and, in 2025, the artwork of the anniversary EP.48

What it inherits and what it introduces

The record’s first inheritance is Madonna’s own. She said in 1994 that her first album had been “more R&B,” and Considine heard the new record as “remarkably close in spirit to the singer’s first album,” emphasizing grooves and melodies over concepts.49 Its second is the R&B of the moment. Austin, Hall, and Babyface were hired from the records that led the 1994 charts: Austin from TLC and Boyz II Men, Hall from Blige and Carey, Babyface from Braxton, Boyz II Men, and Tevin Campbell, and the samples draw on records that hip hop producers had already been using, among them the Isley Brothers by way of Donaldson, the Gap Band, and Hancock.50 The move from pop into this sound had been made before her. Michael Jackson had recorded Dangerous (1991) with Teddy Riley and Janet Jackson janet. (1993) with Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis; Farber judged in 1994 that Madonna had taken to the sound more credibly than Michael Jackson had, and Troy L. Smith wrote in 2016 that those two records had met the moment first and “now it was Madonna’s turn.”51 The third inheritance is British. Hooper brought the sound of his work with Soul II Soul, Björk, and Massive Attack, whose Protection (1994) he produced and released a month before this album; de Vries later described what Hooper had taught him as “a real intuition for how space and untidiness can be a better approach to making music than trying to nail everything down.”52

Three things were new at release, each relative to Madonna’s own records. It was the first of her albums made entirely with producers she had not worked with before, where each of the previous five had been built with Pettibone, Patrick Leonard, or Stephen Bray.53 It was the first on which the restrained lower register that Babyface described carried most of the record, a change the 1994 reviews noticed, and the first on which she gave a whole track to a song written by others; “Bedtime Story” was also her first recording with a British electronic producer, three years before Ray of Light.54 What it introduced to the genre is a matter of critics’ claims. Farber wrote that her early records had anticipated new jack swing’s mixing of sex and romance and that “in a way, she could actually be viewed as new jack swing’s godmother,” and Hawkins wrote in 1997 that critics had received the album as “a significant point of arrival in the artist’s maturity.”55

Release

Maverick and Sire released the album in the United States on October 25, 1994, and in Britain the day before.56 The promotion presented the album as a turn away from sex. A promotional video promised that the album had “no sexual references” and had Madonna announce “a whole new me,” a line Mary von Aue later cited as the label marketing the record as an apology; Madonna played the album to European journalists from a hotel bed in Paris, gave a press conference to fifteen international reporters in Los Angeles on November 19, and was the December cover story of Q.57

Four singles followed. “Secret” was released on September 26, 1994, after Warner Bros. streamed it from its website on September 14. “Take a Bow” followed on December 6; its video, directed by Michael Haussman, was shot in Ronda and Antequera in Spain on November 3 to 8, 1994, cast Madonna as a bullfighter’s neglected lover, and won Best Female Video at the 1995 MTV Video Music Awards. She sent a copy to Alan Parker, who was preparing the film of Evita, as an audition for the title role.58 She and Babyface performed “Take a Bow” together at the American Music Awards in January 1995.59

“Bedtime Story” was released in Europe and Australia on February 13, 1995, and in the United States in April. Its video, directed by Mark Romanek and shot on 35mm film at Universal Studios over six days in December 1994, drew on the surrealist painters Remedios Varo, Leonora Carrington, and Frida Kahlo at Madonna’s direction, cost a reported $5 million, one of the largest budgets on record for a music video, and had a week’s theatrical run in three Cineplex Odeon cinemas from March 10, 1995, before its television premiere; Romanek’s biographies place it in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art.60 Madonna opened the Brit Awards on February 20, 1995, with a Junior Vasquez remix of the song, in a Versace gown between two wind machines; she had invited Björk to join her, and Björk declined.61 “Human Nature” was released in Europe on May 5, 1995, and in the United States on June 6. Its video, directed by Jean-Baptiste Mondino and shot at Raleigh Studios in Hollywood on May 6 and 7, 1995, put her in black latex, chained to a chair by masked men, and played its bondage as comedy.62

A tour to promote the album was discussed after its release and canceled when Madonna was cast as Eva Perón in Evita; she turned down a shorter tour so that she could prepare for the role. “Take a Bow” was not performed in concert until the Rebel Heart Tour in 2016, and “Bedtime Story” was not sung live between the Brit Awards and the Celebration Tour, which opened in London on October 14, 2023; there it was performed atop a cube of projected video that rose from the front of the stage, and ran into “Ray of Light.”63 Rhino reissued the album on vinyl in August 2016. On November 28, 2025, Warner released Bedtime Stories: The Untold Chapter, an eight-track EP of demos and remixes from the sessions edited and mixed by Stuart Price, including Austin’s “Right on Time” (2025) and the Pettibone demo of “Love Won’t Wait,” with the original album on silver vinyl and a two-disc edition; the album returned to the British chart for a week in December 2025.64

Reception

Most of the 1994 reviews were favorable and moderate. The American papers clustered around three and a half stars: O’Dair gave it three and a half of five in Rolling Stone, Edna Gundersen three and a half of four in USA Today, Greg Kot three of four in the Chicago Tribune, and Farber a B+ in Entertainment Weekly, while the British trade papers went higher, with five stars in Music Week and nine of ten in NME. The reservations came from Willman, who gave it two and a half of four in the Los Angeles Times, from Caroline Sullivan in The Guardian, and from Robert Christgau, whose two-star honorable mention was his lowest grade for any of her studio albums to that point; he had given Erotica an A.65

The question the reviews argued over was whether the record was a retreat or a reply. O’Dair framed it as both: “In her retreat from sex to romance, she has enlisted four top R&B producers,” she wrote, and then in her last paragraph that the album says “Fuck off, I’m not done yet,” adding, “You have to listen hard to hear that, though.”66 Pareles, who interviewed Madonna for The New York Times, wrote that she was “trying on a mantle of something like innocence” and put the question to her; she answered that other people thought she had gone as far as she could go, and that she did not.67 Willman called “Human Nature” “one cocky anti-apology” and the album as a whole “the least remarkable of all Madonna’s albums. But it’s not necessarily the least of them.”68 Considine refused the word “comeback,” since she had never been away, and judged her singing the album’s greatest strength.69 Farber found the lyrics her most autobiographical and the two songs aimed at the press “self-righteous and smug.” Kot called the album “Madonna’s most irresistible guilty pleasure,” and Christgau’s capsule was “seductive self-regard over the best tracks fame can buy.”70 At the year’s end Stephen Holden placed it fourth on his list of the year’s ten best in The New York Times, calling it “easily Madonna’s best album,” Larry Flick placed it second in Billboard’s critics’ poll, and the Village Voice poll placed it twenty-sixth.71 The lowest grade on record came a year later, when Rob Sheffield gave it four of ten in the Spin Alternative Record Guide.72

Sales were stronger than the reviews. The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 3 on first-week sales of 145,000, behind the Murder Was the Case soundtrack and Boyz II Men’s II, stayed on the chart for forty-eight weeks, and finished 1995 at No. 28 on the year-end chart; in Britain it entered at No. 2 behind Bon Jovi’s Cross Road.73 The singles divided by year. “Secret” reached No. 3 and “Take a Bow” spent seven weeks at No. 1 from February 25, 1995, her eleventh chart-topper and the longest-running of her career; the song made her the female songwriter with the most No. 1 singles, passing Carole King, while in Britain it stopped at No. 16 and ended a run of thirty-five consecutive top-ten singles that had begun with “Like a Virgin” (1984).74 “Bedtime Story” peaked at No. 42, her first single since “Everybody” (1982) to miss the top 40, and “Human Nature” at No. 46, although both reached the top two of the dance club chart and the top ten in Britain.75 The RIAA certified the album triple platinum in November 2005. At the 38th Grammy Awards on February 28, 1996, it was nominated for Best Pop Album and lost to Joni Mitchell’s Turbulent Indigo.76

Legacy

The album’s reputation rose from 2003. Cinquemani, reviewing it for Slant that March, gave it four stars of five, called it “the creamy balm” after Erotica and Sex, and wrote that Madonna “has never sounded more emotionally vulnerable or more cerebrally plugged in”; Slant placed it sixty-third in its list of the best albums of the 1990s and fourth of her fifteen studio albums in 2026.77 Von Aue, writing for Vice on the twentieth anniversary in 2014, called it her most important album and read “Human Nature” as “a lyrical #sorrynotsorry” against the apology the public had demanded. Billboard’s staff ranked it sixth of her thirteen albums in 2015 and wrote that it “certainly wasn’t a mea culpa.” Gay Times ranked it fifth of fourteen in 2019 and called it “the most criminally underrated,” and Spin ranked it seventh of sixteen in 2024 as “a brief but effective detour.”78 The dissent within the reassessment is Owen Pallett’s 2017 review for Pitchfork, which scored it 6.5 and called it “a curious non-event” in her career, keeping “Human Nature” and “Take a Bow” and asking that the rest be forgotten; the Evening Standard ranked it ninth of fourteen in 2023.79 Stephen Thomas Erlewine’s AllMusic review gives it four stars of five and calls it “her most humane and open music.”80 It is absent from Rolling Stone’s 2020 list of the 500 greatest albums, which includes three other Madonna records.81

Sales since release are documented in two figures. Nielsen SoundScan had counted 2,336,000 copies in the United States by December 2016, more than Erotica, which had opened higher, and press estimates of worldwide sales run from seven to eight million.82 In April 2020 a fan campaign under the hashtag #JusticeForBedtimeStories took the album to No. 1 on the American iTunes chart, on sales of a little over a thousand physical copies in the week; Madonna thanked the fans publicly.83

Its documented influence runs in two directions. The British thread is the better attested. Hooper produced Madonna’s recording of Marvin Gaye’s “I Want You” (1995) with Massive Attack the following year, de Vries co-produced three tracks on Ray of Light, and critics from Cinquemani in 2003 to Peter Piatkowski in 2022 have dated the electronic direction of that album to “Bedtime Story.”84 Björk reworked her original demo as “Sweet Intuition” (1995), a B-side of “Army of Me.”85 The one musician who has named the album as an influence in the first person is Sophie, who told The Observer in 2018 that the production of the Bedtime Stories era was “so much more fully formed and sexy than a lot of the trip-hop stuff that was coming out around that time” and had influenced her own music.86 Claims for the album’s R&B side are critics’ hearings: Andrew Unterberger wrote in Billboard in 2023 that Rihanna had borrowed the skeleton of “Take a Bow” for her own breakup ballad of 2008, and Daniel Megarry wrote in Gay Times in 2019 that the album’s R&B sound had come to seem more current than any other early Madonna record.87
