Bedtime Stories

Madonna
ReleasedOctober 25, 1994 (US); October 24, 1994 (UK)
LabelMaverick / Sire
RecordedFebruary – August 1994
ProducerMadonna, Babyface, Dallas Austin, Dave Hall, Nellee Hooper
StudioAxis, The Hit Factory, Soundworks (New York); Chappell (Los Angeles); DARP (Atlanta); Wild Bunch (London), among nine
Genres
Primary
Contemporary R&B
ListenRYM·Apple Music·Spotify

On March 31, 1994, a segment producer named Daniel Kellison put out his hand in a dressing room at the Ed Sullivan Theater, and the guest did not take it. What she said instead was a one-word obscenity, repeated slowly, “like a petulant 8-year-old child challenging a parent.”1 The bit Kellison had arranged with her by phone was tame: she would scold David Letterman for his jokes about her. What aired was the most censored late-night broadcast in American television history. She handed the host her underwear and asked him to smell it, smoked a cigar, said the word fourteen times, and would not leave; the executive producer told Kellison, “Get rid of her,” and he led her off by the hand under cover of the band.2 Ken Tucker wrote in Entertainment Weekly that for the first time in her career “the primary emotion she inspired was pity,” and that the crowd “was cheering for Dave to kick hers.”3 The record she released seven months later is sung almost entirely in her lower register, over slow beats, by a woman who had just been booed off a stage, and the first question every review asked was whether that was an apology.

The bench

The first sessions were with Shep Pettibone, and she ended them. Asked that November why the liner notes thanked him “for his understanding,” she answered: “Exactly. I started working on this record with Shep. But I found that we were making the same kind of music again. I was more interested in going back to my roots, which are rhythm & blues.”4 One of his demos survived the change of direction. A track called “Something’s Coming Over Me” went to Dallas Austin, who rebuilt it into “Secret” and earned a writing credit beside her.5 The replacements were the people running the R&B charts that year. Babyface got the call because she liked one of his own singles: “Madonna was a fan of a song I did, ‘When Can I See You.’ Because of that, she was interested in working with me. She came to me for lush ballads, so that’s where we went.”6 Austin arrived through Babyface and took her to Atlanta for about a month. Dave Hall came off Mary J. Blige’s first record. Nellee Hooper, the Bristol producer behind Björk’s Debut, was flown to Los Angeles with his assistant Marius de Vries for what her biographer’s account calls a “very European sensibility.”7 Her own brief for the record was a hybrid: “The idea was to juxtapose my singing style with a hardcore hip-hop sensibility and have the finished product still sound like a Madonna record.”8

Lyric sheets

The sessions were fast because she arrived finished. “The minute you walked in the studio, she was giving you the lyric sheet,” the backing singer Donna De Lory said; the atmosphere was work, not hanging out. The harmonies on “Survival” took a couple of hours and no retakes.9 “Take a Bow” began in his house: “It was just a beat and the chords. From there we collaborated and built it up. I was living in Beverly Hills and I created a little studio in my house, so she came over there to write.”10 The live strings were her idea and the first he had used; what he remembers about her performance is the register it required. “She was nervous because she just wasn’t used to singing that controlled and being that vulnerable.”11 That is the vocal the whole record runs on. Her own account of it was a mood: she had been “in an incredibly reflective state of mind,” she told Q, had “done a lot of soul-searching,” and wrote what that produced.12

The answer

Two songs are not romantic, and she said so: “The other songs could be about anybody, but in these two it’s quite obvious that I’m addressing the public. And they’re basically saying the same thing: Hey, get offa my back; don’t hang all of your hang-ups on me.”13 “Human Nature”, written and produced with Hall over a loop from Main Source’s “What You Need,” is the one that names its target. To a room of international reporters that November she called it “my answer to the media, to the press that criticizes me for dealing with taboo subjects and tries to punish me for it. I’m saying in the song that I turn my back on them. I’m not going to apologize.”14 The lyric’s stance is a shrug performed as a whisper, and the critics of 1994 heard exactly what she meant: the Los Angeles Times called it “one cocky anti-apology” and “her post-Letterman F-you to critics,” and Rolling Stone heard “a drive-by on her critics, complete with a keening synth line straight outta Dre.”15 Jean-Baptiste Mondino’s video, shot the following spring, put her in black latex with leather braided into her hair, chained to a chair by masked men, the whole apparatus of bondage played as a joke.16 The Guardian’s Jude Rogers, ranking every single she had released, put it fifteenth of seventy-eight: “Madonna’s best excursion into the sounds of hip-hop and R&B.”17

The London demo

The title came last and from outside. Madonna had told Hooper she loved Debut; he said the admiration was mutual and proposed a song. “I was working on my album in the US, while she was on tour in Europe. Nellee and Bjork, however, managed to find the time to write the basic idea. In only one day she wrote the lyrics and he put together some music.” The tape came from London; Hooper then flew over, “and we made a whole new track that was more like a club record. We also re-structured the words. After that song was finished, I thought Bedtime Stories to be a good name for the record, as all songs on the album are bedtime stories, like a tale, a story to tell.”18 The result, “Bedtime Story,” is the one track on the album she did not write. She sang a Junior Vasquez remix of it to open the Brit Awards on February 20, 1995, between two wind machines that turned, in Mary Gabriel’s description, “her waist-length blond extensions into flames, and her diaphanous Versace gown into a parachute”; it was the song’s only live performance for nearly thirty years.19 Björk, invited to join her, declined.20

What it inherits and what it introduces

The sound it borrows is the new jack swing then running Black radio, and the borrowing is literal in one place: Aaliyah’s “Back & Forth,” months old, sits sampled inside “Inside of Me.” The method it borrows is hip hop’s. Lou Donaldson’s cover of the Isley Brothers’ “It’s Your Thing” runs under “I’d Rather Be Your Lover,” and Herbie Hancock’s “Watermelon Man” under “Sanctuary.”21 Jim Farber, reviewing it for Entertainment Weekly, granted her the lineage — “in a way, she could actually be viewed as new jack swing’s godmother” — and judged the move more credible than Michael Jackson’s parallel one on Dangerous.22 It was not a move she made first. Janet Jackson had already taken a pop career into this sound, and Austin’s and Hall’s own clients, TLC and Blige, were the sound itself; the 1994 reviews read the record as catching up. What was hers was the second import, a British club producer and his assistant, and the line that runs from them through the Massive Attack single of 1995 to Ray of Light is Madonna’s to tell.23

Retreat or answer

The question the 1994 reviews asked was whether the record was a retreat, and the two best of them answered it both ways at once. Barbara O’Dair, in Rolling Stone, gave it three and a half stars and coined the frame: “In her retreat from sex to romance, she has enlisted four top R&B producers,” and “Madonna still wants to go to bed, but this time it’s to pull the covers over her head.” Then her last paragraph reversed it: “Twirled in a gauze of (unrequited) love songs, Bedtime Stories says, ‘Fuck off, I’m not done yet.’ You have to listen hard to hear that, though.”24 Jon Pareles, in the New York Times, heard a woman who “became vulgar instead of shocking” now “trying on a mantle of something like innocence,” and put the question to her directly. Her answer declined its premise: “I think everybody else thinks I went as far as I could go, but we won’t get into what everybody else thinks.”25 The rest of the press did other work. Farber’s B+ took the lyrics as her most autobiographical and the media songs as “self-righteous and smug.”26 Chris Willman, at two and a half stars out of four, registered indifference: “the least remarkable of all Madonna’s albums. But it’s not necessarily the least of them.”27 Robert Christgau gave it two stars, an honorable mention, and a nine-word capsule: “seductive self-regard over the best tracks fame can buy.” Two years earlier he had graded Erotica an A.28

The numbers were steadier than the notices. The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 3 with 145,000 copies, behind Snoop Doggy Dogg and Boyz II Men, and stayed on the chart forty-eight weeks; in Britain it reached No. 2.29 Its long tail outsold Erotica, which had opened higher: by 2016 SoundScan counted 2,336,000 American copies against the earlier record’s 1.91 million, and the RIAA certified it triple platinum in 2005.30 The singles split along the record’s own seam. The two ballads went top three, “Secret” at No. 3 and “Take a Bow” at No. 1 for seven weeks. The two tracks this note calls forward-looking stalled: “Bedtime Story” at No. 42, “Human Nature” at No. 46. The album was nominated for the Grammy for Best Pop Album.31

Influence and legacy

The rehabilitation came from writers who took the record’s refusal at face value. Sal Cinquemani, reviewing it for Slant in 2003, called it “the creamy balm” after Erotica and Sex, “a fluffy-pillowed concept album that unfolds like a musical fairy tale,” on which she “seemed more interested in literature and human psychology than sexual biology.” His closing verdict: “Madonna has never sounded more emotionally vulnerable or more cerebrally plugged in than she does on Bedtime Stories.”32 Mary von Aue, for Vice in 2014, read the stance as a hashtag before the fact: “Instead, she offered a lyrical #sorrynotsorry and a response to the problem of female musicians being scrutinized for their sexuality rather than their music.”33 Owen Pallett, in Pitchfork, called it “the first Madonna album that felt like a non-event, an asterisk to her omnipresence, another hot day in a heat wave,” would keep two songs, and asked that “Take a Bow” be played at his funeral “and let the rest of this sleepy album be forgotten.”34 The influence claim that has held is about the Hooper sound. SOPHIE, in 2018, called this era’s production “so much more fully formed and sexy than a lot of the trip-hop stuff that was coming out around that time,” and an influence on her own.35

The reconciliation with Letterman came first. That September, the month “Secret” went to radio, he walked her onstage at the MTV Video Music Awards, kissed her hand, and left her with one line — “I’ll be in the car. Watch your language” — to a reaction his head writer remembers as the measure of how large the episode had grown.36 The album settled nothing with the press. O’Dair heard the answer in it and said so, and said you had to listen hard; most did not, and the record that talked back was shelved as the one that said sorry.
