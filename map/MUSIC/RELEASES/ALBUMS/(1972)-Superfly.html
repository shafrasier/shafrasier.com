In 1971, backstage at Lincoln Center, a screenwriter named Phillip Fenty and a producer named Sig Shore handed Curtis Mayfield the script for a film called Super Fly, knowing his name would help them raise the movie’s budget. The film was made for under $300,000, with Shore as the largest backer alongside a Harlem businessman named Nate Adams, two Black dentists, and about five thousand dollars from the director’s father, Gordon Parks Sr.1 Mayfield started writing on the flight home to Chicago. Then he saw the rushes. The script had not conveyed how much cocaine would actually be on screen, and his reaction became the record’s founding sentence: “This is a cocaine infomercial.”2 What he built in response was a counterweight in song form — “I did the music and lyrics to be a commentary,” he said, “as though someone was speaking as the movie was going” — and Curtom put the commentary in stores three and a half weeks before the film opened, so audiences walked into the theater already knowing the songs.3 Inside the movie, a character puts “Freddie’s Dead” on a turntable: the record playing inside the film it argues with.4

Musical and production context

The songs came out of a basement. Mayfield wrote most of the album in the bottom apartment of a three-story Chicago building he owned, during a trial separation from his wife. “Freddie’s Dead” took about five minutes at his Fender Rhodes, and “Little Child Runnin’ Wild” was a three-year-old song called “Ghetto Child,” retitled. He synchronized the material to the picture by watching rushes at home on a Sony videocassette machine.5 The recording split in two. In December 1971 Gordon Parks Jr. needed a finished song to shoot Mayfield’s nightclub cameo, so the band cut “Pusherman” overnight at Bell Sound in New York and mimed it on camera the next day.6 Months later, after an interval Mayfield spent touring Europe and writing an album for the Impressions, the rest of the record went down in three days in Chicago, everything except the vocals cut live with as many as forty players on the floor. Engineer Roger Anfinsen: “It seemed about capturing a certain electricity, a live energy.” Guitarist Craig McMullen liked the method for a workman’s reason: with the full orchestra in the room, “when you place your licks, you don’t have to worry about your licks bumping.”7 Mayfield’s vocals were overdubbed afterward — the falsetto floating over a band that had already finished playing.8

The band was his touring core plus Chicago’s session bench: McMullen and Phil Upchurch on guitars beside Mayfield’s own, Joseph “Lucky” Scott on bass, “Master” Henry Gibson on percussion, and the drum chair split by the two-session structure: road drummer Tyrone McCullen on “Pusherman,” studio ace Morris Jennings on everything else.9 On tape that section is the album’s engine, the sound AllMusic’s John Bush later compressed into “deep, dark grooves, trademarked wah-wah guitar, and stinging brass.”10 Over them sat Johnny Pate’s charts. Pate, Mayfield’s arranger since the Impressions years, was working an A&R desk at MGM when Mayfield called to say he couldn’t do it without him. Mayfield supplied cassettes of guitar licks and vocal ideas, and Pate orchestrated them into arrangements of harps, oboes, strings, horns, bells, and flutes. The raw material, Pate said, had “very few chord changes, very few melodic lines,” with Mayfield “almost rapping through these things.”11 The jacket credited the music as arranged and orchestrated “from the Original Dictations of Curtis Mayfield,” wording that galled Pate — and when Mayfield refused him a co-writing credit on the album’s two instrumentals, Pate went to the press, and the two never worked together again.12 The record broke a second partnership on its way out the door: after Superfly, Eddie Thomas, the “Tom” in Curtom and Mayfield’s friend since the late fifties, sold his shares in the label and left.13

What it inherits and what it introduces

The record stands on two inheritances. The first is Mayfield’s own line: a decade of gospel-rooted message songs with the Impressions, then the darker turn of Curtis (1970) and Roots (1971), which had already stretched Chicago soul into the long-form, wah-wah-and-congas funk this record perfected.14 The second came from a year earlier: Isaac Hayes’ Shaft (1971) had built the template of the Black soul auteur scoring Black cinema and taken the Best Original Song Oscar, the award Superfly would be ruled ineligible for.15 Mayfield worried a third model had already exhausted the ground. “I was very influenced by Marvin [Gaye’s] What’s Going On album,” he said of Marvin Gaye; “I thought Marvin had already covered all the social commentary and soul music bases.”16 But the film gave him a subject Gaye’s album didn’t have: a camera treating a cocaine dealer as a hero. What the record introduces follows from that collision — the counter-narrative soundtrack, a score whose songs contest the film they accompany, released ahead of the picture so the argument reached the audience first. It is the move every later soundtrack-as-commentary descends from, and the record’s other proof, that the album could out-earn its movie, built the market the next decade’s soul soundtracks chased.17

The commentary

The track titles read as a cast list: the pusher, the corpse, the running child, the man with no thing on him. The NAACP’s Junius Griffin was attacking the whole movie wave for glorifying pushers and pimps, and Mayfield’s answer was character study without contempt.16 “I didn’t put Priest down,” he said of the film’s dealer-protagonist. “He was just trying to get out. His deeds weren’t noble ones, but he was making money and he had intelligence. And he did survive. I mean all this was reality.”18 The songs hold that double vision: “No Thing on Me (Cocaine Song)” states the refusal in its own title, and his widow Altheida later compressed the whole project into a sentence — Curtis “wanted to turn that around. That was his main purpose there, to say ‘This is nothing pretty.‘”19

Reception

The album was certified gold on September 7, 1972, inside two months of release, and the week of October 21 it began four straight weeks at number one on the Billboard 200 — the gamble of releasing the message before the movie, paid in full.20 It was the only pop chart-topper of Mayfield’s career. “Freddie’s Dead” reached number four on the Hot 100 and number two on the soul chart, and the title single “Superfly” went eight and five. Each sold past a million copies and went gold.21 By the production’s own accounting the soundtrack returned about five million dollars in profit against roughly four million for the film — the concrete form of the legend that the record out-earned the movie.22 Rolling Stone’s Bob Donat called it “not only a superior, imaginative soundtrack, but fine funky music as well”; Robert Christgau graded it A-, hearing songs that “speak for (and to) the ghetto’s victims rather than its achievers… transmitting bleak lyrics through uncompromisingly vivacious music.”23 The awards were stingier: four Grammy nominations, no wins, and “Freddie’s Dead” was ruled ineligible for the Best Original Song Oscar because the film used it only as an instrumental. Mayfield shrugged in character: “I’m from R&B music, so I’d rather lose an Oscar than to lose in the streets.”24

Influence and legacy

Superfly is the record the industry imitated: within a year came Bobby Womack’s Across 110th Street, James Brown’s Black Caesar, Willie Hutch’s The Mack, and Marvin Gaye’s Trouble Man — a whole economy of soul stars scoring Black cinema.25 The critics’ ledger has kept it at the top of that wave: Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums has carried it in the top eighty of all three editions (#69 in 2003, #72 in 2012, #76 in 2020), NME ranked it the thirteenth-best film soundtrack ever made, and the Library of Congress added it to the National Recording Registry in the class of 2018.26 Its afterlife runs through Sampling: the title track turns up in Beastie Boys’ “Egg Man,” The Notorious B.I.G.’s “Intro,” and OutKast’s “Return of the ‘G’,” and Ice-T rebuilt “Pusherman” as “I’m Your Pusher” in 1988, then joined Mayfield himself for “Superfly 1990.”27 The through-line from this record to conscious hip hop is part of a larger argument The song in film makes: commissioned songs can outlive the movie they were written to serve.28
