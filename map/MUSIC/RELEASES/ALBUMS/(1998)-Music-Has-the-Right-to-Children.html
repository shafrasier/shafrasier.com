Music Has the Right to Children

Boards of Canada
ReleasedApril 20, 1998 (US: September 22, 1998)
LabelWarp / Skam (US: Matador)

ProducerMichael Sandison and Marcus Eoin
StudioHexagon Sun, Pentland Hills, Scotland

The debut album Warp and Skam released jointly in April 1998 was out of step with its own moment on purpose. Electronic music’s cutting edge was a blur of chopped breakbeats and digital gloss; the Sandison brothers answered with analog synthesizers that sway off-pitch like tape running slow, over hip-hop beats that move like sleepwalkers. Children’s voices drift through the songs as if from a classroom film left running in another room. “Part of it was an idea to create new music that really felt like an old familiar thing,” Marcus Eoin said of the project’s beginnings, and the album delivers exactly that paradox: sixty-three minutes of brand-new music that arrives pre-remembered.1 Michael Sandison’s account of the method explains the sound’s strange incompleteness: “I always felt rhythm is the part of music that dates it,” he said. “We have a subtractive way of putting our music together,” and this “pushed the whole sound into a kind of apparition of the music that was not quite all there.”2

The making

The press called the studio a bunker; the brothers waved that off as “just an exaggeration on the part of the record label.”3 Hexagon Sun, assembled in the Pentland Hills outside Edinburgh and named for the artistic collective they belonged to, is where the album took shape between 1995 and 1997, on samplers, deliberately detuned vintage synthesizers, drum machines, and reel-to-reel tape.4 The project was much older than the sessions. The brothers had been layering synthesizers over shortwave-radio cassettes since 1981 and self-releasing on their own Music70 imprint since 1987, and some of this album circulated early: “Smokes Quantity” dates to 1995, and “Turquoise Hexagon Sun,” the track that carries the collective’s name, had already appeared on a Skam EP at the end of 1996.5 The polish Warp’s money could have bought was declined; the point was the patina.

The voices in it

Fans have traced the album’s borrowed voices to their sources with the devotion the record invites. The small voice working up to “I love you” on “The Color of the Fire” comes from a Sesame Street segment, and the same broadcast feeds a moment of “An Eagle in Your Mind” — whose narrator, meanwhile, is lifted from a 1983 otter documentary, tracking the animal through its “holts” in the even voice of broadcast nature film.6 On “Aquarius” the layers pile up: a Sesame Street voice reciting “orange” and stray numbers, children’s laughter and a delighted “yeah, that’s right,” all riding a slap-bass line sampled from a 1979 recording of “Aquarius” from the musical Hair.7

Some voices wandered in on their own. The birdsong threading “Rue the Whirl” came through a studio window left open during a take, kept when the playback proved the accident belonged; the bird cry on “Happy Cycling” is borrowed again, a red-legged seriema sampled from a Vangelis documentary score.8 And “One Very Important Thought”, the spoken-word minute that closed the original UK edition, is not a sample at all: the brothers re-recorded the closing anti-censorship message of an obscure 1982 film, altering the text so the warning defends the listener’s right to hear Boards of Canada — a found object forged to fit.9 Even the track titles sample the world: “Kaini Industries” takes its name from a Blood Reserve employment company, and “Pete Standing Alone” from the Blackfoot man who appears in National Film Board of Canada documentaries the brothers watched in school, the institution their own name salutes.10

What it inherits and what it introduces

The inheritance is television. Sandison claimed in 1998 that the theme tunes and soundtracks of vintage TV were “a stronger influence than modern music, or any other music that we listened to back then”: the National Film Board’s synthesizer logos, the sound of institutions addressing children.11 The nearest ancestor on record is My Life in the Bush of Ghosts, with its found voices as lead singers; the comparison is Simon Reynolds’s, with the difference drawn precisely — where that record worked “an exoticism of geography and cultural distance,” this one works “an exoticism of time.”12 Its faded, half-remembered textures became the founding grammar of hauntology, the genre that assembled around Ghost Box six years later. Critics still argue whether Boards of Canada stand at the genre’s head or just before it; this album is the exhibit both sides cite.13 Against the era’s drum-first current the album made a quieter argument for melody, long lines like “Roygbiv’s” unspooling over bars the way little else around it did.14

Reception

The British press heard it immediately. Days before release, the Manchester Evening News called it “the best album you’ll hear this year” and “a landmark in the evolution of emotive electronica.” NME scored it 8 out of 10, and in the Daily Telegraph Alexis Petridis found it “packed with warm, human, even witty material.”15 It reached only No. 193 on the UK Albums Chart, which turned out to mean nothing.16

The canonization ran on its own clock. Pitchfork’s Mark Richardson gave the 2004 reissue a perfect 10.0, and the site’s rankings moved it from No. 35 among the nineties’ best albums in 2003 to No. 26 in 2022. “Decades on,” Jesse Dorris wrote there, “Music remains mystical and deeply human.”17 Its all-time IDM list placed the album second only to Selected Ambient Works 85–92, and AllMusic’s verdict became the standard line: “a landmark for electronic listening music that was widely copied.”18 Fact’s fifteenth-anniversary piece rolled a generation of students, from Four Tet to Leyland Kirby, and Radiohead referenced the duo repeatedly around Kid A.19 The album went Gold in Britain, and nearly three decades on it was still riding the UK Dance Albums chart — a 1998 record about half-remembered childhoods, selling to listeners who grew up remembering it.20 The title, the brothers said, was “a statement of our intention to affect the audience using sound.” Reynolds heard the second reading: the album has been generative — the music had the right to children, and it had them.21
