The voice on “Live to Tell” is a demo. Madonna wrote the lyric sitting on Patrick Leonard’s floor with a pad, sang it once over an eight-track in his house, and Leonard kept that take through to the finished master: “It was so innocent and so shy. It’s as naive, as raw as can be, and that’s part of what gave [‘Live to Tell’] all its charm.”1 Then she told Warner the ballad would go out first, months ahead of the album. Leonard remembered the reaction: “Believe me that was one scared record company! She said, ‘It’s the first single’ and they said, ‘It’s over, you’re doomed, you’re putting out a seven-minute single that stops three times.‘”2 The record it announced was the first she co-wrote and co-produced end to end, and nearly everything on it was decided the same way — her material, her call.3

One a day

The sessions ran from December 1985 to April 1986 at Channel Recording Studios in Los Angeles, with the mix at Master Control in Burbank, and she staffed them by splitting the job in two.4 Patrick Leonard had been music director of the Virgin Tour, a chair he took without enthusiasm — “Going out on a stage and playing Like A Virgin wasn’t my idea of a thrill. And it still isn’t” — and stayed to become her main writing partner for the next decade.5 Stephen Bray, her oldest collaborator, described his job as shaping arrangements to show the songs off.6 She worked with each separately; all three convened only for “Where’s the Party.”7 Leonard’s account of the routine explains the record’s pace: “I would put something together, usually just on piano, and then she would come in at about 11, we’d mess around with whatever needed to be messed around with, she’d write a lyric, she’d sing it, and the next day we would do another song, one a day.”8 The singing was as fast as the writing: “As legend has it, most of those vocals are the only time Madonna sang [the songs in the studio] and I can attest to that.”9

The outside song

“Papa Don’t Preach” came through the window. Brian Elliot wrote it after overhearing girls from North Hollywood High, who used his studio’s mirrored front window to fix their hair, and he called the result a “love song, maybe framed a little bit differently.”10 The demo was cut for an aspiring singer named Christina Dent; Michael Ostin, the Warner A&R man who had carried “Like a Virgin” to Madonna two years earlier, played it for her mid-album.11 She added lines to it, asked Elliot in the studio whether she had “wrecked his song,” and explained the attraction plainly: “[The song] just fit right in with my own personal zeitgeist of standing up to male authorities, whether it’s the pope, or the Catholic Church or my father and his conservative, patriarchal ways.”12 She also knew exactly what was coming: “They’re going to say I am advising every young girl to go out and get pregnant.”13

They did, from both directions at once. Planned Parenthood’s Alfred Moran urged broadcasters to reconsider airing the song, arguing it portrayed pregnancy as fashionable; the feminist attorney Gloria Allred demanded Madonna state publicly that other choices existed — “Pop songs have an enormous impact on kids today” — and no such statement came.14 Meanwhile Tipper Gore, whose committee had listed “Dress You Up” the year before, crossed the aisle to call this one “an important song, and a good one, which discusses, with urgency, a real predicament which thousands of unwed teenagers face in our country.”15 Elliot shrugged at the whole spectacle: “If Madonna has influenced young girls to keep their babies, I don’t think that’s such a bad deal.” Her publicist held the line that she was “singing a song, not taking a stand.”16 James Foley shot the video in three days on Staten Island with Danny Aiello as the father. Aiello later cut an answer record, “Papa Wants the Best for You,” that Stereogum’s Tom Breihan called “ridiculous, terrible, extremely funny.”17 One studio detail outlived the storm: Fred Zarr improvised the song’s string-style introduction on an Emulator II and never received formal credit for it.18

The reject pile

Much of the album was salvage. “Open Your Heart” arrived as “Follow Your Heart,” a rock song by Gardner Cole and Peter Rafelson intended for Cyndi Lauper, who never heard it; Madonna and Leonard rebuilt it around a new bassline and she rewrote enough lyric to earn the credit.19 “La Isla Bonita” began as an instrumental Leonard and Bruce Gaitsch had pitched to Michael Jackson and Quincy Jones for Bad; they passed, and Madonna wrote the words in Hong Kong while filming Shanghai Surprise, calling the finished song a tribute to the “beauty and mystery” of Latin American people.20 “Live to Tell” itself was a reject: Leonard had written the instrumental for a different film, Paramount’s Fire with Fire, and when the studio turned it down Madonna rerouted it to At Close Range, the crime drama starring her new husband Sean Penn.21 The title track was the one pure original: a doo-wop love song named for one of Penn’s favorite expressions and standing, she said, for “a very pure vision of love.”22 The liner notes dedicate the album to him, “the coolest guy in the universe”; by 2015 she was laughing at the song — “I didn’t know what I was talking about when I wrote [that song]” — and Bray’s reading was simpler still: “if she’s in love she’ll write love songs. If she’s not in love she definitely won’t be writing love songs.”23

What it inherits and what it introduces

The title song and “Jimmy Jimmy” reach back past disco entirely, working the Brill Building-era girl-group songbook — the old I–vi–IV–V changes and stacked harmonies of “Chapel of Love” and the Shirelles.24 The new thing on the record is the method: the Leonard partnership and the write-it-in-a-day, sing-it-once discipline became the engine of her records for the next decade. One track left a longer trail — AllMusic’s Stewart Mason heard “La Isla Bonita” anticipating the Latin-influenced dance-pop of the late eighties, while the scholar Eduardo Viñuela read the same song as “deterritorialized clichés” assembled into a “fantasy” for American ears.25

The year of True Blue

Released June 30, 1986, the album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 29 and reached No. 1 in its fifth week, holding for five consecutive weeks of an eighty-two-week run.26 In Britain it did something no American act’s album had done before: it entered the chart at No. 1, stayed six weeks, and finished as the UK’s best-selling album of 1986 at over two million copies.27 The singles kept the record on the radio for a full year. “Live to Tell” hit No. 1 that June, and “Papa Don’t Preach” followed on August 16 (her birthday) for two weeks. “True Blue” stopped at No. 3 at home but went to No. 1 in Britain. “Open Your Heart” made it three American chart-toppers from one album on February 7, 1987, and “La Isla Bonita” closed the campaign at No. 4 while becoming Europe’s biggest single of 1987.28 The album was eventually certified seven-times platinum in America, and the worldwide figure — an estimate, not an audit — passed twenty-five million, with the era’s shorthand claiming No. 1 finishes in twenty-eight countries.29

Rolling Stone’s Davitt Sigerson called the album “sturdy, dependable, lovable” and her singing “better than ever.” The Los Angeles Times’s Robert Hilburn heard a voice “so finely tailored that she actually extends the punch and appeal of the production touches”; Robert Christgau graded it a B.30 The retrospective verdicts run higher. AllMusic’s Stephen Thomas Erlewine placed it as “one of the great dance-pop albums,” praising its “wide reach, accomplishment, and sheer sense of fun.” The BBC’s Mark Savage credited it with cementing her as the “first lady of pop”; Slant’s Sal Cinquemani named it the “supreme archetype for late ’80s and early ’90s pop music.”31

Influence and legacy

The videos made arguments of their own. For “Open Your Heart”, Jean-Baptiste Mondino built a peep show on a set in Echo Park and put Madonna on its rotating stage, with the child actor Felix Howard as the boy trying to get in; Hit Video USA refused to air it, MTV reportedly asked for alterations, and its defenders pointed out what the staging actually does — she looks down at paying viewers rendered “leering and pathetic,” a peep show in which only the dancer has the gaze.32 The “True Blue” clip Americans saw was made by the audience itself: Sire and MTV ran a “Make My Video” contest and screened three thousand fan entries in an all-day marathon on October 30, 1986 (“Blue Thursday”). The winners, Angel Gracia and Cliff Guest, took home a $25,000 check and a directing contract at Geffen for their sepia-toned teenage romance.33 The sleeve became an artifact in its own right: Herb Ritts shot the profile in black and white, Warner’s Jeri Heiden hand-tinted it blue, and the biographer Lucy O’Brien later called the image “our first glimpse of Madonna as a classic icon”; Billboard ranked it among the hundred greatest album covers in 2023.34 The last word belongs to the ledger: five years after the label heard “a seven-minute single that stops three times,” the Guinness Book of World Records listed True Blue as the best-selling album of all time by a female artist.35
