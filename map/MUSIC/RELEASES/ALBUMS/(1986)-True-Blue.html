True Blue is the third studio album by Madonna, released on June 30, 1986, by Sire. It was the first album she co-wrote and co-produced from end to end, working with Patrick Leonard and Stephen Bray separately rather than together, at Channel Recording Studios in Los Angeles between December 1985 and April 1986. Eight of its nine songs are hers; the exception, “Papa Don’t Preach”, came from a writer she had never met. The album is dance-pop whose distinguishing habit is reaching outside itself, to Motown and girl-group changes, to strings, to Latin percussion and flamenco guitar, and to one torch ballad.

It was the year’s biggest record almost everywhere. True Blue entered the Billboard 200 at No. 29 and reached No. 1 in its fifth week, holding five weeks of an eighty-two-week run; in Britain it entered at No. 1, held six weeks, and finished as the country’s best-selling album of 1986. Its five singles kept her on the radio for a full year, three of them reaching No. 1 in America and two in Britain. The 1986 reviews were warmer than anything she had received before, and the retrospective ones warmer still, with AllMusic calling it one of the great dance-pop albums.

Background

The record that preceded it, Like a Virgin, had been made under a compromise: Warner Bros. refused her creative control and gave her a producer instead. By 1986 she had the control, and True Blue is what she did with it. Leonard had been music director of the Virgin Tour, a chair he took without enthusiasm, saying that going out and playing “Like a Virgin” was not his idea of a thrill and still was not, and he stayed to become her main writing partner for the next decade.1 Bray, her oldest collaborator, described his own job as shaping arrangements to show the songs off.2

She had married Sean Penn in August 1985, and the album is named for one of his expressions.3 What she was releasing into was a pop chart she had helped make: dance-pop with synthesizers and drum machines was now the mainstream sound rather than a club import, and the argument she had won with the previous record was no longer available to win again.

Recording

The sessions ran from December 1985 to April 1986 at Channel Recording Studios in Los Angeles, with the mix at Master Control in Burbank, and she staffed them by splitting the job in two, working with Leonard and Bray separately; all three convened only for “Where’s the Party.”4 Leonard’s account of the routine explains the record’s pace: he would put something together on piano, she would arrive around eleven, they would work on whatever needed working on, she would write a lyric and sing it, and the next day they would do another song, one a day.5 The singing was as fast as the writing, and by Leonard’s testimony most of those vocals are the only time she sang the songs in the studio.6

The demo that became the single

The voice on “Live to Tell” is a demo. Madonna wrote the lyric sitting on Leonard’s floor with a pad, sang it once over an eight-track in his house, and Leonard kept that take through to the finished master, saying it was innocent and shy and as raw as could be, and that this was part of its charm.7 She then told Warner the ballad would be the first single, months ahead of the album. Leonard remembered a frightened record company telling her she was doomed, that she was putting out a seven-minute single that stops three times.8 It went to No. 1.

Much of the rest of the album was salvage. “Open Your Heart” arrived as “Follow Your Heart,” a rock song by Gardner Cole and Peter Rafelson intended for Cyndi Lauper, who never heard it; Madonna and Leonard rebuilt it around a new bass line and she rewrote enough of the lyric to earn a credit.9 “La Isla Bonita” began as an instrumental Leonard and Bruce Gaitsch had pitched to Michael Jackson and Quincy Jones for Bad; they passed, and Madonna wrote the words in Hong Kong while filming Shanghai Surprise.10 “Live to Tell” was itself a reject, written by Leonard as an instrumental for a film Paramount turned down and rerouted by Madonna to At Close Range, the crime drama starring Penn.11

“Papa Don’t Preach” came through a window. Brian Elliot wrote it after overhearing girls from North Hollywood High who used his studio’s mirrored front window to fix their hair, and called the result a love song framed a little differently. The demo was cut for an aspiring singer named Christina Dent, and Michael Ostin, the Warner A&R man who had carried “Like a Virgin” to her two years earlier, played it for her mid-album.12 She added lines, asked Elliot in the studio whether she had wrecked his song, and explained the attraction as fitting her own habit of standing up to male authority, whether the pope, the church or her father.13

Music

True Blue is dance-pop built from synthesizers, drum machines, programmed and live percussion, stacked backing vocals and both acoustic and electric guitar, and its distinguishing move is how often it leaves that description behind. Four of the nine songs have published arrangements: “Papa Don’t Preach” at 116 beats per minute in F minor, “Open Your Heart” at 112 in F major and marked “medium funk,” “Live to Tell” at 112 in F major, and “True Blue” at 120 in B major and, alone on the record, in 12/8 — the shuffle that makes it read as a period piece.14 The album runs about forty minutes, which its own liner notes and AllMusic put slightly differently.15

The departures are the point. “True Blue” and “Jimmy Jimmy” work the Brill Building-era girl-group songbook, the old changes and stacked harmonies of the early sixties, and “True Blue” is doo-wop outright. “Live to Tell” is a torch ballad with strings and almost no rhythm section for its first minutes, and its published vocal range is just under an octave, the narrowest on any of her records to that point, which is why it sounds withheld. “La Isla Bonita” brings Latin percussion, Spanish guitar and a flamenco figure, and “White Heat”, the one track with no published arrangement anywhere, opens on dialogue sampled from a James Cagney film. “Where’s the Party” and “Love Makes the World Go Round” are the straight dance-pop, and “Open Your Heart” is the funk one.16

The singing changed more than the songwriting. Robert Hilburn heard a voice so finely tailored that it extended the punch of the production rather than riding on it, and Davitt Sigerson in Rolling Stone said flatly that her singing was better than ever.17 The lyrics are about family, authority and staying: a daughter telling her father she is keeping her baby, a woman asking a lover to open up, a promise of fidelity in the title song, an island remembered, and a secret in “Live to Tell” that the singer declines to name.

Title and artwork

The album is named for one of Penn’s favorite expressions, which Madonna glossed as a very American way of saying something is true.18 Herb Ritts shot the cover profile in black and white and Warner’s Jeri Heiden hand-tinted it blue. The biographer Lucy O’Brien later called the image the first glimpse of Madonna as a classic icon, and Billboard ranked it among the hundred greatest album covers in 2023.19

What it inherits and what it introduces

The title song and “Jimmy Jimmy” reach back past disco entirely into the girl-group songbook, and “Live to Tell” reaches sideways into film scoring. What the record introduced was a method rather than a sound: the Leonard partnership, and the write-it-in-a-day, sing-it-once discipline, became the engine of her records for the next decade and produced Like a Prayer on the same terms.20 One track left a longer trail than the others. AllMusic’s Stewart Mason heard “La Isla Bonita” anticipating the Latin-influenced dance-pop of the late eighties, while the scholar Eduardo Viñuela read the same song as deterritorialized clichés assembled into a fantasy for American ears.21

Release

True Blue was released on June 30, 1986. “Live to Tell” had preceded it by three months, in March, and reached No. 1 in June. “Papa Don’t Preach” followed in June and topped the Hot 100 on August 16, her birthday; “True Blue” went to Europe first, in late September, and to America in October; “Open Your Heart” came in November and “La Isla Bonita” in February 1987, closing the campaign a year after it opened.22

The videos made arguments of their own. James Foley shot “Papa Don’t Preach” in three days on Staten Island with Danny Aiello as the father. For “Open Your Heart,” Jean-Baptiste Mondino built a peep show on a set in Echo Park and put Madonna on its rotating stage; Hit Video USA refused to air it and MTV asked for alterations, and its defenders pointed out what the staging actually does, which is render the paying viewers leering and pathetic while only the dancer has a gaze.23 The “True Blue” clip Americans saw was made by the audience: Sire and MTV ran a Make My Video contest and screened three thousand entries in an all-day marathon on October 30, 1986, and the winners took a check and a directing contract for a sepia-toned teenage romance.24

Reception

“Papa Don’t Preach” drew objections from both directions at once. Planned Parenthood’s Alfred Moran urged broadcasters to reconsider airing it, arguing that it made pregnancy look fashionable; the attorney Gloria Allred demanded that Madonna say publicly that other choices existed, and no such statement came. Tipper Gore, whose committee had listed “Dress You Up” the year before, crossed the aisle to call it an important song about a real predicament. Elliot shrugged at the whole spectacle, and Madonna’s publicist held the line that she was singing a song rather than taking a stand.25

The album reviews were the warmest of her career to that point. Sigerson called it sturdy, dependable and lovable; Hilburn heard the voice extending the production; Robert Christgau graded it a B.26 Retrospect runs higher still: AllMusic’s Stephen Thomas Erlewine called it one of the great dance-pop albums, praising its reach and its sense of fun, the BBC’s Mark Savage credited it with making her the first lady of pop, and Slant’s Sal Cinquemani named it the supreme archetype for late-eighties and early-nineties pop.27

Commercially it did what no American act’s album had done in Britain, entering at No. 1 and holding six weeks on the way to being the country’s best-selling album of 1986 at over two million copies. In America it entered at No. 29 and reached No. 1 in its fifth week, holding five weeks of an eighty-two-week run, and it was eventually certified seven times platinum. The worldwide figure, an estimate rather than an audit, passed twenty-five million.28

Legacy

The record’s afterlife is mostly its singles’. “La Isla Bonita” became Europe’s biggest single of 1987 and has stayed in her live sets ever since; “Papa Don’t Preach” remains the song her arguments about authorship and intention are conducted through; and “Live to Tell” is the ballad the later ones are measured against. Aiello cut an answer record, “Papa Wants the Best for You,” that Stereogum’s Tom Breihan called ridiculous, terrible and extremely funny.29 The album itself is where the method that made the next decade of her records was settled, which is the thing the 1986 reviews could not have seen.
