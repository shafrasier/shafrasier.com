The keys had grease-pencil marks on them. William Orbit did not consider himself a keyboard player. “I’m a two-fingered virtuoso,” he told Keyboard that summer. “Wax pencils play a large part in my keyboard playing. I draw on the keys, and I use them to label what I’m doing — what samples are assigned to what key, and so on.” The gear he went back to England to pack included a Roland Juno-106, a Korg MS-20 and an Atari ST running Cubase II, which at one point caught fire mid-session, and his funniest memory of the sessions was the singer “messing around on a keyboard marked up with grease pencils.”1 The record made in that room was reviewed, in the spring of 1998, as good with reservations. Rolling Stone’s critic heard a producer who “doesn’t know enough tricks to fill a whole CD” and “a spaceship-bleep button that he presses compulsively from song to song”; twenty years later the same critic called it “the album of a lifetime.”2 Nothing on the record changed in between.

Five days

The album had two false starts before the tape arrived. In May 1997 Madonna went back to Babyface, who had produced “Take a Bow” (1994) three years earlier, and wrote several songs with him before deciding they were more of the same; by his account she “didn’t want, or need, to repeat herself.”3 Then came Rick Nowels, who had introduced himself at Barneys on the night of Clive Davis’s Grammy party and worked out of a house on Mulholland Drive with a studio in it. She “always arrived at 3 and always left at 7. We’d start with nothing and she’d walk out at the end of the day with the song demoed and the vocals cut.” Her instruction beforehand was to prepare material “either really radical or really beautiful…nothing in between.” Two five-day weeks produced nine songs. Three survived to the album, “The Power of Good-Bye,” “To Have and Not to Hold” and “Little Star,” and a fourth went to Laura Pausini in Italian.4 Patrick Leonard, her co-writer on True Blue and Like a Prayer, was asked back for songs and, this time, hardly any production. She wanted a producer from the electronica she had been listening to, and the ones she asked first said no. “I went through Tricky and Goldie and Prodigy, who is even on Maverick, and they all basically turned their elitist noses at me and said, ‘Oh, we can’t work with you. You’re a big pop star.‘”5 Guy Oseary, who ran Maverick, asked Orbit for material; Orbit had remixed “Justify My Love” (1990) and the title track of Erotica. Orbit knocked off a DAT of half-formed ideas he had lying around. “I sent it to her and she was back on the phone within five days. She’d already starting writing to it.” The germs of “Drowned World” and “Mer Girl” were on that tape.6

The laboratory

They met at her house in New York, where she played him what she had done with the others, booked The Hit Factory later that week to put her vocals on tape, and then Orbit flew home to pack up his gear. The rest was made at Larrabee North, over about four and a half months, most days by three people besides her: Orbit, the engineer Pat McCarthy and his assistant Matt Silva.7 Her account of the division of labor: “William is a complete madman genius. I’d come to him with an idea of where I wanted to go musically, hum melodies or read lyrics, and then leave him alone in the laboratory.” They came back, she said, as trance tracks eight minutes long, which they added to and cut until there were verses and choruses. Her other image was nautical: “I was the anchor, he was the waves, and the ship was our record.”8 He remembers the anchor. “I knew I had a job. I was being fired every day,” he said in 2022, and he could still recite the sayings she ran the room on, “don’t over-egg the custard” and the one she used most, “Don’t gild the lily.” “Drowned World” went out as its rough mix after every polished version lost something.9 Marius de Vries, who had come to Bedtime Stories as Nellee Hooper’s assistant, was called in near the end to co-produce three tracks and to finish “Frozen” at Oseary’s request: “Look, there’s a lot of cooks in this production kitchen already but it’s not quite finished.”10 The strings on “Frozen” and “The Power of Good-Bye,” arranged by Craig Armstrong, were cut in a single day by twenty violins, six violas, six cellos, four basses, two flutes and an oboe.11 Orbit played every guitar himself, and years later he described the winter as a collapse: “I was in a crisis with my family, which I put on hold. I remember getting very ill that winter; at the age of 43, I was done in physically.”12 The cover was shot on November 28, 1997, by Mario Testino, who remembered the session as a standoff. “At 2pm she said, ‘OK, I’m tired. We’re done.’ And I said, ‘But I don’t have the pictures yet.‘” He kept shooting over her objection, and “the picture she used on the cover came after that.”13

The voice

The instrument that changed most was hers. For Evita she had trained with a coach for the first time. “I suddenly discovered that I was only using half of my voice,” she told Robert Hilburn. “Until then, I had pretty much accepted that I had a very limited range.” Anita O’Day and Edith Piaf had limited ranges too, she added, and she was a fan of both.14 Leonard, who had been writing for the old voice since 1985, heard a singer in a different place because of Evita and declined to grade the change: “not better or worse, just different.”15 Orbit pushed the new range past its edge on purpose. He kept the title track, “Ray of Light,” pitched above where she was comfortable, a semitone by Lucy O’Brien’s account, and defended the strain: “She got frustrated when we were recording but you want that bit of edge with singers, that thing of reaching. You can’t fake it, and you can hear it when she cracks it on the record.”16 Not every ear in 1998 wanted the lessons. Rob Sheffield thought she “still hasn’t unlearned her Evita voice lessons, which means her diction can get painfully prissy; on ‘Drowned World,’ she enunciates the word lovers as if she’s never met any.”17

Where the words came from

The lyric sheet is a record of borrowings, most of them declared. “Ray of Light” began as “Sepheryn,” a 1971 song by the English folk duo Curtiss Maldoon. In 1996 Clive Maldoon’s niece, Christine Leach, sang her own version of it, with a rewritten chorus, over a melody Orbit was working on; Orbit put the result on his tape believing Leach had written the song, and Madonna rewrote the words a third time. Dave Curtiss first heard what had become of it on the radio. The royalties were split with Madonna at 30 percent, Curtiss and Maldoon’s estate at 15 each and the balance to Warner, and the first version of the track ran past ten minutes. “It was completely indulgent, but I loved it,” she said. “It was heartbreaking to cut it down to a manageable length.”18 Two lines in the chorus of “Sky Fits Heaven” came from a Gap commercial in which the poet Max Blagg read them; her publicist confirmed that Blagg had been paid and would receive no credit.19 “Shanti/Ashtangi” sets Sanskrit verse from the Yoga Taravali, a text attributed to Shankaracharya, in a translation credited to Eddie Stern and Vyaas Houston, and Sanskrit scholars noticed the pronunciation; by New Year’s Eve MTV was relaying a Times of India report that she had agreed to take lessons in Varanasi.20 “Drowned World” samples a San Sebastian Strings record by Rod McKuen and Anita Kerr, who are credited as co-writers for it, and “Mer Girl” carries an interpolation of Gábor Szabó’s “Space.”21 “Candy Perfume Girl” began as words and a melody Susannah Melvoin wrote over an Orbit track, on the floor of his studio, about her brother’s death from heroin, for what she assumed would be her own record or a Strange Cargo album; she learned it was going on Madonna’s when her publisher called, and that Madonna wanted a third of the publishing. She says she also wrote the first words and melody of “Swim,” for which she is not credited. The makers of Magnetic Poetry, meanwhile, announced that fewer than four percent of the words in “Candy Perfume Girl” could not be found in one of their refrigerator kits, and Madonna’s spokesperson said she had never heard of the product.22

The wave

The claim that follows the record is that it brought electronica into the pop mainstream, and the dates do not quite hold it up. Her own label had released the Prodigy’s The Fat of the Land (1997) the summer before, and Spin’s later ranking put it plainly: the album “arrived after that wave had crested.”23 The 1998 notices said so too, from both sides. Sheffield had her “riding the electronica wave” with “trip-hop drum loops, jungle snares, string samples and pointless computer bleeps,” and David Browne, who gave it an A− in Entertainment Weekly, said she had “dipped her latest collection of songs in a light batter of electronica” and counted one track, the title song, that reached dance-floor tempo.24 Most of it is slow. Strings and Orbit’s guitar sit under the samplers and the tempos stay midrange; Spin’s ranking counted two cuts built for a floor, the title track and “Sky Fits Heaven.” The restraint was deliberate, and she explained it as feeling. “I’ve always been interested in electronica, techno, trip-hop, that kind of music,” she told Hilburn. “The thing that bothered me about a lot of that music, though, was it seemed devoid of emotion.” She wanted “to take my feelings and marry them to something that is traditionally not considered very emotional or personal.”25 Browne’s comparison was not to the Chemical Brothers or Underworld but to Paul Simon’s Graceland (1986): a star letting a newer music carry her songs.24 The record took the wave to a scale no one inside it had reached, and slowed it down.

What it inherits and what it introduces

Two older strands in it are hers. One is the melodic ballad she and Leonard had been writing since 1985, now with an orchestra on top; “Frozen” and “The Power of Good-Bye” are songs of that kind in a new coat, and Leonard said as much when he described his own role this time as “just watching a little bit.”26 The other runs from Bedtime Stories, through Hooper, de Vries and Björk, to Orbit. The Chicago Tribune granted that the record was less daring than Björk, Tricky, Goldie or Portishead and still found her “the most at home” of the pop stars who had tried the format, “probably because she’s been there all along.”27 It introduced a voice: the trained instrument it showed off is the one the next records kept, and a record built mostly from samplers and secondhand synthesizers by a producer, a singer and two engineers went to No. 1 in Britain and Germany and to No. 2 in America.28 It also made Orbit a first-call producer; within two years he had produced Blur’s 13 (1999) and All Saints’ “Pure Shores.”29 Two years later she spent the license it bought her on Mirwais.

The one with the orchestra

“Frozen” came out of a Leonard session, and her brief to him was a film: “I was obsessed with the movie The Sheltering Sky and the whole Moroccan/orchestral/superromantic/man-carrying-the-woman-he-loves-across-the-desert vibe. So I told Pat that I wanted something with a tribal feel, something really lush and romantic.” She free-associated the melody onto a DAT while he played.30 Leonard remembers the brief as two other titles: “somewhere between The English Patient and Nine Inch Nails.”31 Chris Cunningham shot the video at Cuddeback Lake in the Mojave over five days in January 1998, a location she had chosen over Iceland on the theory that a desert would at least be warm. “It was like 20 degrees below zero, it was bitterly cold, and I was barefoot.” Then it rained, and the crew got sick. On screen she turns into a flock of birds and then a black dog.32 Released on January 23, 1998, the single entered the British chart at No. 1, the first of hers to do so, and stopped at No. 2 in America, the sixth of her singles to stop there.33 Its afterlife was in a Belgian courtroom. In November 2005 a judge in Mons found that its first four sung bars copied “Ma vie fout l’camp,” a song by a composer named Salvatore Acquaviva, and ordered EMI, Sony and Warner to stop the song’s sale and broadcast in Belgium within fifteen days, and to pull the album with it, or face a fine Acquaviva’s lawyer put at “more than 100,000 euros.”34 The appeal court in Mons threw the case out in February 2014, after a third party had surfaced in Paris claiming both songs derived from a 1983 composition called “Bloodnight”; the court found the disputed bars in so many earlier works that Acquaviva’s own song was not original enough to have been plagiarized.35

Reviews and the chart

Robert Christgau met it in Playboy with three warnings: “Maturity is a tough one for pop-music artistes. Enlightenment invariably kicks off an awkward stage. And motherhood can get icky fast.” His verdict on a record that took on all three at once was that “it ain’t half bad.”36 Sheffield’s four stars in Rolling Stone came with a summary verdict: “overly arty, occasionally catchy, confused, secondhand, infuriating and great fun in spite of herself.”37 Stuart Maconie, in Q, called it “a great, mad, modern record” and closed on a coinage: “she remains the only pop aristocrat who’s keeping her ears open. She’s become The Immaterial Girl.”38 Hilburn, in the Los Angeles Times, called it “the most satisfying album of her career,” while the paper’s graded review, by Elysa Gardner, stopped at three and a half stars of four; the Chicago Tribune read Leonard’s return as a hedge; Toronto’s Now gave it two stars of five.39 Stephen Thomas Erlewine’s AllMusic capsule splits the difference: “the first mainstream pop album that successfully embraces techno,” and “her most mature and restrained album, which is an easy achievement to admire, yet not necessarily an easy one to love.”40

The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 behind the Titanic soundtrack on 371,000 copies, the biggest opening week by a woman since SoundScan began counting in 1991, sold another 225,000 the next week and stayed at No. 2, her fifth album held at that position; in Britain it opened at No. 1 on about 139,000 and held the top for two weeks.41 The singles ran downhill in order: “Frozen” No. 2, “Ray of Light” No. 5, “The Power of Good-Bye” No. 11, and “Nothing Really Matters” No. 93, her lowest Hot 100 placing then or since.42 At the MTV Video Music Awards that September she won six of nine nominations, five of them for Jonas Åkerlund’s time-lapse video for “Ray of Light,” and at the Grammys the following February the album took four awards from six nominations: Best Pop Album and Best Dance Recording to her, Best Short Form Music Video to Åkerlund and her, and Best Recording Package to its art director, Kevin Reagan, so her own count is three. They were the first Grammys of her career for the music; her only earlier one had been for a concert video. Album of the Year went to Lauryn Hill.43

Influence and legacy

Gabriel Szatan, in the Guardian in 2018, put the two facts in one breath: “if anything, the album’s stock was modest then compared to now. Ray of Light is probably Madonna’s most widely acknowledged classic.”44 Rolling Stone’s list of the 500 greatest albums had it at No. 363 in the 2003 edition and No. 222 in the 2020 one; Stereogum ranks it second among her albums and Spin fourth.45 Sheffield wrote Rolling Stone’s twentieth-anniversary piece himself. In 1998 he had found Orbit repetitive; in 2018 the same record “holds up as her most soulful and passionate music ever,” and he explained the distance in terms of how people bought records then: “You gave it a few chances before you gave up. So people stuck with Ray of Light, even if they initially laughed at it.” The laugh, he said, had been at the spirituality, which a film comedy would parody a decade on as a pop star’s “Buddhism-in-a-thong philosophy.”46 Alex Frank, reviewing it for Pitchfork in 2017, held both thoughts at once: that she “played a large role in reopening mainstream American music to the club sounds of Europe,” and that the record “has to be in some ways to blame for Goop” and the celebrity wellness trade behind it.47 The influence claims that hold are specific ones. Adele named it a chief inspiration for 25 (2015), Nelly Furtado used it as the template for Loose (2006), Grimes called it a masterpiece, and Britney Spears worked with Orbit on “Alien.”48

In 2025 the remix companion she had wanted in 1998 finally appeared, under the alter ego she had once considered naming the album for, Veronica Electronica.49 Orbit died the following summer, and what he had said of the record in 2021 was about her. “She’s a fabulous producer. When it says ‘produced by Madonna and William Orbit’, people don’t always give her the credit for that. But she’s as responsible as me.”50 The sounds came off keys labeled in wax pencil, and the first reviews heard it. It took twenty years for the same sounds to be heard as decisions.
