Ray of Light is the seventh studio album by Madonna, released on March 3, 1998, by Maverick and Warner Bros. It was her first album of new songs since Bedtime Stories (1994), and it was made after a three-year gap in which she trained her singing voice for Evita (1996) and had her first child. She recorded it between May and November 1997, most of it at Larrabee North in Los Angeles with the English producer William Orbit, whose samplers and secondhand synthesizers give the record its texture; Patrick Leonard, Rick Nowels and Marius de Vries wrote or produced parts of it. The album is downtempo electronic pop, slower than its reputation, and its subjects are fame, motherhood, grief and belief.

Ray of Light entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 on 371,000 copies, the largest opening week by a woman since SoundScan began counting in 1991, and stayed on that chart for seventy-eight weeks. It went to No. 1 in Britain and produced four American singles that fell in order: “Frozen” at No. 2 on the Hot 100, the title track at No. 5, “The Power of Good-Bye” at No. 11 and “Nothing Really Matters” at No. 93, her lowest American placing then or since. Reviews in 1998 were good with reservations, and the record won four Grammy Awards from six nominations, the first of her career for music. Its standing has risen steadily since: Rolling Stone moved it from No. 363 to No. 222 on its list of the five hundred greatest albums, and the critic who complained in 1998 that Orbit did not know enough tricks called it, twenty years later, the album of a lifetime.

Background

Madonna’s commercial position going in was weaker than her fame suggested. Bedtime Stories had sold two million American copies in the three years since its release, a figure Billboard printed in the week the new album was announced, and its biggest hit had been a Babyface ballad rather than a dance record. The compilation Something to Remember followed in November 1995 and gathered ballads. What she had instead of a hit record was respectability. She had written a four-page letter to Alan Parker in December 1994 arguing her case for Evita, recorded the soundtrack at CTS Studios in London over nearly four months with an eighty-four-piece orchestra, and shot the film in Buenos Aires; it took a Golden Globe for her in January 1997 and an Academy Award for its new song that March. She told the Los Angeles Times afterward that five or six films she was to have made had since fallen apart for want of money or casting, so the acting had not opened a second career.1

Her daughter Lourdes was born on October 14, 1996, at Good Samaritan Hospital in Los Angeles, and the record was written in the year that followed.2 So was a change in her singing. Preparing for Evita she worked with a coach for the first time and found, in her account to Robert Hilburn, that she had been using half of her voice and had assumed her range was simply small.3

The music she wanted to make had just been declared the future of the American record business and then quietly failed to be. Billboard had run a front-page package on electronica in February 1997 announcing it as the industry’s salvation; the same paper ran the reckoning on December 6, 1997, three months before her album was finished, reporting a chastened business talking about education and patience. The sentence that measures the moment is a chart fact from that piece: of all the electronica signed and released in the boom, exactly two albums were on the Billboard 200 that week, the Prodigy’s The Fat of the Land at No. 59 and Portishead’s second album at No. 150.4 The American charts in the week before her release belonged to the Titanic soundtrack, which the RIAA had just certified for eight million copies faster than any soundtrack in its history, and to Will Smith.5

She was explicit about what she wanted from a music she had been listening to for years. “The thing that bothered me about a lot of that music, though, was it seemed devoid of emotion,” she told Hilburn, and said she meant to take her feelings and marry them to something not traditionally considered emotional.6

Recording

The album had two false starts. In May 1997 Madonna went back to Babyface, who had produced “Take a Bow” three years earlier, wrote several songs with him, and set them aside; by his account she did not want to repeat herself.7 Then came Rick Nowels, who worked out of a house on Mulholland Drive and remembered a fixed schedule: she arrived at three, left at seven, and walked out each day with a song demoed and the vocal cut. Her instruction beforehand was for material either radical or beautiful and nothing in between. Two five-day weeks produced nine songs, of which three reached the album, “The Power of Good-Bye,” “To Have and Not to Hold” and “Little Star.”8 Patrick Leonard, her co-writer since True Blue, was asked back for songs and, this time, almost no production.

The producers she wanted first said no. “I went through Tricky and Goldie and Prodigy, who is even on Maverick, and they all basically turned their elitist noses at me and said, ‘Oh, we can’t work with you. You’re a big pop star.‘”9 Guy Oseary, who ran Maverick, asked William Orbit for material instead. Orbit had remixed “Justify My Love” and the title track of Erotica, and he sent a tape of half-formed ideas he had lying around. She was back on the phone within five days, already writing to it, and the germs of “Drowned World/Substitute for Love” and “Mer Girl” were on it.10

They met at her house in New York, booked The Hit Factory that week to get her vocals onto tape, and then Orbit flew home to pack his gear. He did not consider himself a keyboard player and labeled his keys in grease pencil to mark what sample sat where. What he brought back included a Roland Juno-106, a Korg MS-20 and an Atari ST running Cubase, which caught fire mid-session.11 The rest was made at Larrabee North over about four and a half months, most days by three people besides her: Orbit, the engineer Pat McCarthy and his assistant Matt Silva.12

Her account of the division of labor was that she brought an idea, hummed melodies or read lyrics, and left him alone in what she called the laboratory; the tracks came back as eight-minute trance pieces that they then cut into verses and choruses. Her other image was nautical, and she was the anchor.13 Orbit remembered the anchor. “I knew I had a job. I was being fired every day,” he said in 2022, and he could still recite the rules she ran the room on, the one she used most being “Don’t gild the lily.” “Drowned World” shipped as its rough mix, because every polished version lost something.14 Marius de Vries, who had come to Bedtime Stories as Nellee Hooper’s assistant, was brought in near the end to co-produce three tracks and finish “Frozen.”15 The strings on “Frozen” and “The Power of Good-Bye,” arranged by Craig Armstrong and conducted by Suzie Katayama, were cut in a single day by twenty violins, six violas, six cellos, four basses, two flutes and an oboe.16 Orbit played every guitar himself and described the winter afterward as a collapse: a family crisis put on hold, illness, and at what he remembered as forty-three being physically done in.17

Music

Ray of Light is downtempo electronic pop, and it is slower than the word electronica suggests. Published sheet music gives a key for twelve of the thirteen tracks and a tempo for seven, and every one of those tempos except three sits at or below 117 beats per minute; the opening song runs at 72. The fast ones are the title track and “Sky Fits Heaven” at 126, and, oddly, the lullaby “Little Star” at 140.18 Under the samplers there are real instruments throughout: Orbit’s guitars on almost everything, Fergus Gerrand’s drums, Armstrong’s orchestra on two songs. J.D. Considine, reviewing it in the Baltimore Sun on the day of release, put the contradiction in one line, hearing music that was “utterly visceral” however ethereal its themes, “rich with soft, swooshing synths while packing all the percussive punch of a club hit.”19

The voice is the other change. Madonna sings lower and more precisely than on her earlier records, which Stuart Maconie in Q measured at about half an octave and thought occasionally added an unwanted portentousness. Not every ear wanted the lessons: Rob Sheffield thought she had not unlearned them and that her diction could turn prissy.20 Leonard, who had been writing for the old voice since 1985, declined to grade the change and called it different rather than better or worse. Orbit pushed the new range past its edge on purpose, keeping the title track pitched above where she was comfortable and defending the strain as the sound of someone reaching.21

Four songs sit well outside the album’s center. “Ray of Light” is its one overt techno record, built on an arpeggiated guitar figure and a synthesizer line that moves between bass and treble, with a solo before the last chorus that reaches back toward progressive rock; Maconie heard it build into thumping rave-anthem mode. “Shanti/Ashtangi” is a techno setting of a Sanskrit devotional text, and Considine caught what makes it unusual: Orbit grounds the chanting “with clanking industrial sounds instead of the usual sitars and tablas,” so the song’s Indianness is entirely in the words and the melody. “To Have and Not to Hold” is a bossa nova, written to that beat by Nowels. “Mer Girl” is barely a song, a spoken-sung art piece over what Considine called a stark, spooky smear of synthesizers and effects.22

The rest range without leaving the record’s world. “Drowned World/Substitute for Love” is ambient pop, opening on a vibraphone and ghostly synthesizers before guitars raise the noise, and the first voice on the album is not hers but a sampled actor’s. “Swim” is hazy trip hop with aquatic effects. “Candy Perfume Girl” is the one place the record touches alternative rock, pairing electronic beats with grunge guitar. “Skin” is its longest and most maximal track, a trance built from skittering beats, a field recording made in a Marrakesh market and a synthesizer squall. “Nothing Really Matters” is the most conventionally Madonna-shaped song here, house-inflected dance-pop with no live instrumentation at all and her old backing singers Donna De Lory and Niki Haris on it. “Sky Fits Heaven” runs on piano and guitar at the album’s fast end. “Frozen” is a downtempo ballad whose orchestral arrangement does structural work rather than decoration, and “The Power of Good-Bye” is its companion, in the same key, with a shuffle beat and acoustic guitar under the strings. “Little Star” is an ambient lullaby with drum-and-bass programming, and the only track Orbit did not produce.23

The lyrics are about fame, water, motherhood and death, and several of them are borrowed. “Drowned World” takes its title from J.G. Ballard’s novel and its subject from the difference between being adored and being loved. “Swim” sets a catalog of social violence against an instruction to go down to the ocean floor and start again. “Nothing Really Matters” is about her daughter, and so is “Little Star.” “Mer Girl” is about her mother’s death and her own, and ends in a cemetery. “Frozen” addresses a man who cannot open his heart, “The Power of Good-Bye” leaves one, and “Candy Perfume Girl” is the record’s erotic writing, such as it is.24

The borrowings are declared on the sleeve, mostly. The title track began as “Sepheryn,” a 1971 song by the English folk duo Curtiss Maldoon; Clive Maldoon’s niece Christine Leach sang her own version of it over an Orbit track in 1996, Orbit put it on the tape believing Leach had written it, and Madonna rewrote the words a third time. Dave Curtiss first heard what had become of his song on the radio, and the royalties were split with Madonna at thirty percent and Curtiss and Maldoon’s estate at fifteen each.25 Two lines in the chorus of “Sky Fits Heaven” came from a poem by Max Blagg used in a Gap commercial; her publicist confirmed that Blagg had been paid and would get no credit.26 “Drowned World” samples a San Sebastian Strings record by Rod McKuen and Anita Kerr, who are credited as co-writers, and “Mer Girl” interpolates Gábor Szabó’s “Space.”27 “Candy Perfume Girl” began as words and a melody Susannah Melvoin wrote about her brother’s death from heroin, for what she assumed would be her own record; she learned otherwise when her publisher called, and says she also wrote the first words and melody of “Swim,” for which she is not credited.28

Title and artwork

The album is named for its third song, and the alter ego Madonna had considered naming it for instead, Veronica Electronica, waited twenty-seven years to appear on a record.29 The cover was photographed by Mario Testino on November 28, 1997, in a session he remembered as a standoff: at two in the afternoon she said she was tired and finished, he said he did not have the pictures yet, and the image she used came after that.30 The package was art-directed by Kevin Reagan with design by Kerosene Halo, and it won the Grammy for Best Recording Package.31

What it inherits and what it introduces

Two older strands in the record are her own. The first is the melodic ballad she and Leonard had been writing since 1985, now with an orchestra on it; “Frozen” and “The Power of Good-Bye” are songs of that kind in a new coat, and Leonard described his own part this time as mostly watching. The second runs from Bedtime Stories through Hooper, de Vries and Björk to Orbit, which is a line of British electronic production she had already been following for four years. The Chicago Tribune granted that the record was less daring than Björk, Tricky or Portishead and still found her the most at home of the pop stars who had tried the form, “probably because she’s been there all along.”32

She was specific in early 1998 about what she had been listening to, and the list is the album’s family tree: Björk, whom she called incredibly brave and daring; Everything but the Girl, for the plaintive quality of Tracey Thorn’s voice; Tricky and Martina; the Verve, until “Bitter Sweet Symphony” was on the radio every two seconds; and Air, whose album she called fierce. What she said she responded to was songs with a bittersweetness, something haunted with a visceral groove under it, and she found more of that in Europe than in America.33 The taste ran through the label she owned. Her own two recent signings at Maverick were both Swedish and both electronic, and she described them by pointing at the same shelf: one reminded her of Air with a Sneaker Pimps vibe, the other of Portishead with twists.34

The precedent everyone in the building had watched was one year old to the week. U2’s Pop, made with Flood and Howie B, had come out on March 3, 1997, entered at No. 1 on 349,000 American copies, fallen fifty-seven per cent in its second week and become the worst-selling album of that band’s career. Ray of Light’s own opening, 371,000, is within six per cent of it. The difference between the two records is entirely what happened after the second week.35

What was new was scale and voice. The trained instrument the record shows off is the one her later records kept, and an album built mostly from samplers and secondhand synthesizers by a producer, a singer and two engineers went to No. 1 in Britain and to No. 2 in America at a moment when the genre it borrowed from could put two albums on the American chart at once.36 It also made Orbit a first-call producer; within two years he had made Blur’s 13 (1999) and All Saints’ “Pure Shores.”37 The claim that usually gets made for it, that it brought electronica into the American mainstream, is the one part of its reputation the record cannot carry. The hype had already come and gone — her own label had released the Prodigy’s The Fat of the Land the summer before — and what this album did was arrive after the wave crested and domesticate what was left of it.38

Release

Radio took the record before the label gave it away. “Frozen” went to European stations on January 23, 1998 and was on BBC Radio 1’s A-list inside a week; the official American airplay date was February 19, by which point Billboard counted about a dozen leaks on top-forty stations since late January. WKTU in New York reported the phones blowing up, people calling nonstop to beg for it; B-96 in Chicago put it on in early February and its programmer said flatly that American pop radio needed Madonna. A fan site in Singapore that posted an unauthorized snippet on January 23 took more than a hundred and forty thousand hits. A Tower Records buyer in New York gave the trade the industry’s own reason for the appetite: the business needed an exciting, mega-star release, and this would fill that void.39

Ray of Light was released on March 3, 1998, on compact disc and cassette, with the double LP a British and European edition; Japan had it on February 22 with a fourteenth track, “Has to Be,” that appeared nowhere else. The commercial double LP carries a misprint on its fourth side, where “To Have and Not to Hold” is listed as “To Have And Not To Have.”40

“Frozen” came first, in January, with a video Chris Cunningham shot at Cuddeback Lake in the Mojave, booked for five days that month and cut to two by the rain, a location she had chosen over Iceland on the theory that a desert would be warm; it was twenty below, she was barefoot, then it rained and the crew got sick. On screen she turns into a flock of birds and then a black dog.41 The title track followed in April, and Jonas Åkerlund’s video for it took Koyaanisqatsi as its model: her own scenes were shot over two days in Los Angeles in late March, and the rest is time-lapse gathered over a fortnight in five cities, one frame every ten seconds to make five seconds of footage, cameras strapped to buses in New York. Her gloss was that it shows a day in the life of the earth, to say that we are rushing toward the end of the century at full speed. It won five awards at the MTV Video Music Awards in September, where she took six of nine nominations.42

The three later clips are a run of collaborations with directors working at the top of the form. Walter Stern filmed “Drowned World/Substitute for Love” at Claridge’s and Piccadilly Circus in June, with Anita Pallenberg and Steve Strange in cameos and Madonna pursued by paparazzi on motorbikes into her daughter’s arms; the British press read it against Diana’s death ten months earlier, and her publicist answered that the video was about Madonna’s own relationship to fame. Matthew Rolston shot “The Power of Good-Bye” in August with Goran Višnjić, the chess scene borrowed from The Thomas Crown Affair and the beach walk filmed on a treadmill. Johan Renck made “Nothing Really Matters” in January 1999 out of Memoirs of a Geisha, in a red Jean Paul Gaultier kimono.43

The controversy of the era was not about the kimono. At the Video Music Awards that September she performed “Shanti/Ashtangi” in henna and tilaka against a backdrop of Hindu deities before stripping to a tank top for the title track. The World Vaishnava Association called the performance sacrilegious. Her reply was one sentence: “If they’re so pure, why are they watching MTV?”44

“Drowned World/Substitute for Love” was issued in August everywhere except the United States, “The Power of Good-Bye” in September, paired in Britain that December with “Little Star,” and “Nothing Really Matters” in February 1999.45 “Sky Fits Heaven” was held back deliberately, so that it would not eat into the title track’s run.46 A tour was announced and then was not. In February 1998 the trade reported she was considering something totally scaled down for late summer, drawing its set from this record, Bedtime Stories and Erotica, with a remix and outtakes compilation called Veronica Electronica due in the fall. The tour slid to 1999, then past The Next Best Thing, her marriage and her son’s birth, and finally ran in the summer of 2001 as the Drowned World Tour, opening each night with the song that opens the album.47

Reception

The 1998 reviews were good with reservations, and the reservations were usually about Orbit or about the spirituality. Robert Christgau met it in Playboy with three warnings, that maturity is hard for pop artists, that enlightenment kicks off an awkward stage, and that motherhood can get icky fast, before concluding that a record taking on all three at once “ain’t half bad.”48 Sheffield’s four stars in Rolling Stone came with a summary verdict of “overly arty, occasionally catchy, confused, secondhand, infuriating and great fun in spite of herself.”49 Maconie in Q called it “a great, mad, modern record.”50 Hilburn, in the Los Angeles Times, called it the most satisfying album of her career, while his own paper’s graded review stopped at three and a half stars of four; Toronto’s Now gave it two of five. Stephen Thomas Erlewine’s AllMusic capsule splits the difference, calling it the first mainstream pop album to embrace techno successfully and also “her most mature and restrained album, which is an easy achievement to admire, yet not necessarily an easy one to love.”51

Commercially it was her largest opening and her longest chart life. The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 behind the Titanic soundtrack on 371,000 copies, sold another 225,000 the next week and stayed at No. 2, and remained on the chart for seventy-eight weeks. In Britain it opened at No. 1 on about 139,000 and held the top for two weeks, running a hundred and thirty-eight weeks in all. The singles fell in order, and “Nothing Really Matters” reached only No. 93. The RIAA certified the album four times platinum in March 2000.52 At the Grammy Awards in February 1999 it took four from six nominations, Best Pop Album and Best Dance Recording to Madonna, Best Short Form Music Video to Åkerlund and her, and Best Recording Package to Reagan, so her own count is three. They were the first Grammys of her career for music; her only earlier one had been for a concert video. Album of the Year went to Lauryn Hill.53

Legacy

The record’s reputation has risen more or less continuously since. Gabriel Szatan put both facts in one breath in the Guardian in 2018, that its stock was modest at the time compared with now, and that it is probably Madonna’s most widely acknowledged classic.54 Rolling Stone had it at No. 363 on its list of the five hundred greatest albums in 2003 and No. 222 in 2020.55 Sheffield wrote that magazine’s twentieth-anniversary piece himself, and reversed: the record he had found repetitive now “holds up as her most soulful and passionate music ever.” He explained the distance by how people bought records then, giving an album a few chances before giving up, and said the laugh in 1998 had been at the spirituality.56 Alex Frank, writing for Pitchfork in 2017, held two thoughts at once: that she had helped reopen mainstream American music to European club sounds, and that the record is in some way to blame for the celebrity wellness trade that followed.57

The mockery was real and it is worth showing, because the reversal only means something against it. Sheffield’s own account is that her spiritual pretensions were ripe for it, and names the parody in Music and Lyrics, where a pop star offers her Buddhism-in-a-thong philosophy. Szatan grants that it is easy to poke fun at her extolling Kabbalah and the legacy of faux depth that followed, and says it sells the record short. Billboard’s anniversary piece put the risk exactly: in lesser hands this would have been Chicken Soup for the Raver’s Soul.58

Where it sits now depends on who is counting. Slant ranks it first of her sixteen albums and Stereogum second; SPIN puts it fourth, and Pitchfork’s own published scores rank it fourth in her catalog, behind Like a Prayer, Erotica and the debut. Rolling Stone’s 2020 list runs the other way and puts it two hundred places above Like a Prayer.59 The influence claims that hold are specific. Adele named it a chief inspiration for 25 (2015), Nelly Furtado used it as the template for Loose (2006), Grimes called it a masterpiece, and Britney Spears later worked with Orbit.60 The 2025 verdicts are about the sound rather than the person: Kelly Lee Owens calls it a perfect blend of pop sensibility and electronic innovation, and one critic’s summary that year was that it is probably the Madonna album that sounds best now.61

“Frozen” had an afterlife of its own in a Belgian courtroom, where a judge in Mons found in November 2005 that its first four sung bars copied a song by the composer Salvatore Acquaviva and ordered the song pulled in Belgium; three labels were given fifteen days to take it off sale and off the air, under a penalty the carriers report at different six-figure sums. Acquaviva’s theory of how she had heard his song was that she passed through Mouscron in the late 1970s, dancing on tour with Patrick Hernandez, whose records were pressed there. She sang it at Werchter in 2009 while the ban still stood, and the Belgian edition of Celebration left it off. The appeal court threw the case out in February 2014, finding the disputed bars in so many earlier works that Acquaviva’s own song was not original enough to have been copied, and ordered him to pay the labels’ costs.62

In 2025 the remix companion she had wanted in 1998 finally appeared as Veronica Electronica, eight 1998 remixes and an unreleased demo, twenty-seven years late; the album itself has still never had an expanded or remastered edition.63 Orbit died the following summer. Months before that he posted that he had sent her camp a sequel and had not had a syllable back in two decades, then closed the subject himself: zero hard feelings, she made his career.64 What he had said of the record in 2021 was about her: “She’s a fabulous producer. When it says ‘produced by Madonna and William Orbit’, people don’t always give her the credit for that. But she’s as responsible as me.”65
