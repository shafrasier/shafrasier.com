Album

Erotica

Madonna
ReleasedOctober 20, 1992 (US); October 19, 1992 (UK)
LabelMaverick / Sire
RecordedJuly 1991 – September 1992
ProducerMadonna, Shep Pettibone, André Betts
StudioShep Pettibone’s home studio, Soundworks, Master Mix, Clinton Recording (New York)
Genres
Primary
Dance-pop
ListenRYM·Apple Music·Spotify

“I hate them.” Madonna had just heard the first batch of songs, and what she objected to was the finish. “I thought it sounded great because some of the songs had a New York house sound and some of them had an L.A. vibe,” Shep Pettibone wrote afterward. “‘If I had wanted the album to sound like that, I’d have worked with Patrick Leonard in L.A.,’ she told me. I got the point pretty fast.”1 What she wanted was a record that sounded, in his phrase, “as if it were recorded in an alley at 123rd street in Harlem.” He went back to what he called his usual style, “pretty bass oriented, analog, hit-you-over-the head kind of stuff,” and the album that resulted is the one she is still defending.2

The penthouse

The method was the argument. Pettibone had flown to Chicago on July 8, 1991, where she was filming A League of Their Own, and handed her a cassette of three tracks he had built with his assistant Tony Shimkin, at a point when neither of them knew there was an album coming.3 She liked all three. The work then moved into his New York apartment and stayed there. “I did everything upstairs in my home studio: keyboards, bass lines, and vocals,” he wrote, naming the gear: an Oberheim OB8, a Korg M3, a Roland D-50, and an Akai S1000 sampler that fed the record its snake charmers and its horns.4 Vocals went to an eight-track Tascam reel-to-reel, three tracks through seven, and stayed there: “Ninety-eight percent of the time, the vocals recorded in my apartment were the keeper vocals, the ones you hear on the album.”5 The demos are the album.

The division of labor had a hard border. “I did the music and she wrote the words,” Pettibone wrote, recalling an earlier session where he had offered a lyric idea and been told, curtly, “That’s what I do.”6 The pace was hers too. She would arrive around one in the afternoon, improvise a vocal in one or two passes, and get impatient when the machines needed time: “What are you guys doing that’s taking so long!”7 Shimkin, then twenty-one or twenty-two, eventually snapped back that he was not finished and she could go make some popcorn until he was. “And she was like, ‘Ah-k.’ I think she appreciated someone not being a sycophant.”8

Dita

The title track went through four complete versions before it settled. “She loved the groove. She would sing it one way, background vocals harmonies and all, then decide to erase everything and start over again,” Pettibone said. “Shame she made me erase stuff.”9 The hook that survived came out of his own record library: “Being a DJ first with DJ ears, I heard [their track] ‘Jungle Boogie’ in my head over the song. I went and found the album in my library, rode it over the existing ‘Erotica’ track and it worked!”10 The horns are Kool & the Gang’s, sampled on the S1000. The song also lifts a vocal from the Lebanese singer Fairuz, who said she had not consented; the matter settled out of court, and the single and the album were both banned in Lebanon.11

The voice arrived last. Pettibone told her the book she was shooting with Steven Meisel was full of material the song could use; she took it out of the room and came back half an hour later speaking into the microphone in a flat, dry register that was not quite hers.12 Shimkin dates the character’s arrival to the mixing: “That’s when the character emerged, this Dita, when she ad-libbed the speaking parts. Then the character became something that took over.”13 Dita was the name Madonna used checking into hotels.14

The appetite for a voice she could put on runs through the smaller decisions too. On “Bye Bye Baby” the telephone-filter effect was printed to tape as she sang, so it could never be taken back off: “the effect inspired Madonna’s performance because she heard her voice as she was recording it,” Shimkin said. “I guess this song is the equivalent of breaking up with someone by text today.”15 On “Deeper and Deeper” she wanted a flamenco guitar through the middle of what Pettibone called a Philly house song, and he lost the argument he describes as not wanting to put “La Isla Bonita” in the middle of it.16 Shimkin, who played the figure, remembers the resolution differently: “When you have Madonna’s enthusiasm for something, her determination even, she’s not really someone you can say no to! […] He was like, ‘If we’re gonna do it, let’s really do it.’ And then the castanets came into play.”17 The “Vogue” line she sings in the last minute got there because Pettibone was idly singing it over the track and she copied him.18

The other end of the spectrum

André Betts had co-produced “Justify My Love,” and got four songs on this record. Madonna hired him for what she described to Billboard in 1992 as the contrast itself: Pettibone and Betts “come from opposite ends of the spectrum in terms of their style and approach to music, but they’re both connected to the street and they’re still young and hungry.”19 Betts’s own read was blunter — “I’ve always felt like my music was too hardcore to make it in the pop world.”20 His instruction from her was to avoid anywhere respectable: “Find a studio, I don’t want to work in a popular studio, I want to be low-key,” which produced a room he calls “a hole in the wall for real,” and a session interrupted by a rat crossing the floor. Only Betts got his feet up.21

“Where Life Begins”, the first thing they wrote, is an argument for oral sex that declares its subject in the opening line and never retreats from it. “I wasn’t surprised that she was being so explicit,” Betts said. “I looked at it like this: ‘She’s Madonna, she can say whatever she wants.‘”22 “Did You Do It?” was an accident that stuck. Freestyling over the “Waiting” instrumental while she was out at dinner, Betts had one of the guys in the room shout the question and answered it himself, then played the result to her as a joke and watched her laugh until she cried. She put it on the album, which is how the album got its Parental Advisory sticker, the first of her career.23 Betts remembers the follow-up call: “You know you’re the reason I have to have an explicit sticker on my album.”24

In This Life

The album’s center of gravity is an elegy, and it was Pettibone’s idea. “Actually it was my idea to write a song for her friend Martin,” he said — Martin Burgoyne, her former roommate, who designed the sleeve for “Burning Up” and died of AIDS-related illness in November 1986, aged twenty-three. Pettibone had the chords when she walked in. She said they were beautiful and doubted they fit the record, then wrote the words in about fifteen minutes.25 Donna De Lory, singing behind her, had buried a friend of her own: “I think nowadays, people don’t really have a grip on what was going on at that time. And there was so much we didn’t talk about. But here she was, talking about it. It’s such a sad and beautiful song. Recording it, you know, it was somber. She didn’t really have to explain what the song was about; we just knew.”26 The song asks whether you have watched a best friend die. It prays for the dying to stop, then turns bitter about who decided any of this was a lesson worth teaching, over a sample of George Gershwin’s Prelude No. 2.27

What it inherits and what it introduces

The eight-track material went to twenty-four-track at fifteen inches per second with Dolby SR, chosen because, as Pettibone put it, records put you in the mix while compact discs move you a step away from it.28 The floor underneath is house, laid by a house producer working bass-first and analog. What it introduced was a register: spoken, deadpan, unaroused, applied to material about sex and refusing to sell it warm. Arion Berger heard that in November 1992 and said so plainly, describing an album that “doesn’t so much evoke sex as provide a fetishistic abstraction of it,” with production that “teases and then denies with the grim control of a dominatrix.”29

The book

Sex was published the day after the album. Steven Meisel shot it, Fabien Baron art-directed it, and it arrived aluminum-bound and sealed in Mylar at fifty dollars, with an initial print run of a million across five continents.30 The print run sold out, the reviews were contemptuous in a register nobody had prepared for, and the reviews of the book became, in practice, the reviews of the record. Pettibone had not been shown it: “She kept the book very secret from me. It probably would have been a bigger album without all the controversy.”31 Her own accounting does not include an apology for the book. “Most people want to hear me say I regret putting out my Sex book. I don’t,” she told Time. “What was problematic was putting out my Erotica album at the same time. I love that record, and it was overlooked.”32

What the reviews actually said

The lines that survived from 1992 came from the two worst reviews the album got; the verdicts with the most status behind them were positive, and nobody remembers them. Berger gave it four stars out of five in Rolling Stone, and the review that produced the reading quoted above closed on this: “Erotica is everything Madonna has been denounced for being — meticulous, calculated, domineering and artificial. It accepts those charges and answers with a brilliant record to prove them.”33 Robert Christgau graded it A, and framed his capsule as an exercise in pretending you had never heard of her: put the record on, hear the bass, notice that “the lyrics are not stupid.”34 Stephen Holden, in the New York Times, called it “far from Madonna’s best album” and her “most courageous,” comparing the Sex-and-Erotica gesture to Lennon and Ono’s bed-ins.35

The pans were real and quotable. David Browne’s Entertainment Weekly review, written as a mock bondage scene, delivered the line that has outlived it — “Erotica may be the most joyless dance music ever made” — while conceding in the same piece that she had not made club music this intense and bottom-heavy since her first album.36 Chris Willman in the Los Angeles Times gave it two stars out of four and coined the insult that stuck, predicting most listeners would “have trouble wading through the neurotica.”37

Commercially it broke a decade-long pattern. The album entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 with 167,000 copies and could not get past Garth Brooks. The RIAA certified it double platinum in January 1993, an award that counts units shipped; its scanned American sales had still not reached two million by 2016.38 “Erotica” reached No. 3 and “Deeper and Deeper” No. 7. Then “Bad Girl” stopped at No. 36, ending a run of twenty-seven consecutive American top-twenty singles that reached back to “Holiday.”39 Britain was steadier, taking five straight singles into the top ten.40

Influence and legacy

Sal Cinquemani, writing for Slant in 2007, put the album’s coldness where it belongs: its “irrefutable unsexiness probably says more about the sex=death mentality of the early ’90s than any other musical document of its time,” and the record is “Madonna at her most important, at her most relevant.”41 The frame that has stuck since is the one i-D’s Paul Flynn gave in 2017, arguing from “In This Life” outward: the song “gives the album a purpose and resonance beyond this simply being Madonna’s sex record next to her Sex book. Because of AIDS, sex was the news.”42 The record that got called the most joyless dance music ever made puts its elegy on side four, three songs after the one about oral sex, and lets the same flat voice carry both.
