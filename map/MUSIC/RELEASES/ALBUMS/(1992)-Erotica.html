Erotica is the fifth studio album by Madonna, released on October 19, 1992, in the United Kingdom and on October 20, 1992, in the United States by Maverick and Sire. It was the first album on Maverick, the label Warner Bros. had given her that April, and it appeared one day before Sex, a book of photographs by Steven Meisel that she had made alongside it. She recorded it between July 1991 and September 1992, mostly in the New York apartment of Shep Pettibone, who produced ten of the fourteen tracks; André Betts, who had co-produced “Justify My Love” (1990), produced the other four. The album is dance-pop built on house rhythm and on sampled hip hop and jazz records, and Madonna sings most of it in a low, flat, half-spoken register she attached to an alter ego called Dita. At seventy-five minutes it remains her longest album.

Erotica entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 and stayed on the chart for fifty-three weeks, the first of her studio albums since her 1983 debut that did not reach No. 1. Its title track reached No. 3 on the Hot 100 and “Deeper and Deeper” No. 7, after which “Bad Girl” stopped at No. 36 and ended a run of twenty-seven consecutive American top-twenty singles going back to “Holiday” (1983). Britain held up better, giving the album five straight top-ten singles. The reviews divided sharply and the two most hostile lines from them have outlasted the favorable verdicts, which included four stars from Arion Berger in Rolling Stone and an A from Robert Christgau. The book absorbed most of the coverage, a displacement Madonna has named as the album’s central misfortune ever since. Critics writing from 2007 onward have rated the record considerably higher than the contemporary press did, generally by reading its coldness as a response to AIDS rather than as a failure of warmth.

Background

Madonna finished 1990 as the most commercially successful woman in American popular music and spent 1991 in public. The Blond Ambition tour had run from April to August 1990; The Immaculate Collection followed that November and sold in the tens of millions; Truth or Dare, the documentary of the tour, opened in May 1991. “Justify My Love”, the new single attached to the compilation, reached No. 1 on the Hot 100 in January 1991 while MTV refused to air its video, which she then sold as a commercial videocassette. That single, spoken instead of sung over a slow programmed track, was co-produced by André Betts and became the model for a good deal of what followed.

The losses behind the record were six years old by then and they were specific. Martin Burgoyne had come to New York and been hired as a bartender by Steve Rubell; he was diagnosed in August 1986 and told Vanity Fair that autumn that he was in constant pain and figured he would either get better or he would not. On November 10 that year Madonna modeled a denim jacket he had painted at a benefit for St. Vincent’s Hospital, and he died twenty days later. What followed was a pattern and not an episode: “Live to Tell” dedicated to him at an AmFAR benefit at Madison Square Garden in 1987, the last American night of Blond Ambition turned into a memorial for Keith Haring that sent more than three hundred thousand dollars to AmFAR in 1990, and a card about AIDS inside every copy of Like a Prayer.1

Her business position changed in April 1992, when Warner Bros. and Time Warner signed her to a contract reported at sixty million dollars and created Maverick, a label and multimedia company in which she was a partner.2 Erotica was the first album Maverick released, and Sex was the first book, which was not a coincidence: the company she had built covered records, publishing, film, television, merchandising and books, so both products came out of one desk. Her partners were Freddy DeMann and Veronica Dashev, and the name was made out of the three of them. She described the arrangement at the time as a marriage of art and commerce and an artistic think tank, something between the Bauhaus and Warhol’s Factory: “it started as a desire to have more control. I want to incorporate them into my little factory of ideas.”3 She spent the summer of 1991 in Chicago and Evansville filming A League of Their Own, which opened in July 1992 and became the year’s most successful film about baseball; she began shooting Body of Evidence in the spring of 1992. The photographs for Sex were taken across 1992 in New York, Miami, and Los Angeles.

American radio in 1992 was moving away from the kind of record she had spent the previous decade making. In the weeks around Erotica’s release the top of the Billboard 200 went to Garth Brooks, Michael Bolton, Ice Cube, and Whitney Houston. What Madonna went toward instead was the club music of New York, which had been the source of her own first records and which Pettibone had been making and remixing throughout the decade. Five weeks before release she told the New York Times what she thought she had made, and the description matches the record better than any review of it did: the album would be “soulful, with a jazzy undertone and a lot of beatnik-style poetry in it.”4 She told Billboard in November 1992 that she had chosen Pettibone and Betts for the distance between them, describing two producers who “come from opposite ends of the spectrum in terms of their style and approach to music, but they’re both connected to the street and they’re still young and hungry.”5

Recording

The album began with a cassette. Pettibone flew to Chicago on July 8, 1991, where Madonna was filming, and handed her three tracks he had built with his assistant Tony Shimkin, at a point when neither producer knew an album was coming.6 She liked all three, and the work moved to his New York apartment and stayed there for the next fourteen months. He recorded the keyboards, bass lines, and vocals upstairs in his home studio, on an Oberheim OB8, a Korg M3, a Roland D-50, and an Akai S1000 sampler that supplied the record its horns and its Middle Eastern figures.7 Vocals went to an eight-track Tascam reel-to-reel, and almost all of them stayed, so that the demos became the album: he estimated that ninety-eight percent of the vocals cut in the apartment are the ones released.8

She rejected the first batch outright. What she objected to was the finish rather than the songs, and Pettibone recorded the exchange in the diary he later published: he had thought it sounded good because some tracks had a New York house sound and others an L.A. one, and she told him that if she had wanted an L.A. record she would have called Patrick Leonard.9 She wanted something that sounded, in his transcription of her, “as if it were recorded in an alley at 123rd street in Harlem.” He went back to what he called his usual style, bass-oriented and analog, and that is the version that shipped.10

The division of labor was fixed and narrow. Pettibone wrote the music and Madonna wrote the words, a border she had drawn at an earlier session by telling him, when he offered a lyric idea, that writing lyrics was what she did.11 She set the pace as well, arriving in the early afternoon, improvising a vocal in one or two passes, and growing impatient whenever the machines needed time. Shimkin, then twenty-one, once told her he was not finished and she could go make popcorn until he was; he took her mild reply as evidence that she valued not being flattered.12

Four versions of the title track

“Erotica” was rebuilt from nothing four times. Madonna would record a full vocal, harmonies and all, then decide to erase it and start again, which Pettibone described afterward with some regret about the tapes he had wiped.13 The hook that survived came out of his own record collection. He heard Kool & the Gang’s “Jungle Boogie” (1973) over the track, found the album, and rode it across what he already had; the horns on the finished record are that sample, played from the S1000.14 The track also carries a vocal by the Lebanese singer Fairuz, taken from a religious song traditionally sung on Good Friday. She said she had not consented to its use, sued, and settled out of court, and both the single and the album were banned in Lebanon.15 The voice arrived last of all. Pettibone told her the book she was shooting with Meisel was full of material the song could use; she took it out of the room and came back half an hour later speaking into the microphone in a dry, flat register that was not quite her own. Shimkin dated the character’s arrival to those overdubs, and said the character then took the record over.16

That appetite for an assumed voice shaped smaller decisions too. On “Bye Bye Baby” the telephone filter was printed to tape as she sang so that it could not be removed afterward, which Shimkin said changed the performance because she was hearing the processed voice as she delivered it.17 On “Deeper and Deeper” she wanted a flamenco guitar in the middle of a Philly house track, and Pettibone argued against putting “La Isla Bonita” inside it and lost.18 Shimkin, who played the figure, remembered Pettibone giving in and then committing, which is where the castanets came from.19 The “Vogue” line she sings in the last minute is there because Pettibone had been singing it over the track and she copied him.20

Betts recorded his four songs elsewhere and to a different brief. Madonna told him to avoid anywhere reputable, which produced a room he described as a hole in the wall, and a session interrupted by a rat crossing the floor.21 “Where Life Begins”, the first thing they wrote, is a song about oral sex that states its subject in the opening line; Betts said he was not surprised by the explicitness, on the reasoning that she was Madonna and could say what she wanted.22 “Did You Do It?” was an accident. Freestyling over the instrumental of “Waiting” while she was out at dinner, Betts had someone in the room shout the question and answered it himself, then played her the result as a joke. She kept it, and it is the reason the album carries the first Parental Advisory sticker of her career.23

The album’s elegy was Pettibone’s idea. He proposed writing something for Martin Burgoyne, her former roommate, who had designed the sleeve for “Burning Up” and died of AIDS-related illness in November 1986 at twenty-three. He had the chords when she arrived; she said they were beautiful, doubted they belonged on the record, and wrote the words in about fifteen minutes.24 Donna De Lory, singing behind her, had buried a friend of her own, and described the session as somber and the subject as one nobody had to explain.25 The eight-track material was transferred to twenty-four-track at fifteen inches per second with Dolby SR, a decision Pettibone justified on the grounds that records place the listener inside the mix and compact discs move the listener a step away from it.26

Music

Erotica is dance-pop with a house floor, made by a house producer working bass-first and mostly analog, and it borrows from hip hop, jazz, reggae, and disco across its fourteen tracks. Pettibone produced ten of them and Betts four, and the difference is audible: the Pettibone tracks are programmed, synthetic, and built for clubs, while the Betts tracks are slower, jazzier, and closer to the sampled loops of contemporary hip hop. Published sheet music exists for only three songs and gives 120 beats per minute for “Erotica” in F-sharp minor, 120 for “Deeper and Deeper” in G minor, and 109 for “Bye Bye Baby” in the same key.27 Berger, reviewing it in Rolling Stone, described a record that was “stunningly reined in” and that cut short every opportunity for a crescendo or an instrumental break.28 At seventy-five minutes it is Madonna’s longest album, and Harry Tafoya wrote in Pitchfork that it is structured like a mix rather than a sequence of songs.29

The most remarked feature is the voice. Madonna sings much of the album low, dry, and close to speech, and every substantial review of 1992 has a line about it. Stephen Holden, in the New York Times, identified it as the voice from the introduction to “Justify My Love” extended across a whole album, and heard the record as a dialogue between that register and the bright one from her early singles.30 Richard Harrington, in the Washington Post, found the effect tedious and noted that on “Deeper and Deeper,” “Why’s It So Hard,” and “In This Life” she works at the bottom of a limited range.31 Giles Smith, in The Independent, made the opposite case and treated the exposure as a production decision, arguing that a voice normally doubled and layered is here left unsupported and gains a cabaret quality from the isolation.32 Sal Cinquemani, writing in 2007, accepted the flatness and reversed its meaning, suggesting that sounding as though she had a cold added to the record’s closeness.33

Four samples are credited on the sleeve. “Erotica” carries Kool & the Gang’s “Jungle Boogie” and the Fairuz vocal; “Bye Bye Baby” takes a hook from LL Cool J’s “Jingling Baby” (1990); and “In This Life” is built over George Gershwin’s Prelude No. 2 (1926). Two further borrowings are from her own catalog: “Waiting” samples “Justify My Love,” and “Deeper and Deeper” quotes the lyric of “Vogue” near its end.34 “Fever” is a cover of the Little Willie John song written by Eddie Cooley and Otis Blackwell, and it uses the additional lyrics Peggy Lee wrote for her 1958 recording without crediting her.35

The songs are mostly about sex, and the ones that are not are about grief, the press, and death. The title track is a domination scene delivered in the Dita voice over maraca percussion and the sampled horns. “Fever” is a house remake at club tempo. “Bye Bye Baby” ends a relationship in an electronically altered chirp. “Deeper and Deeper” is the album’s disco track and its most euphoric, with the flamenco guitar and castanets arriving in the middle. “Where Life Begins” and “Did You Do It?” are the album’s most explicit, the first sultry and the second a straight hip hop track in the style Harrington compared to Q-Tip and Black Sheep. “Bad Girl” is a slow, orchestrated ballad about self-destruction, and Berger and J.D. Considine both singled it out. “Waiting” sets a spoken lyric about being kept waiting against a hard drum break. “Thief of Hearts” is theatrical and carries Madonna’s first outright rap. “Words” answers her critics over a deep house beat and typewriter effects. “Rain” is a synthesizer ballad and the album’s warmest song. “Why’s It So Hard” is a plea for tolerance over a reggae-derived groove that Chuck Arnold traced to Soul II Soul as much as to Jamaica. “In This Life” is the elegy for Martin Burgoyne that names her ballet teacher Christopher Flynn in its lyric and is heard by most carriers as mourning Keith Haring in the same breath, which asks whether the listener has watched a friend die, prays for the dying to stop, and turns bitter about who decided this was a lesson worth teaching. “Secret Garden” closes the album as a jazz-house track with a distant saxophone, and Cinquemani called it the record’s sexiest song.36

Title and artwork

No account of where the album’s title came from, or when it was chosen, has been published. The alter ego is better documented. Dita is drawn from the German actress Dita Parlo, who worked in French and German film in the 1930s, and it was the name Madonna used checking into hotels.37 The artwork was photographed by Steven Meisel and art-directed by Baron & Baron, the firm of Fabien Baron, with Siung Fat Tjia; the same team made Sex, and the album’s images come from those sessions.38 Two configurations shipped: a thirteen-track version omitting “Did You Do It?” and the complete fourteen-track version with the Parental Advisory sticker.39 The reason was retail, not taste. André Betts remembered her calling him back to blame him for it — the sticker meant Kmart would not carry the record, or even stock it — and Doug Wimbish put the same calculation from the other side, parents handing over ten dollars and stopping to think. The consequence outlived the argument: the edition that streams by default today is the clean thirteen-track one, so the album most people now hear is not the album that was reviewed in 1992.40 A picture disc of the “Erotica” twelve-inch was pressed in Britain with the toe-sucking photograph from Sex on its face and withdrawn before it reached shops, most of the run going back to the plant to be destroyed; Warner finally issued it on October 21, 2022, for the song’s thirtieth anniversary, with its three tracks unchanged.41

What it inherits and what it introduces

Alfred Soto placed it at the end of something instead of the start of a decline: 1992, he wrote, “was the last year that AIDS, in scaring the crap out of gay dance culture, turned club tracks into headier and sleazier versions of themselves.”42 The record’s first inheritance is New York club music. Pettibone had been a mixer at WBLS and a remixer for a decade before this, and the album’s rhythm section, its deep house tracks, and its bass-first construction come from that work rather than from pop production; “Vogue” (1990) had already brought it into her records. The second is hip hop, by way of Betts and “Justify My Love”: the four Betts tracks are loop-based and sample-derived, and “Did You Do It?” is a rap record. The third is the standards repertoire, which “Fever” enters through Peggy Lee.

Two things were new at release. The first is the register. A flat, unaroused, half-spoken delivery applied across a full album, on material about sex, refuses the warmth the subject usually carries, and Berger identified this in November 1992 as the record’s organizing decision, describing an album that “doesn’t so much evoke sex as provide a fetishistic abstraction of it” and production that “teases and then denies with the grim control of a dominatrix.”43 The second is the subject matter. “In This Life” put an AIDS elegy on a mainstream pop album at a moment when few did, and it names the illness and the anger rather than gesturing at them.

Release

Erotica was released on October 19, 1992, in the United Kingdom and October 20 in the United States, on compact disc and cassette, on vinyl, and on Philips’ new Digital Compact Cassette, which had reached shops weeks earlier. Sex was published the following day by Warner Books: aluminum-bound, sealed in Mylar, fifty dollars, with an initial print run of a million copies across five continents.44 The print run sold out and the reviews were contemptuous, and because the two arrived together the reviews of the book functioned as the reviews of the record. There was almost no conventional promotion, because none was needed: a party for the book at Industria Superstudio on October 15 with invitations signed Dita and Madonna arriving as Little Bo Peep, an MTV special hosted by Kurt Loder a week later that took the book to a sex therapist and to working dominatrices and filmed a booth at HMV where a dollar bought a minute with a copy, and a Vogue cover. Pettibone’s own account of the party ends on the thing everyone else had stopped discussing: “For all the multimedia extravaganzas that were braying for her attention, it was still the music that mattered and it was the record that we fawned over.”45 Pettibone had not been shown it, and said afterward that the album would have been bigger without the surrounding controversy.46 The week’s retail reports show the swallowing in miniature: a Chicago shop had sold thirty-six copies of the album by Thursday afternoon and a three-hundred-store chain in Georgia eleven per store in a day and a half, while one book chain moved twenty-two thousand copies of Sex nationally in a single day.47

Five singles were issued, not all of them everywhere. “Erotica” came first, in Australia and most of Europe on September 29, 1992, and in the United States on October 13; its video, directed by Fabien Baron from Super 8 footage he had shot during the Sex sessions, premiered on MTV at midnight, was restricted to post-watershed play, was pulled after three airings, and returned seventeen days later behind a content warning.48 “Deeper and Deeper” followed in November, “Bad Girl” in February 1993 with a video directed by David Fincher and starring Christopher Walken, and “Fever” in late March, outside North America only. “Rain” was the last, in July 1993. “Bye Bye Baby” was issued in mainland Europe, Australasia, and Japan in November 1993, and never in Britain or the United States. The later videos are the campaign’s strongest work and the note of it that survives. Mark Romanek shot “Rain” at Santa Monica Airport with Ryuichi Sakamoto playing the director inside it, after Godard and Fellini both declined; it took best art direction and best cinematography at the 1993 Video Music Awards and Romanek director of the year at Billboard’s, which is the only hardware the era produced. Stéphane Sednaoui made “Fever” in Miami in April 1993, her first blue-screen video, in silver body paint, on a brief from Maverick that asked for another kind of Madonna, more pop and more club. Bobby Woods shot “Deeper and Deeper” at Ren-Mar with her as Edie Sedgwick and a cast that included Udo Kier, Sofia Coppola, Chi Chi LaRue and Seymour Stein.49

Madonna promoted the album on Saturday Night Live in January 1993 and toured it on the Girlie Show, thirty-nine dates between September 25 and December 19, 1993, which Billboard reported grossing seventy million dollars.50 The tour was the album’s defense: eight of its songs across seventeen numbers, the whole first act but for “Vogue,” and two songs — “Why’s It So Hard” and “In This Life” — that were never singles anywhere and were performed nowhere else. The itinerary was built to avoid the country that had turned on her. She said she was not interested in preaching to the converted and wanted to start where she had the most enemies, and the schedule bears it out: three American cities in thirty-nine dates. In Puerto Rico the governor said he personally repudiated her placing the flag between her legs and that the people repudiated it too; in Argentina a cardinal called her blasphemous and pornographic and asked the president not to receive her, and a bishop who had failed to get the shows banned recommended that any practicing Catholic who attended go to confession the next day.51

Reception

The reviews were mixed, and they divided on whether the coldness was the point. The two verdicts with the most institutional weight were positive and are the ones least often quoted. Berger gave the album four stars out of five in Rolling Stone and closed by granting every charge made against Madonna and answering it: the record is “everything Madonna has been denounced for being — meticulous, calculated, domineering and artificial. It accepts those charges and answers with a brilliant record to prove them.”52 Christgau graded it A and framed his capsule as an exercise in pretending you had never heard of her, in which case you would hear the bass first and then notice that “the lyrics are not stupid.”53 Holden called it “far from Madonna’s best album” and her “most courageous,” and compared the Sex-and-Erotica gesture to Lennon and Ono’s bed-ins.54

The pans were more quotable and have lasted longer. David Browne’s Entertainment Weekly review, written as a mock bondage scene, produced the line that has outlived every other sentence written about the record, calling it possibly “the most joyless dance music ever made,” while conceding in the same piece that she had not made club music this intense since her first album.55 Chris Willman gave it two stars out of four in the Los Angeles Times and coined the other surviving insult, predicting that most listeners would have trouble wading through “the neurotica.”56

Commercially the record broke a ten-year pattern. It entered the Billboard 200 at No. 2 on the chart dated November 7, 1992, with 167,000 first-week copies, held off the top by Garth Brooks in the fifth week of The Chase, and fell to No. 4 the following week; it stayed on the chart for fifty-three consecutive weeks and finished 1993 at No. 61 on the year-end list. The RIAA certified it double platinum on January 6, 1993, an award that counts units shipped, and its scanned American sales stood at 1,905,000 as of December 2016. The album and its singles drew no Grammy nomination in any category.57 In Britain it entered at No. 2, held that position for three weeks, and spent thirty-eight weeks in the top seventy-five across three runs in 1992 and 1993. Worldwide sales are commonly given as more than six million, a figure assembled from national shipment certifications and trade estimates rather than any audit.58

Legacy

Barry Walters made the case at length in Rolling Stone in 2017, writing as a gay man out of the same New York clubs she had come from, thirty years after his first lover died. Erotica, he wrote, is “Madonna’s concept album about love and intimacy under the shadow of plague,” and the complaint of 1992 was the point: “The frustration of Erotica that critics of the era bemoaned, we applauded because it was our own.” His closing verdict is the one the record’s defenders have used since — that she took what was marginalized at the worst of the epidemic, placed it in an emancipated context, and shoved it into the mainstream for everyone to see and hear.59

The reassessment began in 2007, with Cinquemani in Slant, who located the album’s coldness in its moment and not in its maker, arguing that its “irrefutable unsexiness probably says more about the sex=death mentality of the early ’90s than any other musical document of its time,” and calling the record Madonna “at her most important, at her most relevant.”60 The frame that has held since is the one Paul Flynn set out in i-D in 2017, reading the album outward from “In This Life”: the song “gives the album a purpose and resonance beyond this simply being Madonna’s sex record next to her Sex book. Because of AIDS, sex was the news.”61 Tafoya’s Pitchfork retrospective took the same view of the structure, treating the length and the sequencing as deliberate, and set against it the case that Madonna had taken more from ballroom and house culture than she returned. He quotes bell hooks, writing in 1992, on Madonna’s position as an outsider who could “colonize and appropriate black experience for her own opportunistic ends,” and DJ Sprinkles, on Midtown 120 Blues (2008), on watching Madonna earn from voguing while the queen who taught it to her sat broke at the next table.62 Eric Henderson stated the paradox most precisely in Slant: the irony is not lost on gay listeners that the album where Madonna came closest to giving herself over to a queer point of view was the step too far for straight pop audiences. The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame took the same view institutionally in 2017, running the record in a series on albums that altered the music world and arguing that few women artists before or since have been so outspoken about their own desires.63 The rehabilitation is not unanimous, and the strongest dissent is the one printed in a reference book, not a magazine: Rob Sheffield gave it four out of ten in the Spin Alternative Record Guide in 1995, and never withdrew it. No musician outside the record’s own credits has been found naming it as an influence. Where the record sits can be measured instead of asserted, and the two measures disagree. It appears on no edition of Rolling Stone’s five hundred greatest albums, in 2020 or 2023, while inside her own catalog the rankings have climbed steadily: tenth of twelve at NME in 2011, fifth to eighth across three lists in 2015, and sixth, fourth, third and second from 2019 onward, reaching No. 2 in Slant’s 2026 staff ranking behind only Ray of Light. It has been rehabilitated within a critical subculture and within its artist’s own body of work, without being readmitted to the general canon.64 The plainest evidence of its standing is the stage. The Celebration Tour of 2023 and 2024, a career retrospective built to canonize, carried “Erotica,” “Bad Girl,” “Rain,” a snatch of “Fever,” “In This Life” as an intermission piece and the non-album B-side “Up Down Suite” inside its ballroom sequence, which makes the 1992 album one of the most heavily represented records in the show. It has still never been given an expanded edition, while Bedtime Stories got one in 2025.65

Madonna’s own position has not moved. She has never apologized for the book, and told Time that what was a mistake was releasing the album alongside it; she called the record one she loves and one that was overlooked.66 What she has said elsewhere is less forgiving of the moment than of the work. To Spin four years afterward she drew the line at the book — her fantasy, and she made money from it, which she called a no-no — and described what the epoch taught her: called a whore and a witch, compared in one headline to Satan, while Prince was running around in fishnets and heels with nothing said, which was the first time she understood that women do not have the same freedom as men. She said she felt like the most hated woman in the world.67
