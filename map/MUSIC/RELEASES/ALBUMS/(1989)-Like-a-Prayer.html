Album

Like a Prayer

Madonna
ReleasedMarch 21, 1989 (US); March 20, 1989 (Europe)
LabelSire / Warner Bros.
RecordedSeptember 1988 – January 1989
ProducerMadonna, Patrick Leonard, Stephen Bray, Prince
StudioJohnny Yuma Recording (Burbank), Saturn Sound (Sherman Oaks), Ocean Way (Hollywood), Paisley Park (Chanhassen)
Genres
Primary
Pop
ListenRYM·Apple Music·Spotify

The record announced itself by smell. First pressings were scented with patchouli oil, and the critic Lucy O’Brien’s account of March 1989 begins at the mailbox: “I open a large record envelope and a waft of patchouli oil hits my nostrils. Inside is the new Madonna album.”1 Warner ran the scent through the campaign, printing a patchouli-soaked photograph of her bare midriff in Billboard under the slogans “It Shall Be Released” and “Lead Us Into Temptation.”2 More than two million dollars went into selling a record about her dead mother, her father, and her Catholicism.3

The divorce album

Patrick Leonard named it: “We called it the divorce album. Everything took four or times longer to do as she kept breaking down in the studio. It was a hard time for her.”4 She had made no records through most of 1988, taken a Broadway role and been panned for it, turned thirty — the age her mother was when she died — and gone through the collapse of her marriage to Sean Penn, with a divorce filing in January 1989.5 Leonard’s memory of the room is plain: “I remember some days she wore sunglasses all day in the studio. She was going through very hard times.”6 The album is dedicated to her mother’s memory, and its cover, shot by Herb Ritts, is the first of her career not to show her face: hips, hands, beads, jeans. O’Brien reads it as a nod to the mother, a devout Catholic who covered the family’s Sacred Heart statue whenever a woman came to the house in zip-up jeans.7

She had co-produced True Blue with the same two men, and she settled the credit question in one gesture. “We argued a lot during the recording sessions,” Leonard said. “But one day she held up the True Blue album cover and said: ‘Whose picture is that?’ The more feisty conversations stopped there!”8

The room

The write-it-in-a-day method was True Blue’s, carried over intact. Leonard would build a track at the piano in the morning, she would arrive late and write a lyric to it, and the take she sang that afternoon was usually the one that shipped. Her own account of the first-take rule: “A lot of the vocals we kept were the first takes. They were a lot more spontaneous and emotional and integral to the music.”9 What was new was what she put into it. To SongTalk: “I didn’t try to candycoat anything or make it more palatable for mass consumption. I wrote what I felt… Because this was what was coming out of me.”10 Stephen Bray took two songs on his own terms, working in a more kinetic, dance-floor register while Leonard chased melody; Prince took one.11

The often-repeated claim that this was her first record with live musicians is her own, and narrower than the paraphrase: “I’ve worked with live musicians before, but this is the first time we were all together in one room.”12 “Oh Father” took persuading. Leonard filled the room with a band and had to talk her into it. “I had nine people in the room,” he said. “Madonna said: ‘Why all these people, does it make a difference? Do we have to do this?’ I said: ‘It’s not going to be painful, it’ll be fun.‘”13 “Promise to Try,” her song about her mother’s death, went the opposite way and lost the room entirely: “I played the piano and she sang. She was right at my shoulder, next to the piano, with no headphones. The record button got pushed twice.”14

The choir

The title song was written first, and its architecture is a subtraction. She wanted the instruments stripped back to a voice and an organ, then a choir on top, and the arrangement’s original bongos and Latin percussion came off to make room.15 Andraé Crouch brought the choir, and vetted the lyrics before agreeing: “It’s a song that explores the word ‘prayer’,” he said. “Madonna wanted something very churchy, so I tried to blow up what she did and make it as powerful as I could.”16 What she was chasing in the room was less doctrinal. She told everyone present to surrender themselves to passionate abandon, and Guy Pratt, whose improvised bass figure runs through the track, got the message directly: “It was an out-of-body experience. As I was playing Madonna was going: ‘Guy, more! More!’ By the fade I had run out of licks and had to go back to the beginning again.”17 Leonard heard the chorus’s central line as a description of oral sex, and asked her to take it out. She refused, and the finished song keeps the ambiguity between religious rapture and the other kind.18

The one song built with Prince is the album’s least defended track. Madonna and Prince cut material at Paisley Park, finished “Love Song” by mailing tapes, and got a track O’Brien calls “one of the album’s weaker moments.” Bray was asked in and turned it down: “I am the biggest Prince fan, but to be honest I was too shaken. This was before I discovered beta blockers – I wasn’t emotionally ready. It’s one of my few regrets in life that I said: ‘Nah, you guys don’t need my help.‘”19

What it inherits and what it introduces

A working gospel choir, singing church changes, on a number-one pop single, with the organ and the hand claps left audible: the debt is paid in full rather than gestured at.20 What the album introduced was a permission. A pop star at maximum commercial altitude made a record about her dead mother, her father, her failed marriage and her religion, and her audience followed her into it — the chart evidence is at note 36.21 “Express Yourself”, Bray’s, put the argument in the imperative and kept it there. “We didn’t invent feminism, but this was a call to action,” he said. “Madonna is an incredible example of a woman saying: ‘I’m doing this my way.‘”22

Pepsi

In January 1989 Pepsi signed her for five million dollars: a commercial built on the unreleased title song, plus sponsorship of her next world tour.23 Her reasoning was not defensive. “I consider it a challenge to make a commercial that has some sort of artistic value. I like the challenge of merging art and commerce,” she said, adding that a video is a commercial too and that labels cannot buy publicity on that scale.24 Pepsi’s advertising head Alan Pottasch put the company’s half plainly: the campaign “will put Pepsi first and foremost in consumer’s minds.”25 The two-minute spot, “Make a Wish,” was directed by Joe Pytka and reached an estimated 250 million viewers, with Madonna watching home movies of her own childhood birthday party and trading places with the girl in them.26

Mary Lambert’s video for the song premiered on MTV within a day of the spot, and the two properties were nothing alike. Its plot is a courtroom errand: Madonna witnesses a murder, watches a Black man wrongly arrested for it, and finally testifies to free him, the whole thing framed by a cast curtain call at the end. What drew the objections was everything in between — the stigmata on her hands, the field of burning crosses she dances before, the Black saint she kisses.27 The American Family Association called the video offensive. The commercial leverage lay with the sponsor, so that is where the pressure went, and Pepsi pulled the campaign while letting her keep the fee.28 Her line at the time: “art should be controversial, and that’s all there is to it.”29 That September the video won Viewer’s Choice at the MTV Video Music Awards, whose broadcast Pepsi sponsored, and she used the podium to thank them “for causing so much controversy.”30

Her own telling has tidied since. Posting in 2023, when Pepsi finally re-aired the spot, she wrote that the commercial “was immediately canceled when I refused to change any scenes in the video where I was kissing a black saint or burning crosses,” and that “artists are here to disturb the peace.”31 The record is messier than the memory: the campaign ran for weeks before it was pulled, and no contemporaneous account has Pepsi asking her to recut anything.

The reception

Rolling Stone’s J.D. Considine called it “far and away the most self-consciously serious album she’s made,” found her “brutally frank about the dissolution of her marriage,” and warned readers off expecting the usual: “Like a Prayer is more interested in exorcising demons than entertaining fans.”32 He heard the title track open “with a sudden blast of stun-gun guitar” and judged the Leonard collaborations “stunning in their breadth and achievement.” “Dear Jessie” he singled out for its “kaleidoscopic Sgt. Pepper-isms.”33 On “Till Death Do Us Part” — a portrait of a marriage curdling into damage, sung in a first person the review declines to treat as fictional — he wrote that it takes its wedding-vow title almost mockingly.34 Robert Christgau graded it B+.35

The numbers matched the notices. The album reached No. 1 on the Billboard 200 and held six weeks across a seventy-seven-week run, entered the UK chart at No. 1 and stayed on it seventy-three weeks, and has sold somewhere above fifteen million copies worldwide.36 It was her third consecutive American No. 1 album, after Like a Virgin and True Blue, and it climbed fastest of the three.37 “Like a Prayer” became her seventh American No. 1 single, three weeks at the top; “Cherish” and “Express Yourself” both stopped at No. 2. Then “Oh Father” broke the run, peaking at No. 20 and ending a streak of sixteen consecutive American top-five singles, before “Keep It Together” restored the altitude at No. 8.38

Influence and legacy

The album is the hinge in the standard account of her career, the point at which the argument shifted from whether she was any good to what she was going to make next. Considine’s closing verdict, that the record proved “hers is one of the most compelling voices of the Eighties,” was written in 1989 and has aged into consensus.39 The smaller thing in the sleeve has aged better still. The Christian organizations had been pressuring Pepsi over the video for weeks by the time the album itself reached American shops on March 21, arguing with a record they had not heard — one that shipped with a card telling millions of teenagers that AIDS is “an equal opportunity disease” and that people who have it “deserve compassion and support,” the first such insert to accompany a mainstream commercial release.40
