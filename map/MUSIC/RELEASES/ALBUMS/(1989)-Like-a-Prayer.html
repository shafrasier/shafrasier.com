Like a Prayer is the fourth studio album by Madonna, released on March 21, 1989, by Sire. She wrote and produced it with Patrick Leonard and Stephen Bray between September 1988 and January 1989, on the one-a-day method the two of them had settled on True Blue, with one song written and recorded with Prince. Its subjects are her mother’s death, her father, her Catholicism and the collapse of her marriage to Sean Penn, and Leonard’s name for it while they were making it was the divorce album.

It reached No. 1 in America and held six weeks of a seventy-seven-week run, entered the British chart at No. 1, and has sold somewhere above fifteen million copies. The title song became her seventh American No. 1, and its video, in which she kisses a Black saint and dances before burning crosses, cost her a five-million-dollar Pepsi campaign within weeks of signing it. The reviews were the most serious of her career to that point and have aged into consensus: it is the record that shifted the argument from whether she was any good to what she would make next.

Background

She had made no records through most of 1988. She took a Broadway role in Speed-the-Plow and was panned for it, turned thirty, which was the age her mother had been when she died, and watched her marriage collapse; she filed for divorce in January 1989.1 The album is dedicated to her mother’s memory. She had co-produced True Blue with the same two men, and settled any remaining question about who was in charge in one gesture: after an argument in the sessions she held up the True Blue sleeve and asked whose picture it was, and the feistier conversations stopped.2

In January 1989 Pepsi signed her for five million dollars for a commercial built on the unreleased title song and sponsorship of her next tour.3 Her reasoning was not defensive: she called it a challenge to make a commercial with some artistic value, said she liked merging art and commerce, and pointed out that a video is a commercial too and that no label could buy publicity on that scale.4

Recording

The write-it-in-a-day method was True Blue’s, carried over intact. Leonard would build a track at the piano in the morning, she would arrive late and write a lyric to it, and the take she sang that afternoon was usually the one that shipped; by her own account many of the kept vocals are first takes, and she thought them more spontaneous for it.5 What was new was what she put into them. She told SongTalk that she had not tried to sugar-coat anything or make it more palatable for mass consumption, and had written what she felt because that was what was coming out of her.6

Leonard named the sessions for what they were. “We called it the divorce album,” he said. “Everything took four or times longer to do as she kept breaking down in the studio.” He remembered days when she wore sunglasses in the studio from morning to night.7 Bray took two songs on his own terms, working in a more kinetic dance-floor register while Leonard chased melody.8

The often-repeated claim that this was her first record with live musicians is her own and narrower than the paraphrase: she had worked with live players before, but this was the first time they were all in one room together.9 “Oh Father” took persuading; Leonard filled the room with nine people and had to talk her into it, promising it would not be painful.10 “Promise to Try,” her song about her mother’s death, went the other way and lost the room entirely: Leonard played piano, she sang at his shoulder with no headphones, and the record button was pushed twice.11

Building the title track

The title song was written first, and its architecture is a subtraction. She wanted the instruments stripped back to a voice and an organ with a choir on top, and the arrangement’s original bongos and Latin percussion came off to make room.12 Andraé Crouch brought the choir and vetted the lyrics before agreeing, saying she had wanted something very churchy and he had tried to make it as powerful as he could.13 What she was chasing in the room was less doctrinal. She told everyone present to surrender to passionate abandon, and Guy Pratt, whose improvised bass figure runs through the track, described an out-of-body experience in which she called for more until he ran out of licks and started his part again from the beginning.14 Leonard heard the chorus’s central line as a description of oral sex and asked her to take it out. She refused, and the song keeps the ambiguity.15

The one song built with Prince is the album’s least defended. They cut material at Paisley Park and finished “Love Song” by mailing tapes back and forth. Bray was asked in and turned it down, and has called it one of his few regrets.16

Music

Like a Prayer is a pop record, and the word every carrier agrees on is the plain one. Its texture is what Kenneth Partridge later described as classic psychedelic rock bent into then-current synth-pop: real drums and guitars and an organ, sitting inside programmed arrangements, with strings and a choir where the songs call for them.17 Published arrangements exist for five of the eleven tracks. The title song is in D minor at 120 beats per minute, “Express Yourself” also at 120, “Cherish” at 130 in D major, and “Dear Jessie” at 120 in D major in common time, shifting into 3/4 after its second chorus.18

Six of the eleven songs leave pop behind in a nameable direction. The title track is gospel, with a working church choir singing church changes and the organ and hand claps left audible on a number-one pop single. “Express Yourself” and “Keep It Together” are funk and soul records, Bray’s two. “Dear Jessie” and “Oh Father” are baroque pop, the first with the kaleidoscopic orchestration J.D. Considine compared to Sgt. Pepper. “Cherish” is a doo-wop shuffle. “Pray for Spanish Eyes” is a Latin ballad, and “Act of Contrition” is not a song at all but a studio collage, a backward tape of the choir with a recited prayer over it.19

The sleeve assigns no instrument to any track, only writer and producer, so the per-track personnel that circulate come from later sources rather than from the jacket.20 What the publishing does show is how closed the room was: three publishers across eleven songs, Leonard’s, Bray’s and Prince’s, and nobody else.21

The singing is the least fixed of any of her records. She moves between a girlish upper register and a chest voice she had not used much before, and she lets takes stay rough where the lyric is rough. The lyrics are the most directly autobiographical she had written: a daughter addressing a dead mother, a child addressing a father who could not show affection, a wife describing a marriage curdling into damage on “Till Death Do Us Part”, and, in the title song, a devotion held deliberately between religious rapture and the other kind.

Title and artwork

The cover, shot by Herb Ritts, is the first of her career not to show her face: hips, hands, beads and jeans. Lucy O’Brien reads it as a nod to the mother, a devout Catholic who covered the family’s Sacred Heart statue whenever a woman came to the house in zip-up jeans.22 First pressings were scented with patchouli oil, and Warner ran the scent through the campaign, printing a patchouli-soaked photograph of her bare midriff in Billboard under the slogans “It Shall Be Released” and “Lead Us Into Temptation.” More than two million dollars went into selling a record about her dead mother, her father and her Catholicism.23

What it inherits and what it introduces

The gospel debt is paid in full rather than gestured at: a working choir, church changes and a church arrangement, on a pop single that went to No. 1. The method is True Blue’s, unchanged. What the album introduced was a permission. A pop star at maximum commercial altitude made a record about her dead mother, her father, her failed marriage and her religion, and her audience followed her into it. “Express Yourself” put the argument in the imperative and kept it there; Bray’s account is that they did not invent feminism but that the song was a call to action.24

Release

Like a Prayer was released on March 21, 1989. The Pepsi commercial, a two-minute spot called “Make a Wish” directed by Joe Pytka, aired first and reached an estimated 250 million viewers, with Madonna watching home movies of her own childhood birthday party and trading places with the girl in them.25 Mary Lambert’s video for the song premiered on MTV within a day of it, and the two were nothing alike. Its plot is a courtroom errand: Madonna witnesses a murder, watches a Black man wrongly arrested for it, and testifies to free him. What drew the objections was everything in between, the stigmata on her hands, the field of burning crosses she dances before, and the Black saint she kisses.26

The American Family Association called the video offensive, and because the commercial leverage lay with the sponsor, that is where the pressure went. Pepsi pulled the campaign and let her keep the fee.27 Her line at the time was that art should be controversial and that was all there was to it, and that September the video won Viewer’s Choice at the MTV Video Music Awards, whose broadcast Pepsi sponsored; she used the podium to thank them for causing so much controversy.28 Her own telling has tidied since. Posting in 2023, when Pepsi finally re-aired the spot, she wrote that the commercial was canceled immediately when she refused to change the scenes with the saint and the crosses. The record is messier than the memory: the campaign ran for weeks before it was pulled, and no contemporaneous account has Pepsi asking her to recut anything.29

Five singles followed the album and a sixth went only to Britain. “Like a Prayer” came in March, “Express Yourself” in May with a video by David Fincher, “Cherish” in August with one by Herb Ritts, “Oh Father” in October in America only, “Dear Jessie” in December in Britain and Europe only, and “Keep It Together” in January 1990 in America and Australia.30

Reception

The reviews were the most serious she had received. J.D. Considine, in Rolling Stone, called it far and away the most self-consciously serious album she had made, found her brutally frank about the dissolution of her marriage, and warned readers that the record was more interested in exorcising demons than entertaining fans. He heard the title track open on a sudden blast of stun-gun guitar, judged the Leonard collaborations stunning in their breadth, and singled out “Dear Jessie” for its kaleidoscopic Sgt. Pepper-isms.31 Stephen Holden reviewed it for the New York Times two days before release under the headline “Madonna Re-Creates Herself — Again,” and Robert Christgau graded it B+.32

The numbers matched the notices. The album reached No. 1 on the Billboard 200 and held six weeks across a seventy-seven-week run, entered the British chart at No. 1 and stayed sixty-five weeks. It was her third consecutive American No. 1 album and it climbed fastest of the three. “Like a Prayer” became her seventh American No. 1 single with three weeks at the top; “Cherish” and “Express Yourself” both stopped at No. 2. Then “Oh Father” broke the run at No. 20, ending a streak of sixteen consecutive American top-five singles, before “Keep It Together” restored the altitude at No. 8.33

Legacy

The album is the hinge in the standard account of her career, the point at which the argument shifted from whether she was any good to what she was going to make next. Considine’s closing verdict in 1989, that hers was one of the most compelling voices of the eighties, has aged into consensus.34

The smaller thing in the sleeve has aged better still. The Christian organizations had been pressuring Pepsi over the video for weeks by the time the album reached American shops, arguing with a record they had not heard. It shipped with a card telling millions of teenagers that AIDS is an equal-opportunity disease and that people who have it deserve compassion and support, the first such insert to accompany a mainstream commercial release.35
