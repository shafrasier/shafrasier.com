Presenting the Fabulous Ronettes Featuring Veronica is the only studio album the Ronettes made, issued by Philles in November 1964 as the fourth LP the label had ever put out. It collects the singles the group cut at Gold Star Studios with Phil Spector from 1963 onward and fills out the running time with songs written to order, and its twelve tracks are held together by one voice and one production method rather than by any idea about what an album might be.

It failed. The record entered Billboard’s album chart the week after Christmas at 137, climbed for four weeks to 96, held that for a week and was gone eighteen days later, never having reached the upper half of a chart that ran 150 positions deep. In Britain it did not chart at all. Two of its songs had sold in the millions as singles. That gap between the singles and the album they were collected on is the fact this record is made of, and in 1964 it was the ordinary condition of a pop album rather than a verdict on this one.

Background

The group was three people from Spanish Harlem and Washington Heights who had been singing together since they were children. Estelle Bennett, born in 1941, was the older sister; Veronica, born in 1943, the younger; Nedra Talley, born in 1946, their cousin.1 Their grandmother taught the three of them to harmonize, and in 1959 their mother entered them in the Apollo Theater’s amateur night, which they won as the Darling Sisters.2 Estelle and Veronica were the daughters of a white father and a mother of African-American and Cherokee descent, and Nedra of Black, Native American and Puerto Rican parents; all three were bullied at school for looking like none of the categories on offer.2 The stage presentation they later built, the eyeliner and the piled hair, was assembled out of exactly the ambiguity they had been punished for. Estelle, who had been valedictorian of her high school and studied at the Fashion Institute of Technology, is the reason the clothes did not come from nowhere.3

They reached a stage by mistake. Underage, coached by their mothers on makeup and hair until they could pass for twenty-three, they turned up at the Peppermint Lounge during the twist craze and the manager took them for the dancers booked to work behind the house band. He walked them in and put them on. Mid-set one of the Starliters handed Veronica a microphone, and by most accounts the song she sang was Ray Charles’s “What’d I Say.”4 The club kept all three on at ten dollars a night each, dancing and usually singing one song. That is the act Spector signed, and the detail matters to what the album turns out to contain.

Philles was a singles house. The catalog numbering says so plainly: “Be My Baby” is Philles 116 and the singles run into the hundreds, against a handful of albums numbered from 4001.5 This is PHLP-4006, and the label assembled it the way a singles house assembles an album, around the hits it already owned.

Recording

Gold Star’s Studio A was small, roughly nineteen feet by twenty-four under a thirteen-foot ceiling, and the smallness was the method.6 The walls forced the instruments to leak into one another’s microphones until the ensemble fused into a single mass, and two echo chambers behind the control room, each holding a ribbon microphone and an eight-inch speaker, soaked that mass in the reverb that became the signature.7 The reverb is structural, part of the arrangement rather than an effect laid over it, and the density is additive: many players doing the same thing at once rather than many players doing different things.

Spector stacked doubled and tripled instruments in unison and bounced them down. Larry Levine described the order of assembly as guitars first, then pianos, then bass, with Hal Blaine’s drums brought in last, the drummer working least because he did not start playing until everything else was right.8 The players were the Los Angeles session pool later called the Wrecking Crew, the same musicians Brian Wilson would hire for Pet Sounds two years later. The full roster runs well past the rhythm section that usually gets named. There are four pianists, among them Harold Battiste, the New Orleans arranger who founded AFO Records and worked with Sam Cooke. There is a horn section of Steve Douglas, Jay Migliori, Lou Blackburn and Roy Caton, which most accounts of the Wall of Sound leave out altogether. And Sonny Bono, Spector’s promotion man, is on the record as a percussionist.9 Jack Nitzsche wrote the charts that turned Spector’s humming and gesturing into parts a room could read.

Only Veronica sang lead. The backing stack was session voices, Darlene Love and Bobby Sheen and Nino Tempo among them, with a pre-fame Cher whom Levine remembered having to back away from the microphone because her voice came through stronger than the others.10 The rhythm bed for “Be My Baby” took something like forty-two run-throughs across an afternoon before tape rolled, and the lead vocal took three days of takes into a Neumann U47.11 Blaine told the four-bar introduction as an accident, a dropped stick where he had meant to hit the snare on two as well as four.12 The story has the shape of legend and should be held loosely, but it describes the method exactly: something caught by chance and then drilled into permanence.

Ten of the twelve tracks are 1964 recordings. The British pressing’s copyright line dates only “Be My Baby” and “Baby, I Love You” to 1963 and the other ten to 1964.13 The record is therefore less a scrape of old singles than it appears. Two-thirds of it was cut in the year it came out, to build an album around two hits.

Music

The album runs on one voice and one room. Veronica Bennett’s is a hard, forward instrument with Spanish Harlem diction and gospel phrasing in the turns, and it sits in the middle of the arrangement without being swallowed, which is the technical achievement the record is usually praised for and the reason the wall has a center at all. Keith Richards, inducting the group into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2007, said that despite Nitzsche’s arrangements they could sing all the way right through a wall of sound.14

“Be My Baby” is the engine, the four-bar figure and the handclaps on two and four driving a progression the orchestra floods while the doubled and echoed lead rides over it. “Baby, I Love You” takes the same density and slows it into an ache, the strings carrying as much of the feeling as the vocal. “Walking in the Rain” is the mood piece, slower and darker, with recorded thunder laid across it; Veronica said she sang it slowly so people could hear her voice, and called it her favorite.15 “Chapel of Love” is the road not taken. Spector recorded it with the group and then gave the song to the Dixie Cups, whose version reached number one in 1964 while this recording sat here as track twelve.16

The two songs usually dismissed as filler are the most revealing things on the record. “So Young” is the Students’ “I’m So Young,” a doo-wop side released on Checker in May 1958 and written by a Cincinnati high-school student named William Tyus.17 “What’d I Say” is Ray Charles’s, from 1959, and at four minutes forty it is the longest track here by a wide margin, close to twice the length of anything else. Read against the Peppermint Lounge it is most plausibly the club act on tape, at the length the club act ran, which would make it the only surviving document of what the Ronettes did onstage before Spector reached them.

Those two are also the only tracks Spector takes no piece of. His name is on ten of the twelve, and the two it is missing from are the two the group did not commission.18 The songs he does own come from three writing teams rather than the two usually named: Barry and Ellie Greenwich wrote four with him, Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil two, and Pete Andreoli and Vini Poncia three, a quarter of the record. Poncia also plays guitar on it, which makes him the only writer who was in the room.18

Title and artwork

The title is a piece of billing and reads like one. Presenting the Fabulous Ronettes Featuring Veronica announces a discovery, names the group, and then lifts the lead singer out of it, which is the arrangement the records already described and the one that eventually broke the group apart.

The sleeve of the first pressing credits exactly one person: Phil Spector, producer. No arranger, no engineer, no musicians, no singers beyond the three on the cover.19 A record now read as a landmark of collective studio craft was sold in 1964 as the work of one named man, and the sleeve is the evidence of that. The same pressing misspells the group on the label, as the Ronnettes.19

What it inherits and what it introduces

The record sits at the end of a vocal line it never disguises. Veronica had been a doo-wop child, singing Frankie Lymon songs at the Apollo at eleven and keeping the boys’ repertoire as their voices broke.20 The debt is audible at the exact center of the record, in the “oh-oh-oh” of “Be My Baby,” which she traced to those Lymon records.21 The group form was inherited as well: the Chantels and the Shirelles had built the template of a lead voice against gospel and doo-wop harmony, and Spector’s wall was raised on that architecture rather than in place of it.22 “So Young” makes the inheritance literal. The album does not merely descend from doo-wop; it contains a doo-wop record, six years old, sung by a woman who learned to sing from that exact repertoire.

What it introduces is a way of making a pop single sound like an event. Unison doubling bounced down into one saturated mass with a single voice on top of it is the method the next decade of ambitious pop production argued with, and Pet Sounds is the first and most thorough of those arguments.

Release

Philles pushed it. Billboard gave the album a Pop Spotlight on November 28, 1964, its top album-review designation, and listed it under New Action LPs, the paper’s breakout column, on December 12 and again on December 19.23 The trade backed the record for three weeks running. It then entered the chart at 137 in the issue dated December 26, the deadest week of the year to arrive on an album chart.

It climbed to 121, 114 and 97, held 96 for a single week on January 23, and fell away through 101, 103 and 116. Eight weeks in all, and it never entered the top ninety of a 150-position chart.24 In Britain, the territory that had taken the group most seriously, the album did not chart at all.

The singles had gone the other way and then gone the same way more slowly. “Be My Baby” reached number two in America and held it three weeks, kept off the top by Jimmy Gilmer and the Fireballs’ “Sugar Shack,” and reached four in Britain.25 The American peaks then descend: 24, 39, 34, 23. The British ones descend further and faster, from 4 to 11 to 43 to 35, and “Walking in the Rain,” which America made the group’s late high point, never charted in Britain at all.26

Reception

The Billboard notice is worth reading closely, because it is not a review of a record. It says the group’s track record speaks for itself, notes a string of hit singles and a distinctive sound, observes that “Walking in the Rain” is selling, and closes by saying the group, with Veronica in the lead, take up the Phil Spector gauntlet.23 Every clause is about the singles or the producer. The paper’s panel rated records for sales potential rather than quality, so a Pop Spotlight was a commercial forecast, and the forecast was wrong.

There was nothing else in America. Rock criticism did not yet exist to review a pop album; Crawdaddy! began in February 1966 and Rolling Stone in November 1967. The only other contemporary notice found is British and late, four out of five from Record Mirror in May 1965 by Peter Jones and Norman Jopling, for a pressing sold in a country where the album never charted.27

The reappraisal, when it came, was narrower than it is usually described. Across the seventy-seven critics’ lists held in rhapsode’s archive this album appears in exactly three, and all three are editions of one list: Rolling Stone’s 500, at 427 in 2003, 422 in 2012 and 494 in 2020.28 Nobody else’s canon has found room for it. The single is in the Library of Congress and the album is 494th on one magazine’s list, and the distance between those two facts is the accurate account of where this record stands.

The magazine’s own copy changed along the way. In 2003 the entry called the group more a Spanish Harlem street gang than a girl group and described Veronica as Spector’s teen protégée. By 2012 the street-gang line and the word protégée were gone, she was named Ronnie Bennett rather than Ronnie Spector, and Spector had become her future husband.29

Legacy

Brian Wilson heard “Be My Baby” on a car radio, pulled over, and went home to work out how the guitars, pianos, bass, drums and percussion had been assembled; he has called it the greatest record he ever heard, and the line runs from that radio to Pet Sounds, which used the same players in the same room.30 The more concrete debt runs the other way. Wilson wrote “Don’t Worry Baby” as an answer to “Be My Baby” and took it to Gold Star to give to the Ronettes, and Spector, who would not record a song he had no part of, refused it; Wilson cut it with the Beach Boys instead, and Veronica did not record it until 1999.31 Two months after this album appeared the Beach Boys cut “I’m So Young” themselves.32 Whether Wilson took it from here cannot be established and should not be claimed, but two producers in Hollywood reached back to the same obscure 1958 side within six months of each other.

The route by which the record actually became canonical runs through film, and it is the same route by which the singers were dispossessed. Martin Scorsese opened Mean Streets in 1973 with those four bars cut against home-movie footage, which is regularly named as the moment a director found a new way to tell a story with a pop record.33 In 1987 Philles licensed the song to Dirty Dancing and the group was paid nothing for it. That license is the specific act behind the suit the Ronettes brought against Spector, decided by the New York Court of Appeals in 2002.34 The record became canonical by being licensed, and being licensed is the thing the women who made it were never paid for.

The other inheritances are plainer. The Ramones kept the beat and threw away the orchestra, then closed the circle by having Spector produce their cover of “Baby, I Love You” on End of the Century in 1980, their only British top twenty hit.35 The Jesus and Mary Chain ran the introduction through feedback to open Psychocandy in 1985.36 Veronica, who spent her later life taking back her work and her name, sang “Be My Baby” onstage with Bruce Springsteen in 1976 and released “Say Goodbye to Hollywood” the following year backed by the E Street Band.37 “Be My Baby” entered the National Recording Registry in 2006 and the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1999.38

The group was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame on March 12, 2007. Estelle was by then too frail to perform, and her entire acceptance was one sentence thanking them for the award.39 It was the last time the three of them appeared together. She died in 2009, Veronica in 2022, and Nedra in April 2026, which closed the story.1
