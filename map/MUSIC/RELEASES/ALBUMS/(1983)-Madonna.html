Madonna is the debut studio album by Madonna, released on July 27, 1983, by Sire. It exists because a single sold: Sire had signed her to a singles deal at fifteen thousand dollars a twelve-inch, with recording costs out of her end and an album only as an option, and the option came due after “Everybody” reached No. 4 on Billboard’s dance chart in late 1982.1 Three producers made it, each routed to her from the same few blocks of New York’s club world: the Danceteria disc jockey Mark Kamins, the rhythm-and-blues professional Reggie Lucas, and the Fun House resident John “Jellybean” Benitez. The album is dance-pop at club length, built from synthesizers and drum machines with almost no band on it, and seven of its eight songs run past four minutes.

Nothing about its commercial life was quick. It entered the Billboard 200 at No. 190 in September 1983 and climbed for more than a year, pulled up by one single after another, reaching No. 8 in October 1984 with Like a Virgin about to ship, and staying on that chart for a hundred and sixty-five unbroken weeks, the longest run of any album she has made. “Holiday” gave her a first Hot 100 entry at No. 16, “Borderline” her first top ten, and “Lucky Star” her first top five. The 1983 reviews were skeptical about the singing and warm about the records; the retrospective file reversed that, and AllMusic’s verdict is that the album “set the standard for dance-pop for the next 20 years.”2

Background

Madonna spent 1981 being shaped into a rock singer under a management deal she walked away from. The demo she and Stephen Bray cut afterward was pure dance music, and its route from a Danceteria booth to a contract is told at Downtown scene.3 Kamins’s own summary of why he played it kept the mechanism and dropped the romance: “I liked it and the floor liked it.”4 Seymour Stein signed her from a hospital bed, and his A&R man Michael Rosenblatt remembered a singer “just radiating star power” who answered the question of what she wanted with “I want to rule the world.”5

What she was walking into was a dance-music business that radio had spent four years pretending to have buried. The disco backlash of 1979 had driven the music off American pop radio without touching the floors, and what replaced it in New York was made on machines: the post-disco and boogie records of the early eighties, electro, and the imports that filled the twelve-inch bins. Club records reached the public through disc jockeys and specialist charts rather than through programmers, which is exactly the route her first single took.6 The other new fact was MTV, which had launched in August 1981 and by 1983 could turn a record into a hit on its own, a lesson the album’s campaign learned in its second year.7

Recording

“Everybody” was cut in three days in April 1982 at Bob Blank’s Blank Tape Studios, with Kamins producing. He remembered the budget by its arithmetic: the musicians were on three hundred dollars an hour and got it right the first time.8 For the album Rosenblatt brought in a professional. Reggie Lucas had played rhythm guitar in Miles Davis’s electric band and then turned hitmaker alongside James Mtume, and he was hired for an R&B feel.9

Lucas came with songs. “Borderline” and “Physical Attraction” were his, written for her after studying her demos and her shows rather than pulled from a drawer; he pitched them by singing onto a cassette, which he said he could not do.10 The sessions ran at Sigma Sound’s New York rooms from late 1982 into 1983 with a small circle of players: Fred Zarr and Dean Gant on keyboards, Gwen Guthrie leading the backing vocals, and Lucas programming the LinnDrum, the first drum machine he had used on a record.11 He found the singer already running on studio time and said so plainly, and his fondest memory was what “Lucky Star” became: the same song as the demo, he said, but barely.12

The Casiotone demo

“Lucky Star” was written on a giveaway Casiotone with a built-in tape deck, in about two weeks, and dedicated to Mark Kamins in the hope that he would play it. The lyric came off a yellow legal pad.13 The distance between that cassette and the finished track is the album’s whole story in one song: Lucas rebuilt it in the studio, and Benitez then remixed it again into a synthesized disco pulse with a jittering guitar line over the top.14

She heard Lucas’s results differently from Lucas. By the standard account she felt he had moved the songs away from the sparse shape of her demos, and he left without altering the tracks; Benitez, then her boyfriend, was brought in to remix.15 Lucas never accepted the framing. “Jellybean was a remixer, and we didn’t have time to remix records,” he said in 2013, and the revisionism still stung: “It’s hurtful because I didn’t understand it. I did the same thing I always did.”16 Benitez described his own part as additional production or co-production, with Lucas keeping the credit and the mixes coming out completely different, and he watched the client convert as the playbacks came up.17

“Holiday” was the remixer’s own production and the album’s hit. The song came from Pure Energy’s Curtis Hudson and Lisa Stevens, after their label rejected it and both the Supremes’ Mary Wilson and Phyllis Hyman passed on it.18 Warner gave Benitez a deadline with a prize attached: finish it by Friday and the album was his to complete. It was the first record he had ever produced from scratch. Zarr replaced the demo’s LinnDrum with an Oberheim DMX and layered Moog bass, Fender Rhodes and the piano solo Madonna filed among his “Zarrisms”; Hudson played his own guitar part, and Madonna added the cowbell.19 Benitez then worked it like the disc jockey he was, debuting it at the Fun House and passing a copy to Larry Levan for the Paradise Garage, so the record was a club fact before it was a single.20

Music

Madonna is dance-pop with post-disco and electro underneath it, and a synthesizer sheen that let 1983 radio hear it as something other than disco. There is almost no band: the record is keyboards, drum machines and programmed bass, with guitar used as a line rather than a rhythm section. Published arrangements exist for six of the eight songs and give a tempo for five, and those five sit in a narrow band between 108 and 138 beats per minute, four of them at 120 or below. It is a mid-tempo record for all its dancefloor reputation, and “Burning Up” is the outlier, marked not “medium tempo” like the rest but “bright dance beat,” thirty beats above the album’s center.21

The singing changes from track to track, and that variation is the record’s one vocal strategy. On “Borderline” she sings higher than anywhere else on the album, treated to sound more youthful, and ends in throaty scat over the fade. The refrain of “Lucky Star” is the helium-tinged sound every critic reaches for, and it is the only song here whose published range goes above B4. The hook of “Holiday” is delivered deadpan while the verses drop into her lower register, and “Everybody” is double-tracked and gum-chewing in tone.22 The 1983 reviews fixed on this. Don Shewey, in Rolling Stone, heard “a voice that takes some getting used to” and a girlish hiccup used until it was irritating, and still ended at surrender.23

Three songs step outside the album’s dance-pop center. “Burning Up” is a dance record with new wave in it and a rock guitar figure over the top. “I Know It” is pop rather than dance, with the melodrama of a 1960s girl-group record. “Physical Attraction” is mid-tempo R&B, which is Lucas’s hand showing. “Borderline,” also his, is the slowest thing here and the most clearly pop with R&B under it, and “Everybody,” the outlier at the end, is post-disco and electro.24 “Think of Me” is the other R&B-inflected track, and its Latin-tinged percussion is echoed on “Holiday.”

The lyrics are almost entirely about wanting and dancing, and they are direct about both. “Lucky Star” and “Borderline” address a lover as a fixed point and complain about being pushed past a limit; “Burning Up” and “Physical Attraction” state desire without apology or metaphor; “Holiday” asks for one day off, and “Everybody” is an instruction to the floor. Only “I Know It” turns to loss, and it does so in the vocabulary of a girl-group lament.

Title and artwork

The album carries her name and nothing else. Gary Heery shot the cover at his Broadway studio, and remembered a singer who arrived with a small bag of clothes and jewelry and no entourage, then went off in front of the camera like a great model.25 The image, cropped platinum hair with stacked black rubber bangles and a dog chain at the throat, became the first draft of a much-copied look, with Carin Goldberg’s art direction framing it. The British reissue of 1985 retitled the record Madonna: The First Album and put a new George Holy photograph on it, with crucifix earrings.26

What it inherits and what it introduces

The materials came straight from the twelve-inch culture of early-eighties New York, the boogie and electro grooves that had kept dancing alive after disco’s collapse on American radio, and the running times came from the club rather than from radio. Robert Christgau caught the lineage in his first sentence, writing that “disco never died — just reverted to the crazies who thought it was worth living for,” grading the album A− and hearing in it “essence of electro.”27 The R&B on it is Lucas’s, and traceable to the Mtume records he had been making.

What the album introduced was the assembly itself. Its three producers were a club disc jockey, an R&B professional and a remixer, none of them a rock producer; the sound they left is machines with a singer on top; and the campaign ran floor-first, breaking songs in clubs and letting radio catch up months later. That template, dance production under bright pop songwriting delivered by whoever currently ran the best floor in town, is what AllMusic means by a standard that held twenty years, and it stayed the operating method of its maker’s career.28

Release

Madonna was released on July 27, 1983, on vinyl and cassette. “Everybody” had preceded it by nine months, in October 1982, and “Burning Up,” backed with “Physical Attraction,” followed in March 1983; both were dance records and neither entered the Hot 100; “Everybody” reached No. 4 on Billboard’s dance chart and the “Burning Up” twelve-inch No. 3. Billboard tracked the “Holiday” and “Lucky Star” dance No. 1 of September 1983 as album cuts rather than as a single, which is why no single of that name appears in the chart record above.29 “Holiday” was issued in the United States that September and in Britain in December; “Borderline” followed in February 1984, and “Lucky Star,” which had gone to Britain and Europe first in September 1983, reached American shops in August 1984.30

The videos did the rest. Steve Barron shot “Burning Up” straight off “Billie Jean”; Mary Lambert’s “Borderline” became her first video in heavy rotation and, in the biographer Mark Bego’s phrase, turned “a voice without a face” into a recognizable star, earning a Best New Artist nomination at the first MTV Video Music Awards in 1984. The “Lucky Star” clip cost fourteen thousand dollars: a white void with her brother Christopher Ciccone and the dancer Erika Belle beside her, and a mesh-and-bracelets look that set off a wave of imitation.31

Reception

The reviews of 1983 were skeptical about the singer and warm about the records, and the objection was almost always the voice. Smash Hits rated it six out of ten and heard too much Donna Summer; Music Week complained that she sounded like a different singer on every track; a Phoenix daily dismissed it as “Chipmunks in Discoland.”32 Shewey’s Rolling Stone notice worked the same way, from the hiccup to an irresistible invitation to the dance, and Christgau’s A− was the outlier in taking the record’s genre seriously on its own terms.

The chart tells the same story slowly. The album entered at No. 190 in September 1983, climbed for over a year and peaked at No. 8 in October 1984, in the same week “Lucky Star” reached No. 4; Billboard’s own weeks-on-chart counter runs unbroken from one to a hundred and sixty-five. It sold 2.8 million American copies in its first year and was certified five times platinum in 2000, and it stands at roughly ten million worldwide. Britain took the long way too, the record reaching its No. 6 peak in August 1985, well over a year after it first charted, on the wave of the album that followed it.33 The retrospective file reversed the contemporary tone wholesale: five stars at AllMusic, an A from Entertainment Weekly, and a standing place on greatest-debut lists.34

Legacy

The record’s genre legacy is argued by critics more than claimed by heirs. Rolling Stone’s staff credited it with pointing the direction for the women who followed immediately, Janet Jackson among them, and with putting downtown New York electro grooves all over the Top 40; the Spin critic Michaelangelo Matos wrote that it “reinvigorated disco for a generation that wanted nothing to do with polyester suits.”35 “Borderline” opened the run the chart historians cite most, seventeen consecutive top-ten singles between 1984 and 1989.36

The songs themselves kept working. The Avalanches sampled “Holiday” into Since I Left You, and on the Celebration Tour in 2023 “Holiday” played inside a full recreation of the Paradise Garage, disco ball and all, which is the fortieth-anniversary vindication of Benitez’s first move, a copy slipped to the right disc jockey.37
