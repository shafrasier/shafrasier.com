"I Feel Love"

Dance music’s electronic era began on the B-side of a ballad. On May 1, 1977, Casablanca released a Donna Summer single called “Can’t We Just Sit Down (And Talk It Over),” a torch song that climbed the R&B chart on its own merits, with an album track from I Remember Yesterday on the flip.1 Clubs played the flip. Two months later the single was reissued with the sides reversed, and by July 9 the promoted B-side was on the British chart under its own name.2 Summer had told a British paper what the Munich records were built to do: “In Germany it’s very hard to make a decent record because they have no taste. So the only way is to shock them and then they will start to accept you.”3 The session that made the shock — the borrowed Moog, Robby Wedel’s tape-click sync, the kick drum two producers still describe differently — is Electro-disco’s telling, and the voice floating over it is Donna Summer’s. This note stays with the object: the pressings, the charts, the versions, and the record’s long afterlife as other people’s material.

The flip pays off

Britain answered first. The single entered the UK chart on July 9, 1977, on GTO, and spent four weeks at No. 1, the weeks ending July 23 through August 13.4 The continent fell in behind it — No. 1 in Austria, Belgium, France, Italy, and the Netherlands, No. 3 in West Germany, where the record was made — and Australia followed at No. 1.5 America moved slower: the Hot 100 peak came at No. 6 in the week of November 12, four months after London, with Cash Box placing it at No. 4, and on Billboard’s disco chart the entire parent album charted as a single entry at No. 1, cuts and all, the way that listing then worked.6 Certification came slowly too: Gold in America, Platinum in Britain, where its tallied sales run past 1.1 million.7 The trade press heard the design at once — Record World wrote that the song “establishes a mood first, then builds on it,” with particular admiration for the synthesizer work.8 The instrument’s inventor was harder to win over: “Warm, lyrical vocals,” Robert Moog said of the record, “but essentially it sounded like [Summer] was fighting the sequencer.”9

Three lengths of 1977

One recording left Munich at three sizes, and the crate below keeps them straight. The album and the US stock seven-inch carried the full 5:53; radio was serviced separately with a 3:46 promotional edit. The UK seven-inch printed 5:43 on its label for the same cut. The extended version, 8:15, lived on a one-sided American twelve-inch whose single playing face also held Summer’s own recording of John Barry’s theme from The Deep.10 Patrick Cowley’s sixteen-minute rebuild spent four years as a bootleg before Casablanca sanctioned it as a twelve-inch in 1982, its sleeve billing the “15 Min Remix.”11 In August 1995, Mercury’s new UK dance imprint, Manifesto, opened its catalog with the record: remixes from Rollo & Sister Bliss, the pair behind Faithless, and Masters at Work, a re-equalization of the 1977 recording by its original engineer, and new vocals from Summer herself, eighteen years on. It reached No. 8 in Britain and No. 1 on the UK dance and club listings.12

Other voices

Bronski Beat folded the song into a medley with the 1961 death-disc “Johnny Remember Me,” and the 1985 single version, sung with Marc Almond, carried the pairing to No. 3 in Britain — the Hi-NRG generation the record had seeded, covering it back.13 Blondie had covered it live in the seventies, and a heavily bootlegged 1979 recording finally surfaced as an official B-side in 1995.14 Sam Smith’s 2019 cover, produced by Disclosure’s Guy Lawrence, went to No. 1 on Billboard’s dance-club chart.15 The largest tribute closed an album: Beyoncé’s “Summer Renaissance” (2022) ends Renaissance by re-singing the hook over a pulse built in the original’s image, with Summer, Moroder, and Bellotte in the songwriting credits.16

The rank

The canon lists arrived decades behind the floors. Rolling Stone placed the song at No. 52 among the 500 greatest of all time and, in 2022, at No. 1 among the 200 greatest dance songs; Billboard’s 2025 dance-song century put it first as well.17 The Grammy Hall of Fame inducted it in 2024, thirteen years after the Library of Congress added it to the National Recording Registry.18 What remains is one Munich recording, five decades of hands on it, and the pulse unchanged under all of them.

What it inherits and what it introduces

The twelve-inch it stretched onto was the format “Ten Percent” had made commercial the year before, and the slot it filled on its parent album was that record’s own design. Even the promotion was traditional: a label flipping a single after the clubs chose the other side. The record introduces the sequenced pulse as a hit single, a dance record with no certain human sound but the voice, at No. 1 across Europe and Australia. Running forward from it comes the relay: a bootleg made canon, and remix packages arriving decades apart. The versions never replaced the original. They orbit it.
