The number in the title has never been explained. The album called the track “#5 (Go Bang!),” the single turned it around, and Arthur Russell — a student of numerology, by Philip Sherburne’s account — left no key to the figure.1 What plays under the number is downtown New York’s whole argument compressed to seven and a half minutes: a funk rhythm section locking a groove while an avant-garde horn line wanders through it, Julius Eastman driving an organ climb that rooms full of dancers learned to wait for, and a chanted phrase about going bang promoted, in François Kevorkian’s mix, into the refrain.2 Four decades of DJs have kept re-cutting it: one composition, seven versions and counting, and the authorship question the record never stopped asking.

One session, twenty records

The session was June 9, 1979, at Bob Blank’s Blank Tapes studio, and Russell booked it as a collision. On one side sat the Ingram brothers and the bassist Wilbur Bascomb, players who could hold a groove to the inch; on the other, the downtown wing — the saxophonist Peter Gordon, the trombonist Peter Zummo, the punk guitarist Denise Mercedes, and backing vocalists who included Eastman and the composer Jill Kroesen.3 No songs were written in advance. Russell ran the room on graphic scores and verbal prompts in the tradition of John Cage, then played the 24-track reels back across parallel decks, building new pieces from the collisions — the process the album’s title names.4 Lola Blank, the engineer’s wife, who had sung behind James Brown, was added to the vocal stack later. 24→24 Music appeared in late 1981 on Sleeping Bag Records, the label Russell had co-founded with Will Socolov to release it, with “#5 (Go Bang!)” running nearly eight minutes as its centerpiece.5 Contemporary DJs found the album cut too dense to program, its rhythm changing direction every twenty-four bars, and it took a specialist to find the record inside it.6

Kevorkian’s mix

Kevorkian had made his name remixing Musique’s “In the Bush”, and by his count the session reels held at least twenty songs.7 His job was to cut one loose. The mix he delivered honed the drums, dubbed the horns, and turned a chanted stretch of the vocal into the record’s refrain. Kevorkian, Sherburne wrote decades later, “doled out key sounds like so many breadcrumbs, offering dancers the chance to find their own way through the mix.”8 Sleeping Bag released it in 1982 as a twelve-inch 45, “Go Bang! #5” backed with “Clean on Your Bean #1” — the B-side its own small collision: produced by Russell with Peter Gordon, mixed by Kevorkian, with a rap credited to Andre and Abel.9

The floor answered immediately. An acetate went down to The Loft in Socolov’s hands, and David Mancuso refused to let the record end — each time the needle ran out he set it back to the start, four plays that night, “to an increasingly rapturous reception.”10 At the Paradise Garage it settled in as a peak-time fixture, Larry Levan holding Eastman’s organ climax for the instant the room’s confetti guns fired.11 The new-music wing heard it differently. Arnold Dreyblatt, the composer who had watched Russell premiere this music at the Kitchen, remembered “this music that had a disco beat, lovely melodies, strange twists, and bizarre lyrics.” The two rooms that mattered had just adopted it as an anthem.12

The versions

The single was the second version of seven and counting. Walter Gibbons — the remixer whose “Ten Percent” had been the first commercially released twelve-inch — stretched the session to twelve and a half minutes in scrambled-together hours at Blank Tape, a taut, epic cut closer to the 1979 session’s freestyle spirit than to Kevorkian’s tighter dance floor edit. It sat on the shelf for more than twenty years, circulating as a Japanese bootleg before its first official release in 2009.13 A 1988 UK twelve-inch carried a remix credited only to Hard Times Productions, an alias no source has ever resolved to human names.14 The 2000s added the editors: Prince Language’s “Thank You Arthur” edit slipped onto the official reissue canon with its credit missing, Danny Krivit cut his own edit of the Gibbons mix in 2020, thirty-eight years downstream of the single, and a one-off 1996 Joey Negro acetate is documented in a single copy, cut per its Discogs listing for the editor himself.15 Every version is another set of hands on the same reels: Russell wrote a composition loose enough that finishing it became a relay.

What it inherits and what it introduces

Each part of the record arrives with a parent. The rhythm section’s discipline comes down the funk line the Ingram brothers grew up inside, and the format is the commercial twelve-inch Gibbons had opened six years earlier. The session’s method — graphic scores and verbal prompts — walks Cage’s tradition into a disco date, and the rooms that received the acetate were the private-party network Mancuso had been building since 1970. What the record introduces is the relay. Its finished state was never fixed: the reels passed from mixer to mixer across four decades, every version a fresh claim on the same material, until the discography read as a family tree. The twelve-inch entered this story as a DJ’s tool. This record made it an authorship argument: the mix as composition, the composer as one hand among several.

Afterlife

The record’s second life ran through the samplers and the lists. Todd Terry built “Bango (To the Batmobile)” on it in 1988, and Common interpolated the chant in 1992, the record passing into hip hop and house within the same handful of years.16 Hot Chip covered it in 2014 for Master Mix: Red Hot + Arthur Russell, the AIDS-awareness tribute — a pointed homecoming for a record whose chanted invitation to friends Sherburne read against the epidemic that would devastate the community dancing to it, Russell included.17 When The Vinyl Factory ranked the hundred greatest disco twelve-inches, “Go Bang! #5” was first — “an unrivalled exercise in disco-not-disco.”18 A 2013 Record Store Day reissue even grazed the UK physical singles chart, thirty-one years after Sleeping Bag pressed the original.19 For Sherburne, the catalog it sits inside “comes to resemble a river: forever changing, forever the same.”20
