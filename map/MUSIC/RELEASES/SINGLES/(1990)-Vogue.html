The most famous stretch of “Vogue” has no melody. Over the vamp, Madonna reads a roll call of golden-age Hollywood names — Greta Garbo and Monroe, Dietrich and DiMaggio, Marlon Brando, Jimmy Dean — with what the critic Tom Breihan calls “a certain haughty imperiousness.”1 The record underneath is built the same way, from borrowed glamour: a dance carried out of Harlem’s ballrooms and a groove sampled from a Salsoul twelve-inch, assembled on a five-thousand-dollar budget as prospective B-side filler. Released in March 1990, it held number one for three weeks in America and four in Britain, topped the charts in more than thirty countries, and is generally cited as the best-selling single of 1990; in Breihan’s accounting, it was “almost certainly the first house track to make it all the way to #1.”2

The B-side that ate its A-side

The commission was modest on purpose. Craig Kostich, Warner’s head of dance music, asked Shep Pettibone for a track that could sit behind “Keep It Together,” the last single planned from Like a Prayer, and gave him five thousand dollars to make it. Pettibone cut the track in a West 56th Street basement studio whose vocal booth was a converted closet; Madonna flew in within two weeks and sang it in first takes.3 What the label heard ended the B-side plan. “This isn’t gonna be a b-side,” Pettibone remembered the reaction running, and the hierarchy inverted: “Vogue” went out as the A-side — backed in Britain by the song it was built to sit behind, in America by its own dub — and was added late to I’m Breathless, the Dick Tracy companion album, as its closing track.4

The groove that caused all this is a house record with a disco memory. Breihan hears “a burbling deep house thump that sampled the bongo ripples and horn stabs” of the Salsoul Orchestra’s “Ooh, I Love It (Love Break)” — a record Pettibone knew from the inside, since the mix in question was his own 1983 remix from the Salsoul Records chair.5 Tony Shimkin cut the edits and Goh Hotoda mixed. The spoken section that became the song’s signature was Pettibone’s suggestion; the lyric itself Madonna wrote largely on a flight to New York.6

The dance

Voguing is decades older than the record. It came up through Harlem’s drag-ball world as a competitive style of angular poses struck and held, named for the fashion magazine whose pages it quoted — a world Langston Hughes wrote about attending in the 1920s.7 The ball world had already put the dance on record: Malcolm McLaren’s “Deep in Vogue” (May 1989), with the ballroom legend Willi Ninja on rap vocals, topped Billboard’s dance chart in July 1989, eight months ahead of Madonna’s single — and carried additional production by Mark Moore and William Orbit, whose desk Madonna would reach nine years later.8 Her own route into the dance ran through the clubs and the House of Xtravaganza; that discovery, and the hiring of Jose Gutierez and Luis Camacho as dancers and choreographers, is told at Madonna’s note, along with the argument over who profits when a subculture’s dance goes to number one. Steven Canals, who co-created Pose: “what Madonna did was bring ballroom to the mainstream.” The balls themselves reached the screen in Paris Is Burning (1990), which premiered that September and met general audiences the following spring.9

Sixteen hours in black and white

The video is where the borrowing turns literal. David Fincher shot it in black and white across sixteen hours on February 10 and 11, 1990, composing frames after the painter Tamara de Lempicka and the studio-era photographer Horst P. Horst; Horst was displeased at the unauthorized borrowing and never gave permission. Madonna poses through it with Gutierez and Camacho at her shoulders.10 At that September’s VMAs she performed the song in Marie Antoinette dress and powdered wig, a performance Rolling Stone’s readers later ranked the sixth-best in the award show’s history; the Blond Ambition tour the dancers choreographed carried the song around the world the same summer.11

The versions

The 1990 suite is a closed circuit: every version is Pettibone’s, with no outside remixer anywhere in the single’s first-release catalog, a rarity for a Madonna single by 1990, when her hits routinely went out to multiple specialists.12 The 4:19 single version is the one that went to number one, and the album cut stretches it to 4:50. The 8:19 twelve-inch opens the record out to club scale, and the two dubs — the Bette Davis Dub at 7:24, its title one roll-call name promoted to a mix title, and the Strike-A-Pose Dub at 7:37 — strip the vocal back toward chant; in America the Bette Davis Dub even rode the 7” as the single’s own B-side.13 Britain ran the format ladder hard: seven UK editions including two limited poster twelve-inches and picture discs. That November, The Immaculate Collection added a sixth object: the whole compilation was reprocessed in QSound by Pettibone with Goh Hotoda and Michael Hutchinson, every track altered and most shortened, so the 5:19 version a generation of CD owners knows matches nothing that came before it.14

What it inherits and what it introduces

The inheritance is disco’s whole apparatus. The dub B-sides and the multi-format ladder are the twelve-inch grammar the seventies dance floor built, and the producer’s remix chair ran back through Salsoul to the first commercial twelve-inches; the closed Pettibone circuit is that machinery working at pop’s summit. The new thing was proof. After “Vogue,” a house record had held the top of the American chart and a ballroom vocabulary had held prime time, and neither door swung shut again.

A horn too small for the law

The Salsoul lift ended up defining American sampling law. VMG Salsoul sued over a 0.23-second modified horn segment lifted from “Love Break,” and in 2016 the Ninth Circuit held the borrowing de minimis — too trivial to infringe — in VMG Salsoul v. Ciccone, explicitly splitting from the Sixth Circuit’s get-a-license-or-don’t-sample rule in Bridgeport.15 The record that built a number one out of Salsoul’s crate became the case that said a quarter-second of it is not theft.

Afterlife

The spoken bridge kept working after the record left the charts. In August 2022, “Break My Soul (The Queens Remix)” made Beyoncé and Madonna’s first official collaboration an interpolation of “Vogue” — and rewrote the roll call, replacing the golden-age Hollywood names with Black women artists and with the ballroom houses themselves, Xtravaganza, Aviance, and LaBeija among them.16 Thirty-two years on, the record’s most famous device was returned to the rooms that invented the dance, with the names of those rooms read out where Garbo’s used to be.
