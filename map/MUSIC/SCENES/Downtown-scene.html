Downtown scene

Dance | Punk | Hip hop → Downtown scene

Periodc. 1978–1983
Locationdowntown Manhattan, New York
Genres
No waveMutant discoPost-discoPost-punkHip hop

The Mudd Club opened in October 1978 in a loft building at 77 White Street, TriBeCa, and the B-52’s soon became its unofficial house band. Steve Mass opened it with the curator Diego Cortez and Anya Phillips, and the door borrowed Studio 54’s velvet rope while inverting its criteria: the doorman Richard Boch turned away rowdy groups and once refused Paul Simon for asking “Do you know who I am?”1 Mass ran the room for the crowd — “Who needs all these bands? They’re just a distraction from the audience, which is just as interesting.” “At the Mudd Club,” Andy Warhol said, “mistakes could pass as experiments.”2 The club’s five years turned out to be the span of something larger: the era when the city’s separated undergrounds began working each other’s rooms, and the same faces started turning up in all of them. The plainest statement of the arrangement sits in the history of a club that did not exist yet: Danceteria, opening in 1980, catered to the after-hours crowd of “Mudd Club, Trax, Tier 3, Chinese Chance, CBGB, and gay discos.”3 One crowd, many rooms. The scene was the traffic between them.

Two downtowns

The word already had an owner. Since Yoko Ono opened her Chambers Street loft to concerts in 1960, “downtown” had named the experimental tradition of John Cage’s heirs — the critic Kyle Gann, the tradition’s chronicler at the Village Voice, traces “the very terminology Uptown/Downtown” to “the then-anomaly of Yoko Ono having presented concerts in her loft,” and describes that lineage running from Fluxus through minimalism to the free improvisers.4 The clubs of 1978 were something else wearing the same word, and the historians never settled the question. The scholars who map the territory describe overlapping circles rather than one scene — an NYU project on the era counts half a dozen competing starting points for it, Ornette Coleman’s Five Spot residency to the Mudd Club itself.5 Tim Lawrence split the difference himself: spanning 1973–1992 for the Arthur Russell biography, he titled the territory “the Downtown Music Scene”; tightening to 1980–83, he dropped the word from the title and described a “party scene” instead.6 The collision is the point. For five years the word’s two meanings shared a neighborhood, the Kitchen’s composers a short walk from the Mudd Club’s dance floor, and Russell worked both ends of the street in the same week. CBGB scene tells how punk’s cohort assembled; this note follows its children and the dance floor’s survivors into the shared rooms.

The rooms

Each room ran its own experiment. Inside the Mudd Club the booth was as unorthodox as the door: David Azarch, Anita Sarko, and Johnny Dynell played Blondie into Carl Perkins into James Brown, and on the fourth floor Keith Haring ran a rotating gallery that showed roughly a hundred artists working at the margins of the gallery world.7 Across town on St. Mark’s Place, Club 57 ran the opposite temperament in the basement of a Polish church, with Ann Magnuson managing the room and hosting its events, and Klaus Nomi, John Sex, and Joey Arias among the regulars. “The Mudd Club was more into coolness and being hip and shadowy and mysterious,” Magnuson said, “while Club 57 was about being loud and bright and colorful and kooky and silly — and doing mushrooms.”8 Tier 3, a three-hundred-capacity TriBeCa room founded in 1979 by Hilary Jaeger, stacked the whole scene vertically: live music on a ten-inch stage downstairs, art shows on the second floor, a dance floor with a disco ball on the third. Jaeger initially handed the door proceeds straight to the bands, and Jean-Michel Basquiat painted the DJ booth.9

Danceteria scaled the arrangement up. Rudolf Piper and Jim Fouratt opened the first location on West 37th Street in May 1980 and lost it before the year was out: an October raid for selling liquor without a license, then a final shutdown weeks later. The 1982 reopening on West 21st Street filled the first three floors of a ten-story industrial building and put the scene in the movies as the club in Desperately Seeking Susan.10 Under the booker Ruth Polsky the calendar drew Madonna, New Order, the Smiths, and Run-DMC through the same doors.11 The room’s resident DJ, Mark Kamins, met Madonna on his own dance floor. She gave him the demo of “Everybody”; he played it at the club, watched the floor answer, carried it to Sire’s Seymour Stein, and produced the single himself. Released October 6, 1982, it was her first record. “She was the first one that had the whole package,” Kamins said. “She had the look, the dance and the song.”12

The crossing

The bridge to the Bronx was built deliberately, and mostly by painters. In 1979 the filmmaker Michael Holman threw the Canal Zone party to introduce uptown graffiti writers to the downtown art crowd — “Let’s have a party that will bring these two worlds together,” as he put it — and formed the noise band Gray with Basquiat, whose declared method was to play as though they were aliens from another world with no idea how the instruments were meant to be played.13 “I brought it downtown in pursuit of my career as a painter,” said Fab 5 Freddy, a Brooklyn graffiti writer with a foot in the galleries. In April 1981 Haring’s Mudd Club gallery hosted “Beyond Words,” with Freddy among its curators: Basquiat’s SAMO alongside Dondi, Rammellzee, Lady Pink, and Lee Quiñones, the Bronx scene’s core figures showing in a downtown gallery for the first time. After the show, Mass asked Freddy to bring the music too, and Afrika Bambaataa, Grand Wizzard Theodore, and the Cold Crush Brothers began playing 77 White Street.14

The traffic ran to vinyl and film almost immediately. Debbie Harry and Chris Stein had met the Bronx scene through Freddy in 1978, and Blondie’s “Rapture” — the title Stein’s pun on “rap” — reached No. 1 on the Hot 100 in early 1981 with a name-check for Freddy and Grandmaster Flash in the verses; its East Village video became the first rap video aired on MTV, with Basquiat standing in at the turntables after Flash missed the shoot.15 The scene also filmed itself in real time. Downtown 81, shot that winter with a then-homeless Basquiat starring as himself, sat unreleased for two decades; Charlie Ahearn’s Wild Style, filmed in 1981 with Quiñones, Freddy, Flash, and the Rock Steady Crew, dramatized the crossing as its actual plot: a Bronx graffiti writer drawn downtown. “I wanted graffiti to be seen as a serious movement like Futurism or Dada, or other great movements in painting,” Freddy said of it.16

The crossing got its own room in 1982, when Ruza Blue, a British expatriate who had found the culture at a Bambaataa show the year before, moved her Friday parties into the Roxy, a Chelsea roller rink. She wanted, she said, “a mash-up club, a cross-cultural experience for all tribes to share – a club for everyone.” Bambaataa, Jazzy Jay, Afrika Islam, D.ST, and Grand Wizzard Theodore held the decks while the Rock Steady Crew danced, and the punk crowd came to watch. “The night had a thousand styles, a hundred dialects,” the club queen Chi Chi Valenti remembered.17 ZE’s art-star disco and 99’s punk-funk singles pressed the crossing onto vinyl — the catalog Mutant disco tells at full length.18

The scattering

The ending came from several directions at once, and the illness arrived first, from the dance floor’s far coast. Patrick Cowley, the Hi-NRG pioneer in Sylvester’s circle, died in San Francisco in November 1982, when the disease was still so poorly understood it was being called GRID. Nine months later it took one of the scene’s own: Klaus Nomi died at Memorial Sloan Kettering on August 6, 1983, among the first widely known figures from the arts to die of AIDS, his visitors required to wear masks and gloves.19 The Mudd Club closed that spring; 99 Records bled out in a lawsuit over the bassline hip hop had borrowed from it.20 Lawrence’s history names the larger forces that pulled the scene apart: the rents, the Reagan economy, the corporations moving in, and the virus. The parties outlived the rooms for a while — Haring took his birthday celebration to the Paradise Garage in 1984, hanging the room with spray-painted banners for the first Party of Life — and then Ruth Polsky, Danceteria’s booker, was killed by a runaway taxi outside the Limelight in September 1986.21 What survived was the habit the rooms taught: the crossing itself.
