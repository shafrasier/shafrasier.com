Fisher — “Ghosts of My Life”

Cited in 4 notes
Changelog, Hauntology, Lost futures, Reynolds — Retromania

Find it in a library:WorldCat·Open Library

Author: Mark Fisher
Title: Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures
Year: 2014
Type: Essay collection (Zero Books; 2022 reissue with a new introduction)

The book opens inside a canceled TV serial. In the 1982 finale of Sapphire & Steel, two agents sit trapped in a roadside café suspended outside time, and Fisher claims the image for the whole century: “It is the contention of this book that 21st-century culture is marked by the same anachronism and inertia which afflicted Sapphire and Steel in their final adventure.”1 The stasis hides, he argues, behind a surface frenzy of novelty, and his test for it is musical. Beam a current hit back to 1995 and nobody flinches; “play a jungle record from 1993 to someone in 1989 and it would have sounded like something so new that it would have challenged them to rethink what music was, or could be.”2 The frame is borrowed and credited: “the slow cancellation of the future” belongs to the Italian theorist Franco Berardi, and what Fisher adds is the ear, down to the dare that the years “from roughly 2003 to the present will be recognised… as the worst period for (popular) culture since the 1950s.”3 The thesis lands away from nostalgia entirely: what should haunt us, Fisher argues, is the not yet — the futures popular modernism promised and never delivered, the argument Lost futures takes up at full depth.4

The collection rebuilds a decade of Fisher’s writing — the k-punk blog he ran from 2003, pieces for The Wire, Sight & Sound, and Film Quarterly — into the closest thing hauntology has to criticism from inside, written as the records appeared. The central section runs the canon. Fisher interviewed Burial for The Wire while the producer was still anonymous, and he had introduced the Caretaker’s Theoretically Pure Anterograde Amnesia in its own liner notes. The Ghost Box acts he had called hauntology as early as September 2005 follow, with the party-hauntology strain he heard reaching the mainstream through James Blake and Drake.5 The title is a lineage lesson in miniature: Japan’s “Ghosts” (1981) passed into “Ghosts of My Life” (1993), the jungle track Goldie cut as Rufige Kru with the Japan original sampled inside it, and Fisher admitted he nearly named the blog “Ghosts of My Life” after the pair of them. The epigraph is a line from Drake likening himself to the amnesiac lead of Memento: a pop star announcing he can no longer form new memories.6 Depression, the subtitle’s first term, runs through the book via Joy Division, read as evidence that the era’s mental anguish is public rather than private, the claim that turned record reviews into politics.7

The reception split between the critic outside the music and the musician inside it. In ArtReview, Brian Dillon heard “a voice of relentless intelligence” but found the contents “remarkably comforting and familiar rather than energising,” a “soft canon” of Fisher’s own generational landmarks.8 In The Quietus, Paul Wolinski of 65daysofstatic took the book “as a yardstick” for electronic culture’s paralysis: “It’s rare for non-fiction to resonate with me in such a compelling way.”9 Then the book’s meaning changed. Fisher died by suicide on January 13, 2017, at forty-eight, and a body of writing about mourning lost futures began to be read as mourning its writer; by 2019 the New Statesman could say he had “posthumously assumed the sort of legendary status reserved for thinkers considered to be among the most important or insightful of their time.”10 A decade on, its standing is plain: the genre’s primary documents collected between covers, the posts that named the music bound to the argument for why it mattered.

Key contributions

  • The lost-futures argument made audible: Berardi’s cancellation thesis run through records, the 1993-jungle thought experiment, and “formal nostalgia” as the name for a culture recycling itself.
  • The two senses of hauntology, de-supernaturalized: the no-longer that keeps repeating and the not-yet that never arrived, with the specter stripped of the supernatural.
  • Popular modernism named as the canceled trajectory: the music press, post-punk, brutalist architecture, Penguin paperbacks, and the BBC Radiophonic Workshop as one public project.
  • The genre’s primary documents gathered: the September 2005 Ghost Box post, the anonymous-era Burial interview, the Caretaker liner notes.
  • Depression made political: read through Joy Division as the era’s public symptom.
