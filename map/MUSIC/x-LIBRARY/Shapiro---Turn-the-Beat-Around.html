Shapiro — Turn the Beat Around

Cited in 5 notes
Changelog, Disco, Echols — Hot Stuff, Hi-NRG, Sylvester

Find it in a library:WorldCat·Open Library

Author: Peter Shapiro
Title: Turn the Beat Around: The Secret History of Disco
Year: 2005
Type: Book (Faber and Faber)

The premise sits in the subtitle: a mocked music had a history worth telling straight. Peter Shapiro, a Wire contributor who had edited Modulations, an anthology history of electronic music, framed the case the way the book’s own jacket does — disco “may be the most universally derided musical form to come about in the past forty years,” yet “like its pop cultural peers punk and hip hop, it was born of a period of profound social and economic upheaval.”1 The origin story arrives in one sentence: disco was “essentially a shotgun marriage between a newly out and proud gay sexuality and the first generation of post-civil rights African Americans, all to the serenade of the recently developed synthesizer.”2

The structure runs outward from a broke city. The opening chapter, “The Rotten Apple,” is a portrait of fiscal-crisis New York, the decaying city where the marriage was performed, and the history then reaches back past the genre entirely, to the Swingjugend of Nazi Germany and the French Zazous, dancers for whom moving to forbidden American records was already resistance.3 From there the book works the machinery. A chapter on “automating the beat” traces the four-on-the-floor into the synthesizer age and out to euro-disco, and the Village Voice’s Geeta Dayal praised the book’s “rapturous passages describing the way songs feel” — her verdict on its “I Feel Love” chapter ran “worth the asking price alone.”4 The breadth was the point. Dayal noted the book “is wholeheartedly populist in its approach,” giving “equal ink to ‘The Hustle’ and to underground party-starter David Mancuso” — the mainstream craze and the private floor held in one frame, where the earlier histories had picked a side.5 And the book states the sympathy’s limits plainly: Shapiro, Dayal observed, “is quick to point out disco’s less-than-utopian aspects too”: a music that “could be a force for exclusion” and for “creepily nihilistic excess.”6

Among the disco histories, the book is the single-volume middle term. Lawrence had published the downtown decade two years earlier, and his own survey of the field places Shapiro at the end of the first post-backlash wave of disco books; he thought the subtitle oversold the secrecy — “Even in 2005, Shapiro’s subtitle seemed a little melodramatic given that so much of the story had been told…”7 Dayal drew the cleaner division: Lawrence keeps the technical and club-historical detail, Shapiro tells you how the records feel. Echols arrived five years later to argue both of them had lionized the underground at the mainstream’s expense.8 The British press received the book as a rescue operation: the Observer called it a “rehabilitation of the dancing dinosaur,” and the Guardian’s reviewer left “knowing a great deal more about disco” than he arrived with. Jeff Chang blurbed it as riveting and essential.9 Shapiro filled out a strange and complete life around it: the Rough Guides to drum ‘n’ bass and hip hop, then a second career in intellectual-property law, before his death in July 2025 at fifty-five. Faber had already answered the derided-genre question by then, reissuing Turn the Beat Around in its Faber Greatest Hits series in 2020 — back in 2005, reviewers had to be talked into taking the subject seriously at all.10

Key contributions

  • The shotgun-marriage origin: out gay sexuality and post-civil-rights Black America, with the synthesizer playing the wedding.
  • The deep prehistory: the Swingjugend and the Zazous — the argument that dancing to forbidden records was defiance before the genre had a name.
  • The mechanics chapter: the four-on-the-floor, the synthesizer, and euro-disco treated as craft history, with the “I Feel Love” reading as its showpiece.
  • The populist frame: the Hustle and the Loft in the same book — mainstream craze and underground floor as one story, against the field’s habit of choosing.
  • The stated dark side: disco as a force for exclusion and nihilistic excess, from a sympathetic historian.

See also

  • Last Night a DJ Saved My Life — the booth history whose two disco chapters came early in the post-backlash wave
  • Love Saves the Day — the downtown decade at archival depth; Shapiro is the reach and the feel to its detail
  • Hot Stuff — the academic successor that took the mainstream’s side against both its predecessors
