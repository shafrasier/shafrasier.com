Changelog

rhapsode grows in perpetuity. This page contains a dated record of substantive changes such as new notes, real revisions to prose, and new features that are added. Mechanical fixes such as link maintenance and plumbing don’t appear here. Nothing is ever silently rewritten.

September 2026
  • Sep 10 — Album notes gain a critical record beneath the chart record, first on Ray of Light: the contemporary notices with each grade as its outlet printed it, the year-end polls (Pazz & Jop above all, with points and ballots), and the retrospective canon lists drawn on one timeline — year across, rank down — so a record’s movement through Rolling Stone’s three editions of the 500 reads as a line. Folds like the blocks above it. On phones the chart record had lost its Expand and run off the page after the fold landed; fixed the same afternoon.
  • Sep 11 — Like a Virgin rewritten, from 1,845 words to 4,065, and its account of 1984 turned around. The note had said the reviews were cool and amused; the trades were the opposite — Billboard gave it a Spotlight, Cash Box made it the lead album pick, and Brian Chin called it her fine second album and worked through it track by track. The cool notices were the rock press, and Matthew Rettenmund’s Registry essay says why: critics still warm from Disco Demolition Night found ways to review a dance record without writing that disco sucks. Also corrected: four of the nine songs are by outside writers, not two — “Dress You Up” is Andrea LaRusso and Peggy Stanziale’s, and the note had twice called it hers.
  • Sep 10 — True Blue rewritten, from 1,797 words to 4,217. The record now has the position it was made from: Billboard reported that Whitney Houston had been certified at six million twelve days before it shipped, equalling and about to pass the only other album by a woman to get there, which was Madonna’s; Madonnalike was already a category noun in the trade’s dance column, and the week the album arrived, a Madonna soundalike produced by her own collaborator sat at No. 1 on the club chart. “Love Makes the World Go Round” turns out to be the first song she and Patrick Leonard finished, written at a barbecue and sung at Live Aid eleven months before the album existed. Six corrections went in with it, including three British No. 1s where the note had said two, and a Los Angeles Times link that did not resolve.
  • Sep 10 — Like a Prayer rewritten properly, at nearly twice its former length. The eighteen months of silence before it now carry what the trade made of them, including Billboard calling a thirty-year-old a veteran in the week her single outdebuted every record on the chart; the Broadway year has its particulars and its pans; and the Pepsi affair is told in the order it happened, which is not the order it is remembered in — the company defended the commercial for five weeks, the boycott was called off before the ad was pulled, and the reason Pepsi gave was that nobody could tell the ad from the video. Her own account of the songs, from the Rolling Stone interview she gave that March, runs through the Music section: Sly Stone and Simon and Garfunkel in one magazine, Karen Carpenter and Nancy Sinatra in another, the same fortnight.
  • Sep 10 — All seven Madonna albums gain a critical record: the notices of their own year with each grade as its outlet printed it, the Pazz & Jop placings with the poll’s points and ballots, the retrospective lists on a timeline, and the later verdicts. Read across the shelf it shows what a single note cannot. Madonna placed on no Pazz & Jop at all, in either 1983 or 1984. Erotica drew the widest spread of any of them — the Los Angeles Times at two of four in 1992, Christgau’s A a season later, Rob Sheffield’s four out of ten in the Spin guide, and Pitchfork’s nine in 2026. And where Ray of Light climbed the three editions of Rolling Stone’s 500, from 363 to 367 to 222, Like a Prayer fell through them, 237 to 239 to 331.
  • Sep 10 — The critics’ lists are now kept, not looked up. A list archive holds one file per list edition with every entry it printed and a record of where and when it was read — Pazz & Jop for every year the poll’s own results pages carry, 1971 and 1974 through 2007, albums and singles with their points and ballots; Rolling Stone’s 500 in each of its three editions; NME’s 500; Pitchfork’s 1980s, 1990s and 2000s; Slant’s 1980s and 1990s; Q’s 100; Apple Music’s. A record’s standing is a question the archive answers in a second, and no page is ever fetched twice.
  • Sep 10 — The tracklist and the chart record fold. Each block’s caption is now a control with a chevron: press it and the block collapses to its caption line, so a reader after the text gets there in a screen or two, and the choice is remembered across pages in that browser.
  • Sep 10 — The chart-run graph is rebuilt around the way it actually stretches. The position axis now keeps its expanded look at all times, in its own pinned column, and Fit and Expand only change how far apart the weeks sit, with the toggle animating that spread sideways instead of snapping. The titles under each single’s first dot keep clear of one another, so “Oh Father” no longer disappears under “Keep It Together” on a phone; the No. 1 star in the key sits level with its label; and the axis column no longer paints over the toolbar as the page scrolls. The chart table’s sections are now headed by the chart’s own name, Billboard 200 or Hot 100, in the same small caps as the columns beside it.
  • Sep 10 — The albums shelf shows one release date per record again. It had been printing every date a note’s header carried, so four Madonna albums showed two and Ray of Light showed three. The notes themselves still give every territory.
  • Sep 10 — Madonna rewritten to the rhapsode standard, which finishes the shelf: eight notes, seven albums and the artist, all now sharing the same sections. The artist note gains the Music section it never had, on the voice that changed once and late, the one-a-day writing method, and the rule behind every producer hire — she takes the person running the best floor, usually years before the charts know the name.
  • Sep 10 — True Blue and Like a Prayer rewritten to the rhapsode standard, which completes the seven Madonna albums. Every one now carries a Music section and a chart record built from Billboard’s own weekly charts. Reading those charts corrected the debut too: “Everybody” peaked at No. 4 on the dance chart, not the No. 3 that gets repeated, which is the “Burning Up” twelve-inch’s peak.
  • Sep 10 — Madonna and Like a Virgin rewritten to the rhapsode standard, with the Music sections both notes lacked. The Like a Virgin one corrects the record’s own legend: the Chic rhythm section plays on four of its nine tracks and the other five are drum-machine records, which the sleeve says and the received story does not. Quotation on the two notes falls from 23 and 35 percent to 7 and 7.
  • Sep 10 — Chart records for Madonna, Like a Virgin and True Blue, each with the week-by-week runs of the album and its singles in both countries, read from Billboard’s own weekly charts. The debut turns out to have spent 165 unbroken weeks on the Billboard 200, and the Official Charts page has “Like a Virgin” entering Britain at No. 51 rather than the No. 5 usually reported.
  • Sep 10 — Ray of Light rewritten to the rhapsode standard, the third note to get it: a lead, the standard sections, and a Music section built from the published scores and the 1998 reviews, which shows the record is slower than its reputation. Its Background now carries what the trade papers actually said: three months before the album was finished, exactly two electronica albums sat on the whole Billboard 200. Quotation is down from 29 percent of the note to 13.
  • Sep 10 — Tracklists no longer print vinyl sides for records most people bought on compact disc. Erotica, Bedtime Stories and Ray of Light now run as one continuous list; records through the 1980s keep their sides, because side one ending is a fact of how they were heard.
  • Sep 10 — Ray of Light gains its chart record, with the week-by-week runs of the album and all five singles in both countries. Reading Billboard’s own weekly charts settles the peak of “The Power of Good-Bye” at No. 11, where the secondary sources disagreed.
  • Sep 10 — Cover art, theme colors and full genre lines for Like a Virgin, True Blue, Like a Prayer and Ray of Light.
  • Sep 10 — Erotica now links the explicit edition throughout, so the tracks play as recorded and “Did You Do It?”, which the clean edition drops altogether, has a link at last.
  • Sep 10 — The top bar is readable in dark mode on a phone again. On album notes it was painting itself with the cover’s pale wash while the wordmark, node mark and menu button stayed pale too, which measured 1.2 to 1 against it and left the bar effectively invisible; in dark mode it now takes the dark page ground, measuring 10 to 1 and better. The desktop rail already did this.
  • Sep 10 — The chart graphs’ Fit and Expand now animate. The box eases between the two heights over four tenths of a second and the arriving plot fades up with it, instead of the views swapping on a single frame. Reduced-motion settings skip straight to the new view.
  • Sep 10 — Erotica is dressed: cover art, the white and blue theme sampled from it, and the full genre line.
  • Sep 9 — Erotica rewritten to the rhapsode standard, the second note to get it: a lead in place of the opening quotation, the epithet headers replaced with the standard sections, a Music section that names what the record sounds like and the genres of the tracks that leave the album’s own, quotation cut from a third of the note to an eighth, and a full chart record with the week-by-week runs of the album and all five singles in the United States and Britain. Its MTV story is corrected too: the “Erotica” video was not banned outright but premiered at midnight, restricted, pulled after three airings, and restored seventeen days later behind a warning.
  • Sep 9 — The chart-run graphs gain a Fit / Expand control on the time axis: collapsed the whole run fits the column, expanded the weeks spread out and the graph scrolls sideways with the position axis pinned beside it, so a forty-eight-week run can be read week by week on a phone.
  • Sep 9 — The chart record gains chart-run graphs: week by week, the album’s Billboard 200 run and the singles’ Hot 100 runs on a chart toggle (UK Albums and UK Singles on the country toggle), No. 1 at the top, a dot per week with the date and position on hover or tap, a star for every week at No. 1, and each title under its first dot. The positions come from Billboard’s own weekly charts, now archived in the repository so no chart week is ever fetched twice, and from the Official Charts Company’s run pages.
  • Sep 9 — The chart record on Bedtime Stories is now one table with its columns aligned, a US / UK toggle so only one country’s figures show at a time, and each single’s release date set under its title, with territory and dating notes as a small popover on the date.
  • Sep 8 — Bedtime Stories rewritten as the first note to the new rhapsode standard: a lead that states what the record is and how it did, standard sections a reader can scan, the Letterman appearance set off as a story panel, and a chart-record block under the tracklist that carries the album’s and singles’ debuts, peaks, and weeks at peak, with their sources. Two features ship with it: the story panel and the chart record.
  • Sep 7 — Ray of Light finishes the Madonna run through the nineties: the record told from the grease-pencilled keys and the Atari that caught fire in the room at Larrabee — Nowels’ three-to-seven sessions, the DAT she answered in five days, the Sanskrit and the Gap ad and the 1971 folk song the words came from, the Belgian judge who banned it, and the same Rolling Stone critic who called Orbit’s bleeps rinky-dink in 1998 and the album of a lifetime in 2018. Every quotation checked at its carrier.
  • Sep 7 — Bedtime Stories takes the Madonna shelf into the mid-nineties: the record told from the Letterman dressing room forward — her own words on dropping Pettibone, the lyric-sheet sessions and Babyface on a voice she had never used that way, “Human Nature” as the answer she said it was, the London demo that named the album, and the 1994 reviews’ retreat-or-answer question set against the one critic who heard both.
  • Sep 7 — Erotica opens the nineties shelf: the album that began with Madonna saying “I hate them” to a set of finished-sounding songs, then got made on an eight-track in her producer’s apartment where the demos stayed the record — Dita arriving in the mix, André Betts’s hole-in-the-wall sessions, an AIDS elegy on side four, and the reviews it actually got, which were far better than the ones everyone remembers.
  • Sep 7 — Like a Prayer closes the eighties run: the album that arrived smelling of patchouli with an AIDS card in the sleeve — Leonard’s “divorce album,” the nine players in the room for “Oh Father” and the two that made “Promise to Try,” Andraé Crouch’s choir vetting the lyrics before it sang them, and the Pepsi affair set beside the tidier version she tells now.

  • Aug 28 — One menu now, everywhere: the categories are the same set of coloured pills whether you open them from a note or pull up the menu on the home screen. Everything is one tap away and nothing is hidden behind a scroll.
  • Aug 28 — Madonna opens the Madonna album shelf: the debut told from the singles deal up — the Casiotone cassette behind “Lucky Star,” Reggie Lucas’s sessions and the Jellybean finish that rebuilt them, “Holiday” arriving through two refusals and a Friday deadline, and the fifteen-month climb from a club record to No. 8.
  • Aug 27 — The Warehouse joins the VENUES, built on the City of Chicago’s own landmark report: Robert Williams’s US Studios parties and the West Loop chosen for safety and volume, the four-dollar members-only juice bar with no closing time, the Richard Long sound system, the free-for-all that ended the Knuckles years, and the room’s long second act — endangered in 2022, landmarked in 2023, and given its Englewood Arts Collective plaque at a 2024 block party with the founder among the hosts.
  • Aug 27 — Frankie Knuckles joins the PRODUCERS: the Continental Baths apprentice who gave Chicago five years and stayed to name a genre’s room — the reel-to-reel surgeries, the 909 Derrick May drove down wrapped in a towel, the Def Mix decade from “Tears” to “The Whistle Song,” the first Grammy ever given a remixer, and the White House letter his family received when he died.
  • Aug 27 — House joins the GENRES as the EDM branch’s trunk: the name that got there first — a tavern sign, a record bin, a room on South Jefferson Street — then the practice becoming product through Trax and D.J. International, the 303’s beautiful accident, the British crossing, and the family the first room seeded from deep house to ballroom.
  • Aug 27 — The EDM branch gets its own wing and its Chicago shelf: the family now files under GENRES/DANCE/EDM/ with room for House and its children, and Do You Remember House? joins the LIBRARY — Salkind’s Chicago telling, where house’s first-decade crossover made the music a shared archive and its queer Black and Latino makers hold the center of the remembering.
  • Aug 27 — About becomes a set of doors rather than one long scroll: tap a topic — what rhapsode is, how it’s organized, what it isn’t — and it opens in place. The eight note families are now colour-coded rows you can open one at a time, instead of a table that never fit a phone. The last places the old name survived have been swept.
  • Aug 27 — The site takes its name: the wordmark now reads rhapsode, and the name carries through the About card, the atlas popups, and everywhere the site introduces itself. Light is properly the default again — an embedded graph had been quietly saving its own dark theme into every visitor’s browser, and anyone it caught is reset on their next visit.
  • Aug 26 — “Vogue” joins the SINGLES with a six-version crate: the $5,000 B-side that became the best-selling single of 1990, the Harlem ball dance that reached the charts first in “Deep in Vogue,” the all-Pettibone version suite from 7″ to QSound, and the quarter-second of Salsoul brass that ended up defining American sampling law.
  • Aug 24 — Madonna joins the ARTISTS: the whole career as one long conduit between the dance floor and the mainstream — the faceless “Everybody” sleeve, the producer chain from a Danceteria DJ booth to SOPHIE, the ballroom question held open in bell hooks’s own terms, and the nineties given their full weight from the Letterman cigar to the Maida Vale attic.
  • Aug 24 — Introspective joins the ALBUMS: the Pet Shop Boys album planned at twelve-inch length from the start — an opera singer sings “house” as its first word, Frankie Knuckles and a Sterling Void cover carry Chicago into a No. 2 British pop record, and the singles were edits cut down afterward.
  • Aug 22 — Pet Shop Boys join the ARTISTS: the duo that ran pop’s hierarchy backward — a Smash Hits editor and an architecture student who apprenticed to Bobby O, got rebuilt by Stephen Hague, and then made the twelve-inch the primary text, from Disco’s printed BPMs to Introspective, where the album track is the club mix and the single is the edit-down.
  • Aug 21 — Shep Pettibone joins the PRODUCERS: the KISS-FM mastermixer who took the Salsoul chair after Moulton, Gibbons, and Levan, hid behind the Mastermix Productions credit, rebuilt Madonna’s singles from the voice up, co-wrote “Vogue” in a $5,000 closet-booth session — and walked away mid-nineties to run an Asbury Park hotel and the nightclub that helped revive the town.
  • Aug 21 — “Go Bang! #5” gets its number back: the single now carries the title as Sleeping Bag printed it, on its own page and everywhere it’s shelved.
  • Aug 20 — The Disco family completes its European wing: Euro-disco (Munich’s six-word No. 1, the Frankfurt hoax, the Paris opus), Space disco (Meco’s Star Wars conversion, the helmet dodge, the cosmic-disco fence), and Italo-disco (the ZYX sticker, the broken-English export economy, and the Chicago purchase from Importes Etc. to Derrick May) all join the GENRES.
  • Aug 20 — Sylvester joins the ARTISTS and Salsoul Records joins the LABELS: the falsetto that insisted on being real, from the Palm Lane choir through the Opera House to the will that funds AIDS charities still — and the label that put the first twelve-inch on sale, raided Philadelphia’s orchestra, and kept the DJ booth on staff from Gibbons to Levan to Pettibone.
  • Aug 20 — Electronic Dance Music opens as the umbrella between the Dance and Electronic families, with The Underground Is Massive on its shelf beside Energy Flash: the EDM 1 sleeve joke of 1980, the Chicago and Detroit birth certificates, Storm Raves and the Furthur campouts, the boom the industry branded and the bust the name outlived — and Electro-disco and Hi-NRG re-rooted under the new umbrella per RYM’s own tree.
  • Aug 20 — Release titles now read naturally everywhere: index pills and search say Abbey Road (1969) and “I Feel Love” (1977), the home graph drops the year prefixes and sets album titles in italic, and double A-sides join with a slash — “Strawberry Fields Forever” / “Penny Lane.”
  • Aug 20 — “I Feel Love” joins the SINGLES with rhapsode’s second crate: six versions from the 5:53 album cut to the 1995 Manifesto remixes, the flipped-B-side origin, the world chart run, and the covers from Bronski Beat to Beyoncé.

Sources drawn on

  • Vincent — Funk — Funk is a worldview, an aesthetic philosophy rooted in Black consciousness and organized around James Brown's One — and funk itself, running from Sly and Hendrix through P-Funk into hip hop, is one more Black American tradition borrowed without acknowledgment.
  • Jackson — A House on Fire — Philadelphia International was a self-made Black recording empire second only to Motown — designed as a deliberate cross of Motown's centralized machine and Stax's family feel, and undone by no single villain: payola exposure, competition, a hard economy, and changing tastes all at once.
  • Shapiro — Turn the Beat Around — Disco was 'a shotgun marriage between a newly out and proud gay sexuality and the first generation of post-civil rights African Americans,' serenaded by the synthesizer — and its secret history runs from dancing under the Nazis to house and hip hop, with the craze and the backlash as two chapters in a longer story of the beat as defiance.
  • Masters — No Wave — No wave was wider than the Eno compilation — an anti-movement of untrained artists spanning bands, film, and public-access television, best documented through its recordings, whose refusal ran forward into Sonic Youth.
  • Lawrence — Hold On to Your Dreams — Arthur Russell's refusal to choose between the Kitchen, the pop song, and the dance floor was the point — a biography that follows one boundary-crossing musician through every downtown room and, by tracing him, draws the network the rooms made.
  • Echols — Hot Stuff — Disco was inseparable from three American remakings at once — gay macho, a rising Black middle class, and a culture in which female desire finally mattered — and the backlash struck at all three; the shallow, polyester surface was the disguise the revolution wore.
  • Reynolds — Retromania — The 2000s were the 'Re' Decade — pop stopped producing its own present and began consuming its archive — and the open question is whether nostalgia stalled the culture or a stalled culture made nostalgia inevitable.
  • Fisher — Ghosts of My Life — The twenty-first century's cultural stasis is political — the futures popular modernism trained us to expect were slowly canceled — and hauntology is the music that mourns them.
  • Matos — The Underground Is Massive — America's rave decades run as one continuous underground — Chicago house and Detroit techno through the nineties regional scenes to the 2010s festival boom — and the industry's 'EDM' names a format, not a genre: a market formula laid over that lineage.
  • Lawrence — Life and Death on the New York Dance Floor — For four years, 1980 through 1983, New York's post-disco, post-punk, and hip hop undergrounds converged into one hybrid party scene — the city's most ferociously inventive stretch of dance-floor culture — until gentrification, Reaganomics, corporate intrusion, and AIDS divided it and dispersed the dancers.
  • Salkind — Do You Remember House? — House's first-decade crossover publics did not corrupt a pure underground; they made Chicago house a shared archive of collectivity and congregation, with its queer Black and Latino makers at the center of the remembering.
